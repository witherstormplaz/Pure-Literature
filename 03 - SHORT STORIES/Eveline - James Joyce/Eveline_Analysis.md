# Eveline by James Joyce
*A Comprehensive Literary Analysis and Annotation*

>[!abstract] **Introduction & Background**
> **James Joyce (1882-1941)** wrote *The Dubliners* as a "chapter of the moral history" of Ireland, focusing on childhood, adolescence, maturity, and public life. "Eveline" represents the phase of adolescence. 
> Joyce believed that the socio-cultural conditions of his homeland—specifically the dominating influence of the **Roman Catholic Church** and **England**—had a paralyzing effect on the lives and destinies of its inhabitants.
> **Title Origin:** While some cite Thomas Moore's "Eveleen's Bower" as a source, critic Terence Brown notes that a well-known Victorian pornographic novel bore the name "Eveline," in which the heroine has a sexual relationship with her father. This connects deeply with the story's underlying themes of abuse.
> **Publication Context:** The story first appeared in *The Irish Homestead* on 10 September 1904—a journal sponsored by the Irish Agricultural Organizations and committed to a nationalist agenda. In that publication context, the story could be read as a didactic cautionary tale warning Irish women against emigration and overseas marriage. When Joyce later revised it for the *Dubliners* collection (finally published 1914), he layered in additional complexity and ambiguity that subverted that earlier moralism.
> The story is a masterclass in modern narrative techniques, relying heavily on **Free Indirect Discourse**, **Stream of Consciousness**, and the concept of an **Epiphany** (a sudden realization or manifestation of truth, often of one's own paralysis).

***

## The Story

She sat at the window watching the evening invade the avenue. Her head was leaned against the window curtains and in her nostrils was the odour of dusty cretonne. She was tired. 

> [!info] **Literary Techniques & Analysis**
> - **Personification & Diction:** The evening does not just fall; it *"invades"* the avenue. This hostile, militaristic diction establishes an immediate sense of oppression and entrapment.
> - **Olfactory Imagery:** The *"odour of dusty cretonne"* engages the sense of smell to introduce the **motif of dust**, which symbolizes decay, stagnation, and the paralysis of Dublin life.
> - **Setting as Symbol:** **The Window** places Eveline at a physical and psychological threshold between the inside (her oppressive past/present) and the outside (her potential future). Feminist spatial critics (drawing on Henri Lefebvre and Michel Foucault) identify the window, doorways, and the harbor as *threshold scenes*—symbolic representations of Eveline's liminality and her unfulfilled quest for agency within a patriarchal domestic geography.
> - **Aqueous Motif (Pervasive):** The very language of the opening paragraph initiates a submerged aquatic motif that runs the length of the story. Epifanio San Juan Jr. notes that the *"description of her existence in time and place tallies with the recurring image of drowning or of being engulfed."* From the evening "invading" like rising water to the names of families on the avenue ("the Waters," "the Devines"—suggesting both the divine and *divining*, searching for water), Joyce establishes the sea and drowning as the controlling metaphor for Eveline's life and her failed escape.

Few people passed. The man out of the last house passed on his way home; she heard his footsteps clacking along the concrete pavement and afterwards crunching on the cinder path before the new red houses. One time there used to be a field there in which they used to play every evening with other people's children. Then a man from Belfast bought the field and built houses in it-not like their little brown houses but bright brick houses with shining roofs. The children of the avenue used to play together in that field-the Devines, the Waters, the Dunns, little Keogh the cripple, she and her brothers and sisters. Ernest, however, never played: he was too grown up. Her father used often to hunt them in out of the field with his blackthorn stick; but usually little Keogh used to keep nix and call out when he saw her father coming. Still they seemed to have been rather happy then. Her father was not so bad then; and besides, her mother was alive. That was a long time ago; she and her brothers and sisters were all grown up; her mother was dead. Tizzie Dunn was dead, too, and the Waters had gone back to England. Everything changes. Now she was going to go away like the others, to leave her home.

> [!note] **Literary Techniques & Analysis**
> - **Auditory Imagery & Onomatopoeia:** The *"clacking"* and *"crunching"* of footsteps break the silence, highlighting her static observation of others moving.
> - **Juxtaposition & Color Symbolism:** Joyce contrasts the *"little brown houses"* (brown consistently symbolizes paralysis and decay in Joyce's work) with the *"bright brick houses with shining roofs"* built by a Protestant man from Belfast, subtly introducing the theme of foreign/English encroachment.
> - **Stream of Consciousness:** The sight of the avenue triggers a non-linear flow of memories, transitioning smoothly from the physical setting to her past. 
> - **Foreshadowing:** "Little Keogh the cripple" subtly foreshadows Eveline's own eventual emotional and physical paralysis. 
> - **Syntax:** The paragraph ends with short, declarative sentences (*"Everything changes."*) mirroring the blunt, inescapable reality of time passing and death.

Home! She looked round the room, reviewing all its familiar objects which she had dusted once a week for so many years, wondering where on earth all the dust came from. Perhaps she would never see again those familiar objects from which she had never dreamed of being divided. And yet during all those years she had never found out the name of the priest whose yellowing photograph hung on the wall above the broken harmonium beside the coloured print of the promises made to Blessed Margaret Mary Alacoque. He had been a school friend of her father. Whenever he showed the photograph to a visitor her father used to pass it with a casual word:
"He is in Melbourne now."

> [!danger] **Literary Techniques & Analysis**
> - **Exclamatory Repetition:** *"Home!"* establishes the central conflict of the story.
> - **Visual Imagery & Metaphor:** The *"yellowing photograph"* and *"broken harmonium"* (a musical instrument) serve as visual metaphors for the decay of religion and the lack of harmony in her household. 
> - **Allusion:** **Blessed Margaret Mary Alacoque** was a 17th-century French nun who paralyzed herself with self-inflicted tortures, but was miraculously cured when she dedicated herself to a holy life. Joyce added this allusion during revisions to create a symbolic parallel to Eveline's own life of emotional and physical paralysis. Promise #6 of the Sacred Heart devotions reads: *"Sinners shall find in My Heart the source and infinite ocean of mercy."* This identification of God's heart with the ocean deepens the drowning motif—Eveline's fear of the sea is simultaneously her fear of divine salvation.
> - **Lacanian "Name-of-the-Father":** Lacanian critic Josephine Sharoni (2016) reads the nameless priest's photograph as emblematic of a *failed paternal metaphor*. For Jacques Lacan, the "Name-of-the-Father" is the signifier of paternal authority that organizes a subject's entry into the symbolic order—the domain of language, law, and social structure. The priest in the photograph is both nameless and silent: a figure lacking symbolic power. Her biological father, though physically present, fails to fulfill this organizing symbolic role. In Lacan's terms, what is absent is not a biological father but *"the lack of the signifier itself"*—and this foreclosure of the symbolic leaves Eveline trapped in a pre-symbolic, affect-laden psychic space that accounts for her ultimate inability to act.

She had consented to go away, to leave her home. Was that wise? She tried to weigh each side of the question. In her home anyway she had shelter and food; she had those whom she had known all her life about her. Of course she had to work hard, both in the house and at business. What would they say of her in the Stores when they found out that she had run away with a fellow? Say she was a fool, perhaps; and her place would be filled up by advertisement. Miss Gavan would be glad. She had always had an edge on her, especially whenever there were people listening.
"Miss Hill, don't you see these ladies are waiting?"
"Look lively, Miss Hill, please."
She would not cry many tears at leaving the Stores.

> [!info] **Literary Techniques & Analysis**
> - **Free Indirect Discourse:** This is a hallmark Joycean technique where the third-person narrator intimately adopts the character's subjective voice and vocabulary. The rhetorical question *"Was that wise?"* and the social anxiety of *"What would they say of her in the Stores... "* blend Eveline's internal monologue seamlessly with the narrative.
> - **Dialogue as Characterization:** The sharp, commanding dialogue of Miss Gavan directly demonstrates the public humiliation Eveline endures, contrasting with her private silence.

But in her new home, in a distant unknown country, it would not be like that. Then she would be married-she, Eveline. People would treat her with respect then. She would not be treated as her mother had been. Even now, though she was over nineteen, she sometimes felt herself in danger of her father's violence. She knew it was that that had given her the palpitations. When they were growing up he had never gone for her, like he used to go for Harry and Ernest, because she was a girl; but latterly he had begun to threaten her and say what he would do to her only for her dead mother's sake. And now she had nobody to protect her. Ernest was dead and Harry, who was in the church decorating business, was nearly always down somewhere in the country. Besides, the invariable squabble for money on Saturday nights had begun to weary her unspeakably. She always gave her entire wages-seven shillings-and Harry always sent up what he could but the trouble was to get any money from her father. He said she used to squander the money, that she had no head, that he wasn't going to give her his hard-earned money to throw about the streets, and much more, for he was usually fairly bad on Saturday night. In the end he would give her the money and ask her had she any intention of buying Sunday's dinner. Then she had to rush out as quickly as she could and do her marketing, holding her black leather purse tightly in her hand as she elbowed her way through the crowds and returning home late under her load of provisions. She had hard work to keep the house together and to see that the two young children who had been left to her charge went to school regularly and got their meals regularly. It was hard work-a hard life-but now that she was about to leave it she did not find it a wholly undesirable life.

> [!quote] **Literary Techniques & Analysis**
> - **Implication of Abuse:** The distinction made between how her father *"used to go for Harry and Ernest"* but threatens her specifically *"because she was a girl"* strongly hints at the threat of sexual abuse, adding a deeply disturbing layer to her desire to escape.
> - **Repetition (Epizeuxis):** *"Then she would be married-she, Eveline."* The repetition of her name emphasizes her search for identity and her disbelief that she could escape her current station.
> - **Tactile & Kinesthetic Imagery:** *"holding her black leather purse tightly in her hand as she elbowed her way through the crowds"* physically grounds the reader in the stress, tension, and labor of her everyday survival. 
> - **Revisions for Realism:** In later revisions, Joyce altered the syntax to catch the authentic rhythm of the father's abusive speech (*"only for her dead mother's sake"*), enhancing the psychological realism.
> - **Irony:** The paragraph concludes with deep psychological irony; despite documenting a life of abuse, theft, and exhaustion, the fear of the unknown makes her view it as *"not... a wholly undesirable life."*
> - **Marxist-Feminist Reading:** Marxists and Marxist-feminist critics read this passage as a document of ideological and economic exploitation. Eveline surrenders her *"entire wages—seven shillings"* to her father and must then beg to retrieve the money for household provisioning—a literal extraction of surplus value within the family unit. The capitalist system oppresses and exploits the working class; the patriarchal structure further subordinates women within that class. The economic powerlessness Joyce depicts is inseparable from the gendered nature of that powerlessness. Joyce, who considered himself a socialist, conceived of Ireland's predicament not as one of national oppression alone but of class-based economic exploitation. Eveline's father, in this framing, is the embodiment of capitalist patriarchy operating at the most intimate level.

She was about to explore another life with Frank. Frank was very kind, manly, open-hearted. She was to go away with him by the night-boat to be his wife and to live with him in Buenos Ayres where he had a home waiting for her. How well she remembered the first time she had seen him; he was lodging in a house on the main road where she used to visit. It seemed a few weeks ago. He was standing at the gate, his peaked cap pushed back on his head and his hair tumbled forward over a face of bronze. Then they had come to know each other. He used to meet her outside the Stores every evening and see her home. He took her to see The Bohemian Girl and she felt elated as she sat in an unaccustomed part of the theatre with him. He was awfully fond of music and sang a little. People knew that they were courting and, when he sang about the lass that loves a sailor, she always felt pleasantly confused. He used to call her Poppens out of fun. First of all it had been an excitement for her to have a fellow and then she had begun to like him. He had tales of distant countries. He had started as a deck boy at a pound a month on a ship of the Allan Line going out to Canada. He told her the names of the ships he had been on and the names of the different services. He had sailed through the Straits of Magellan and he told her stories of the terrible Patagonians. He had fallen on his feet in Buenos Ayres, he said, and had come over to the old country just for a holiday. Of course, her father had found out the affair and had forbidden her to have anything to say to him.
"I know these sailor chaps," he said. 
One day he had quarrelled with Frank and after that she had to meet her lover secretly.

> [!note] **Literary Techniques & Analysis**
> - **Character Foil:** Frank is positioned as the antithesis to Eveline's father and her Dublin life. While her home is silent, dusty, and violent, Frank brings music, exoticism, and affection.
> - **Metaphor & Color Imagery:** Frank's *"face of bronze"* contrasts sharply with the pale, dusty brown of Dublin. It represents the vitality, the foreign sun, and worldly experience.
> - **Motif of Escape / Exoticism:** The frequent mentions of *"distant countries,"* *"Buenos Ayres,"* and *"terrible Patagonians"* amplify the romantic, almost fairy-tale allure of escape, which Eveline struggles to process realistically. Joyce deliberately invokes the structure of Othello's courtship of Desdemona—a sailor recounting exotic adventures to a captivated woman—imbuing the seduction with an ominous intertextual shadow.
> - **Symbolism of Buenos Ayres:** While the city was a magnet for adventurers, critic Terence Brown suggests that at the time, "going to Buenos Aires" was also a euphemism for choosing a life of prostitution, adding moral ambiguity and potential peril to her "escape."

> [!warning] **New Historicist / White Slavery Subtext**
> A substantial scholarly tradition (Hugh Kenner, Katherine Mullin, Laura Barberan Reinares) has radically reinterpreted Frank and the danger he represents by situating the story within the moral panic about "white slavery" (sex trafficking) in early 20th-century Europe and South America. Buenos Aires was, at the time, the foremost destination for trafficked women and the headquarters of the international Jewish criminal association **Zwi Migdal** (the Warsaw), dedicated entirely to sex trafficking. Kenner first challenged conventional readings by noting the extreme improbability of Frank's three claims:
> - **The "terrible Patagonians":** A myth. By 1904, the Patagonians were long known not to be "terrible." Frank is deploying orientalizing fictions to captivate Eveline.
> - **A "home" in Buenos Aires:** Highly improbable. By 1914, four-fifths of working-class immigrant families in Buenos Aires lived in single-room *conventillos* (crowded tenement dwellings). Real estate was the monopoly of the Argentine oligarchy.
> - **"Fallen on his feet":** Implying prosperity from a sailor's deck-boy salary runs counter to the documented poverty of immigrant labourers.
> - Mullin goes further, demonstrating that Frank's courtship behavior (seduction through promises of marriage, international travel, and tales of wealth) closely matches the modus operandi of Zwi Migdal procurers, who recruited impoverished European women through false courtship to prostitute them in Argentine brothels. The phrase *"going to Buenos Ayres"* was contemporary slang for "taking up a prostitution life."
> - This subtext, once activated, recasts Eveline's paralysis at the quay not as cowardice or failure of will but as a potentially **rational survival instinct**—a deeply buried recognition, triggered by trauma, that the unknown Frank promises may be more dangerous than the known Dublin oppression. As Reinares provocatively asks: could Frank have been a Zwi Migdal recruiter and Eveline a potential sex slave?

The evening deepened in the avenue. The white of two letters in her lap grew indistinct. One was to Harry; the other was to her father. Ernest had been her favourite but she liked Harry too. Her father was becoming old lately, she noticed; he would miss her. Sometimes he could be very nice. Not long before, when she had been laid up for a day, he had read her out a ghost story and made toast for her at the fire. Another day, when their mother was alive, they had all gone for a picnic to the Hill of Howth. She remembered her father putting on her mothers bonnet to make the children laugh.
Her time was running out but she continued to sit by the window, leaning her head against the window curtain, inhaling the odour of dusty cretonne. Down far in the avenue she could hear a street organ playing. She knew the air. Strange that it should come that very night to remind her of the promise to her mother, her promise to keep the home together as long as she could. She remembered the last night of her mother's illness; she was again in the close dark room at the other side of the hall and outside she heard a melancholy air of Italy. The organ-player had been ordered to go away and given sixpence. She remembered her father strutting back into the sickroom saying: 
"Damned Italians! coming over here!" 

> [!danger] **Literary Techniques & Analysis**
> - **Chiaroscuro (Light & Dark Imagery):** *"The evening deepened... The white of two letters in her lap grew indistinct."* The fading light mirrors Eveline's fading resolve and her descent into psychological darkness and doubt. Can be interpreted as foreshadowing.
> - **Auditory Motif:** Music acts as a trigger for memory. The *"melancholy air of Italy"* from the street organ yanks her out of her romantic fantasies and violently back into the trauma of her mother's deathbed.
> - **Symbolic Dialogue:** The father's irrational cry, *"Damned Italians!"*, subtly reminds the reader of the seat of the Catholic Church's power in Rome, emphasizing how foreign/religious authority exerts an invisible, paralyzing control over Irish life.
> - **Intertextuality—Moore's "Fionnulla":** The title "Eveline" echoes Thomas Moore's lyric "Eveleen's Bower," which itself draws on the Irish legend of Fionnulla (or Fionnuala), a mythological figure transformed into a swan and condemned to wander the waters for 900 years. The intertext sets up a contrast Joyce deliberately dismantles: the mythical Ireland of song and enchantment versus the "scrupulously mean" reality of Dublin. Joyce mocks the myth and insists that the Irish poet must now *"pick apart the myths and deal with reality."* The "bell" that clangs upon Eveline's heart recalls the "sweet bell" that calls Fionnulla to heaven and freedom; but for Eveline, the church bell calls only to duty and paralysis. She cries out her own name—*"Eveline! Evvy!"*—in a tragic moment of self-recognition, and the answer is only the silence of the unsaved.

As she mused the pitiful vision of her mother's life laid its spell on the very quick of her being-that life of commonplace sacrifices closing in final craziness. She trembled as she heard again her mother's voice saying constantly with foolish insistence:
"Derevaun Seraun! Derevaun Seraun!" 
She stood up in a sudden impulse of terror. Escape! She must escape! Frank would save her. He would give her life, perhaps love, too. But she wanted to live. Why should she be unhappy? She had a right to happiness. Frank would take her in his arms, fold her in his arms. He would save her. 

> [!abstract] **Literary Techniques & Analysis**
> - **Repetition & Motif of Madness:** *"Derevaun Seraun!"* is an obscure and garbled Gaelic phrase. Scholars translate it to mean *"the end of pleasure is pain"* or *"the end of song is raving madness"*—the latter making perfect sense in view of her mother's *"final craziness."* The repetition embodies the mother's descent into madness—the ultimate form of mental paralysis that Eveline is desperate to avoid.
> - **Epizeuxis & Exclamatory Syntax:** *"Escape! She must escape!"* The sudden shift to short, panicked exclamations mimics Eveline's racing heartbeat and rising terror.
> - **The "Knight in Shining Armor" Trope:** Eveline views Frank less as a real person and more as a literary savior (*"Frank would save her"*), showing her reliance on fantasy to cope with her reality. Like the boys in the stories preceding "Eveline," she harbors a desperate desire for an exotic escape.
> - **The Mother-Daughter Dyad & Matrophobia:** Feminist critic Adrienne Rich (in *Of Woman Born*, 1976) theorized the mother-daughter relationship under patriarchal institutions as a "cathectic and passionate bond that cracks consciousness"—a bond in which a daughter experiences *"the phobic fear of becoming one's mother."* Rich called this "matrophobia," defining it as *"a womanly splitting of the self,"* a psychic wound where *"our personalities seem dangerously to blur and overlap with ours; and, in a desperate attempt to know where mother ends and daughter begins, we perform radical surgery."* Eveline's terror at the vision of her mother's life—her palpable desire to *"Escape! She must escape!"*—is a textbook moment of matrophobic crisis. The ghost of the mother is not merely a "haunting" but a recognition of the mother as *both* victim and mirror: *"the mother stands for the victim in ourselves, the unfree woman, the martyr."* Crucially, this bond is the product of how the Roman Catholic Church institutionalized motherhood into a life of obligatory sacrifice, not a natural pathology. The tragedy is that matrophobia binds Eveline more tightly to the mother's fate even as it drives her to flee it: the promise she made ensured she could never fully become anything except her mother.

She stood among the swaying crowd in the station at the North Wall. He held her hand and she knew that he was speaking to her, saying something about the passage over and over again. 
The station was full of soldiers with brown baggages. Through the wide doors of the sheds she caught a glimpse of the black mass of the boat, lying in beside the quay wall, with illumined portholes. She answered nothing. She felt her cheek pale and cold and, out of a maze of distress, she prayed to God to direct her, to show her what was her duty. The boat blew a long mournful whistle into the mist. If she went, tomorrow she would be on the sea with Frank, steaming towards Buenos Ayres. Their passage had been booked. Could she still draw back after all he had done for her? Her distress awoke a nausea in her body and she kept moving her lips in silent fervent prayer. 
A bell clanged upon her heart. She felt him seize her hand:

> - **Visual Imagery (Foreboding):** The ship is not described as a vessel of freedom, but as a *"black mass"* with *"illumined portholes,"* appearing monstrous and intimidating. 
> - **Auditory Imagery & Personification:** The boat blows a *"mournful whistle into the mist,"* personifying the ship with a sense of grief that foreshadows the story's tragic end.
> - **Metaphor:** *"A bell clanged upon her heart."* This evokes the sound of a church bell, linking her sudden, final paralysis directly back to the deeply ingrained religious guilt and duty that controls her.

"Come!" 
All the seas of the world tumbled about her heart. He was drawing her into them: he would drown her. She gripped with both hands at the iron railing.
"Come!"
No! No! No! It was impossible. Her hands clutched the iron in frenzy. Amid the seas she sent a cry of anguish.
"Eveline! Evvy!"
He rushed beyond the barrier and called to her to follow. He was shouted at to go on but he still called to her. She set her white face to him, passive, like a helpless animal. Her eyes gave him no sign of love or farewell or recognition.

> [!danger] **Literary Techniques & Analysis**
> - **Hyperbole & Destructive Metaphor:** *"All the seas of the world tumbled about her heart... he would drown her."* Defying logic, the ocean—which earlier represented escape—transforms into a symbol of destruction. She unconsciously conflates Frank with her abusive father, fearing he will destroy her.
> - **Epizeuxis (Repetition for Intensity):** *"No! No! No!"* encapsulates her absolute psychological break and refusal.
> - **Simile & The Joycean Epiphany:** *"passive, like a helpless animal."* The narrative zooms out from her internal thoughts (Free Indirect Discourse) to an entirely detached, objective perspective. This is her **Epiphany**—not a moment of triumph, but the devastating realization of her own inescapable paralysis. She is stripped of her humanity, rendered entirely incapable of action, expression, or even feeling.
> - **The Freudian Oedipal Subtext:** Several critics have observed that the final scene can be read through a Freudian Oedipal lens. Frank's repeated command *"Come!"* carries the unmistakable resonance of sexual climax—Eveline's answer is not *"Yes!"* (as in the conclusion of *Ulysses*) but *"No! No! No!"* The iron railing she frantically grips functions as a phallic substitute, echoing the first image of her father's "blackthorn stick." She clings to the object representing paternal authority rather than submitting to the lover. Her orgasm is denied; the railing sends her into a "frenzy" that recalls both her heart palpitations and her mother's nervous breakdown—a psychosexual collapse onto the safe, known, and structurally deadly.
> - **Two Critical Interpretations of Her Paralysis:** Critics debate the root cause of her final choice. **1.** Her harsh environment of duty has completely sapped her strength and ability to believe in hope, reducing her to a "helpless animal." **2.** Faced with the dichotomy of known Dublin vs. unknown Buenos Aires, she actively chooses the community she knows, realizing she cannot truly love a man who drifts without such roots.

> [!warning] **Psychoanalytic Reading: Trauma, Anxiety, and Paralysis**
> Psychoanalytic critics (Duš Ivanović, 2021; Murielle El Hajj, 2026) offer additional deep interpretations of the final collapse. Drawing on Freud's revised theory of anxiety (*Inhibitions, Symptoms, and Anxiety*, 1926), they argue that Eveline is the victim of *repressed psychological trauma* that erupts as an incapacitating anxiety attack at the quay. The momentary trigger—Frank seizing her hand and commanding *"Come!"* —reactivates the original trauma of her mother's deathbed and her father's violence. Freud theorized that when anxiety is *excessive*, the mind's "protective action" is overwhelmed: the flight mechanism fails, the ego collapses, and *paralysis from anxiety* sets in. Eveline's final state is not a choice but a neurological collapse. The iron railings she franticly grips become symbolic prison bars. The El Hajj synthesis extends this further through Lacanian, Kristevan, and Žižekian frameworks, arguing that the symptom *"precedes its cause"* —that is, the paralysis was always latent, awaiting a trigger; this reading ties the convergence of melancholic identification with the mother, foreclosure of the symbolic, ideologically impasse, and surplus-repression into a multidimensional model in which paralysis was *inevitable, not contingent.* A separate school of trauma scholars (Uddin, 2020) reads the story through *transgenerational trauma* theories (Cathy Caruth, Nicolas Abraham), arguing that Eveline did not suffer trauma directly: she *inherits* her mother's trauma, living with the symptoms and dissociation of a traumatized individual despite having experienced no direct victimization herself.

> [!abstract] **The Feminist Double-Bind & The Subaltern Woman**
> Feminist readings increasingly reject the frame of "paralysis from cowardice." Drawing on Marilyn Frye's theory of oppression as a "double bind"—in which *"options are reduced to a very few and all of them expose one to penalty, censure, or deprivation"*—scholars argue Eveline cannot escape either way. Stay with her father and she remains an unpaid domestic servant heading toward her mother's fate. Go with Frank and she becomes his possession in a city synonymous with sex trafficking. Either patriarch (father or future husband) will dominate her. The influence of colonial, patriarchal, and religious conditioning is so deeply internalized that she genuinely has *no choice at all*—every path leads to domination. Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's famous question "Can the Subaltern Speak?" is relevant here: Eveline is a subaltern woman who, despite desperately wanting to speak for her own life, cannot, because the structures that might give voice and reality to her desire do not yet exist. As scholar Bruce Stewart summarizes: she is *"damned if she goes and damned if she doesn't."*
