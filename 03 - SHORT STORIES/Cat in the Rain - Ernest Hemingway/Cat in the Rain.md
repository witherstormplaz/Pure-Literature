THERE WERE only two Americans stopping at the hotel. 

> The Iceberg Theory & Plot of Revelation (Craft · Hemingway's Style)
> 
> Hemingway's famous 'Iceberg Theory' (or 'Theory of Omission') posits that the emotional weight of a story lies in what is unstated. The opening sentence is a masterclass in compression: _two Americans, a hotel, no social connection._ Scholars often describe this as a "plot of revelation" disguised as a "plot of resolution." The surface narrative is a simple, futile search for a cat, but the submerged narrative reveals the profound emotional bankruptcy of their marriage. The physical isolation of the hotel immediately sets a tone of spatial confinement that mirrors their psychological state.

They did not know any of the people they passed on the stairs on their way to and from their room. 

Their room was on the second floor facing the sea. It also faced the public garden and the war monument.

> Isolation & Alienation (Theme · Isolation)
> 
> The repetition of _'their room'_ — twice in the opening — is not accidental. Their world is reduced to a single space. They are tourists in Italy but not participants in it; they pass people without knowing them. This anonymity characterizes the expatriate experience of the Lost Generation — Americans adrift in post-WWI Europe, belonging nowhere.

There were big palms and green benches in the public garden. In the good weather there was always an artist with his easel. 

Artists liked the way the palms grew and the bright colors of the hotels facing the garden and the sea.

> Theme · Creativity & Fulfillment
> 
> The Artist — Contrast & Irony
> 
> The artist is doing what the American wife cannot — he is present, engaged, creating, finding beauty in the world. He appears only _in good weather._ The wife looks out in the rain. She watches but does not create; she desires but cannot act. His presence sets up a quiet contrast: he has vocation and freedom; she has neither.

Italians came from a long way off to look up at the *war monument. It was made of bronze and glistened in the rain.*

> The War Monument — Historical Displacement
> 
> Written in 1923 and set in post-WWI Rapallo, Italy, the story juxtaposes the couple's banal domestic dissatisfaction against the backdrop of historical trauma. The monument, which _glistens in the rain_, represents the monumental sacrifice of the war—something the "Lost Generation" survived but could not escape. Biographically, Hemingway visited Rapallo with his wife Hadley shortly before she revealed her pregnancy. Scholars argue the monument anchors the expatriate alienation: they are tourists in a landscape of grief, unable to connect to either the local culture or each other.

It was raining. The rain dripped from the palm trees. Water stood in pools on the gravel paths. The sea broke in a long line in the rain and slipped back down the beach to come up and break again in a long line in the rain. 

> Literary Device · Spatial Criticism & Pathetic Fallacy
> 
> Pathetic Fallacy and Stagnation
> 
> Pathetic fallacy attributes human emotions to nature, but here Hemingway uses the weather as a structural boundary. Spatial criticism notes how the rain enforces physical confinement, trapping the characters in their room and exacerbating their psychological claustrophobia. The hypnotic repetition of the sea—_'break... slip back... come up and break again'_—mirrors the cyclical, futile nature of their communication. The landscape itself is devoid of progress, reflecting a marriage paralyzed by emotional impotence and inertia.

The motor cars were gone from the square by the war monument. 

Across the square in the doorway of the café a waiter stood looking out at the empty square.

> Literary Device · Symbolism
> 
> Emptiness as Setting
> 
> The square is _empty._ The cars are gone. The only figure is a waiter _looking out_ — just as the wife will soon be _looking out_ the window. Both are observers of an empty world. But the waiter is at work, with a function. The wife has no function given to her in this life.

The American wife stood at the window looking out. Outside right under their window *a cat was crouched under one of the dripping green tables. The cat was trying to make herself so compact that she would not be dripped on.*

> Literary Device · Symbolism
> 
> The Cat — Psychoanalytical Interpretations
> 
> The cat is the story's central, polyvalent symbol. From a psychoanalytical perspective, it represents the wife's unarticulated maternal desires and unmet emotional needs (often linked to Hadley Hemingway's pregnancy). It is explicitly _female_ ('herself', 'she'). The cat also functions as the wife's mirror: trapped, seeking shelter, and forced to make herself compact in an inhospitable, phallocentric environment. The crouching cat under the table signifies her own repressed agency and vulnerability within a marriage that provides no emotional shelter.

"I'm going down and get that kitty," the American wife said.  
"I'll do it," her husband offered from the bed.  
"No, I'll get it. The poor kitty out trying to keep dry under a table."  
  
The husband went on reading, lying propped up with the two pillows at the foot of the bed.

"Don't get wet," he said.

> Craft · Characterization
> 
> "Offered from the bed"
> 
> Three words that contain an entire characterization. George does not _get up,_ does not _move,_ does not even fully commit — he _offers._ And he does it _from the bed_ — his permanent position throughout the story. The bed represents his passivity and withdrawal. Hemingway's minimalism means he never says George is checked out. He just keeps him horizontal.

The wife went downstairs and *the hotel owner stood up and bowed to her as she passed the office.* His desk was at the far end of the office. He was an old man and very tall.

> Theme · Being Seen
> 
> The Padrone — Dignity & Recognition
> 
> The hotel-keeper does what George does not: he _stands up_ when she passes. This is acknowledgment, respect, ceremony. The wife is starving for this — to be _seen_ as a person of worth. The contrast between the padrone who rises and George who stays flat on his back is one of the story's most pointed structural ironies.

"Il piove," the wife said. *She liked the hotel-keeper.*

> She Liked the Hotel-Keeper
> 
> She speaks Italian — imperfectly ('Il piove' is slightly off from natural usage), which signals she is _trying._ She is reaching toward the local culture, toward something real and rooted. This is a story about a woman who wants to _belong_ somewhere — in a culture, in a marriage, in a domestic space — and is denied all three.

"Si, si, Signora, brutto tempo. It is very bad weather."

He stood behind his desk in the far end of the dim room. 

The wife liked him. She liked the deadly serious way he received any complaints. She liked his dignity. She liked the way he wanted to serve her. She liked the way he felt about being a hotel-keeper. She liked his old, heavy face and big hands.

> Literary Device · Anaphora
> 
> Anaphora — "She Liked"
> 
> Anaphora is the repetition of a phrase at the start of successive clauses. Hemingway repeats _'She liked'_ five times in quick succession. Each thing she likes about the padrone is something she does NOT have with George: seriousness, dignity, a feeling of being served, a sense of vocation, physicality. The list is the wife's marriage, written in negative.

Liking him she opened the door and looked out. It was raining harder. A man in a rubber cape was crossing the empty square to the café. The cat would be around to the right. Perhaps she could go along under the eaves. *As she stood in the doorway an umbrella opened behind her. It was the maid who looked after their room.*

*"You must not get wet," she smiled, speaking Italian. Of course, the hotel-keeper had sent her.*

> Craft · Structure & Contrast
> 
> The Umbrella — Unsolicited Care
> 
> The maid appears, sent by the padrone, umbrella already open. Nobody asked. He anticipated her need and acted on it — this is _proactive care,_ the kind of attentiveness she never receives from her husband. Compare: George's 'Don't get wet' is a warning, not an action. One man tells her not to get wet; another ensures she doesn't.

With the maid holding the umbrella over her, she walked along the gravel path until she was under their window. The table was there, washed bright green in the rain, but the cat was gone. She was suddenly disappointed. 

> Literary Device · Symbolism
> 
> The Cat Is Gone — Thwarted Desire
> 
> The cat's disappearance is the story's first structural disappointment. She geared herself up, went into the rain, and arrived — to find nothing. The _table is still there, washed bright green,_ but the thing she wanted under it is gone. The domestic space (table) remains; what should animate it has fled. This mirrors the larger pattern of her life.

The maid looked up at her.

"Ha perduto qualque cosa, Signora?"  
"There was a cat," said the American girl.  
"A cat?"  
"Si, il gatto."  
"A cat?" the maid laughed. "A cat in the rain?"  
"Yes," she said, "under the table." Then, *"Oh, I wanted it so much. I wanted a kitty."*

> Literary Device · Subtext
> 
> "Oh, I wanted it so much"
> 
> This is one of Hemingway's great moments of subtext breaking the surface. The wife has been composed, but now, talking to a maid she can barely communicate with, something breaks open. _'Oh, I wanted it so much.'_ The 'Oh' is the crack in the dam. This grief is too large for a stray cat — unless the cat is not really what she's grieving. This is the story's emotional hinge.

When she talked English the maid's face tightened.  
"Come, Signora," she said. "We must get back inside. You will be wet."  
"I suppose so," said the American girl.

They went back along the gravel path and passed in the door. The maid stayed outside to close the umbrella. *As the American girl passed the office, the padrone bowed from his desk.* 

> Craft · Linguistic Demotion
> 
> "American Girl" vs "American Wife"
> 
> Hemingway shifts between _'the American wife'_ and _'the American girl'_ intentionally. As literary critic David Lodge notes, this linguistic demotion maps her social and emotional infantilization. When she returns from her failed quest, standing in the rain, she is named 'girl.' She is stripped of the dignified adult status of 'wife' and rendered vulnerable and subordinate. This shift highlights how the men around her—both her neglectful husband and the paternalistic hotel-keeper—reduce her agency, treating her as a child rather than an equal.

Something felt very small and tight inside the girl. 

The padrone made her feel very small and at the same time really important. She had a momentary feeling of being of supreme importance. 

> Theme · Identity & Worth
> 
> "Small and Important" — A Paradox
> 
> _'The padrone made her feel very small and at the same time really important.'_ This paradox perfectly encapsulates her psychological dilemma. In her marriage, she experiences the smallness (infantilization, neglect) _without_ the compensating importance. The padrone, acting as a patriarchal but benevolent surrogate, provides both: she is diminished in power but elevated in significance. This _'momentary feeling of being of supreme importance'_ highlights her desperate need for external validation, framing her tragedy as an inability to generate self-worth independently.

She went on up the stairs. She opened the door of the room. George was on the bed, reading

Did you get the cat?" he asked, putting the book down.  
"It was gone."  
*"Wonder where it went to," he said, resting his eyes from reading.*

> Craft · Characterization
> 
> "Resting his eyes from reading"
> 
> George does not look at his wife — he rests his eyes _from reading._ She is not the focus of his gaze; she is merely where his eyes go when they need a break from the page. This is a surgically precise portrait of marital neglect delivered in a single subordinate clause. Hemingway never says George doesn't care. He just keeps him reading.

She sat down on the bed.  
  
"I wanted it so much," she said. "I don't know why I wanted it so much. I wanted that poor kitty. It isn't any fun to be a poor kitty out in the rain."  
  
George was reading again.  
  
She went over and *sat in front of the mirror of the dressing table looking at herself with the hand glass. She studied her profile, first one side and then the other. Then she studied the back of her head and her neck.*

> Literary Device · Symbolism
> 
> The Mirror — The Lacanian Gaze
> 
> The mirror scene represents a critical moment of self-examination. From a psychoanalytic (Lacanian) perspective, she is confronting her fragmented identity. By studying her profile, back, and neck, she is attempting to see herself from the outside, desperately searching for a cohesive sense of self that her husband refuses to validate. The mirror, typically a symbol of vanity, functions here as an instrument of existential inquiry. She is dissecting her physical form because her emotional form feels invisible.

"Don't you think it would be a good idea if I let my hair grow out?" she asked, looking at her profile again.  
  
George looked up and saw *the back of her neck, clipped close like a boy's.*

> The Bobbed Hair — Subverting the New Woman
> 
> The bobbed haircut (the _garçonne_ look) was a defining symbol of the 1920s "New Woman," signifying liberation and modernity. However, Hemingway subverts this feminist iconography. The wife is _tired_ of looking like a boy. The style that was supposed to represent freedom has become its own cage. Her desire to grow her hair out is a rejection of the androgynous modernism that George prefers (he likes it "the way it is"). It represents her longing to reclaim a traditional, tactile femininity in a modern world that feels sterile.

"I like it the way it is."  
  
"I get so tired of it," she said. "I get so tired of looking like a boy."

George shifted his position in the bed. He hadn't looked away from her since she started to speak.  
  
"You look pretty darn nice," he said.

She laid the mirror down on the dresser and went over to the window and looked out. It was getting dark.

"I want to pull my hair back tight and smooth and make a big knot at the back that I can feel," she said. "I want to have a kitty to sit on my lap and purr when I stroke her."  
  
"Yeah?" George said from the bed.  
  
"And I want to eat at a table with my own silver and I want candles. And I want it to be spring and I want to brush my hair out in front of a mirror and I want a kitty and I want some new clothes."

> Literary Device · Anaphora
> 
> The "I Want" Catalogue
> 
> The wife unleashes a litany of desires, each beginning with _'And I want'_ — anaphora at its most emotionally charged. Notice what she wants: _long hair_ (femininity), _a kitty_ (warmth, nurturing), _her own silver_ (domesticity, permanence), _candles_ (intimacy), _spring_ (life, renewal), _new clothes_ (self-expression). These are not materialistic demands — they are desires for _rootedness, tenderness, femininity, and a home._

"Oh, shut up and get something to read," George said. He was reading again.

> Literary Device · Dialogue as Violence
> 
> "Oh, shut up and get something to read"
> 
> George doesn't argue with her desires. He doesn't engage, challenge, or even mock them seriously. He simply _shuts them down_ — and returns to his book. The book is George's primary relationship in this story. He is always either reading it, returning to it, or resting his eyes from it. 'Was reading again' after dismissing his wife's deepest longings is a complete portrait of emotional abandonment.

His wife was looking out of the window. It was quite dark now and still raining in the palm trees.  
  
"Anyway, I want a cat," she said, *"I want a cat. I want a cat now. If I can't have long hair or any fun, I can have a cat."*

> Literary Device · Repetition
> 
> Triple Repetition — The Cat as Final Claim
> 
> _'I want a cat. I want a cat. I want a cat now.'_ After her entire catalogue of desires gets dismissed, she doesn't give up — she distills everything into the one thing. The triple repetition is not childishness; it is desperation pressed to its smallest form. She cannot have long hair or 'any fun' — so she plants a flag on the smallest possible territory: _a cat._ Heartbreaking and quietly defiant.

George was not listening. He was reading his book. His wife looked out of the window where the light had come on in the square.  
  
Someone knocked at the door.  
  
"Avanti," George said. He looked up from his book.  
  
In the doorway stood the maid. *She held a big tortoise-shell cat pressed tight against her and swung down against her body.*  
  
*"Excuse me," she said, "the padrone asked me to bring this for the Signora."*

> Literary Device · Ambiguity
> 
> The Ending — A Grotesque Substitution
> 
> The ending is deliberately ambiguous and heavily debated in literary criticism. A "big tortoise-shell cat" arrives. Critic Warren Bennett argues this is not the same cat, but a grotesque parody of her desire. Tortoiseshell cats are genetically almost always female, often symbolizing chaotic or sterile fertility. Pressed against the maid's body, the image mimics pregnancy, yet it is a coarse, unfulfilling substitute for what the wife truly wants. George's dismissive "Avanti" reinforces his continued blindness. Hemingway's submerged truth: superficial appeasement cannot fix structural brokenness.
