Tudors and Stuarts. The great Tudors, Henry VII, Henry VIII, and Elizabeth I, were not particularly impressive rulers. But theirs was a time of great success for England. The nation grew in unity, power, and respect among the nations of Europe. The monarchy generally worked in close cooperation with Parliament. Their era was marked by the glory of literature and the arts. The Stuart period was a stark contrast. Instead of unity and peace, there was chaotic civil war. Instead of harmony between king and parliament, there was constant conflict, ending in the victory of parliament. During most of this period, the state declined in power, and only under Cromwell did it grow in power.  Although the prosperity of literature and the arts continued, the main interesting aspect of this period was the religious and legal disputes.

Divine inheritance. Both James VI and I became kings of Scotland and England. Thus, the marriage arranged by Henry VII produced the desired result, and England and Scotland were united under a single ruler. Strong, wise. A king had the opportunity to rule well. But King James, although educated and talented, was neither strong nor wise. Before the end of his reign, he had undone many of the things that Queen Elizabeth had done. He also expressed ideas about the divine inheritance of kings that the Tudor kings had not discussed with their subjects. These famous words have meant many things at different times. James meant by this that kings received their power directly from God, that their power was unlimited, and that it was a sin to oppose it.  The country, though royalist, was not prepared to conform to such views. Thus James, by trying to obtain money by levying taxes without asking his parliament to give him money, was constantly at odds with that parliament. This conflict took various forms and continued until the Revolution of 1688. It was not that the kings did not accept that no tax could be levied without a mandate from parliament. But the kings were constantly trying to raise money by means that parliament thought were different from the imposition of taxes. Moreover, the king had quarreled with his parliament on religious matters. A strong Presbyterian wave was spreading throughout England. Knowing that Presbyterianism in Scotland had always been opposed to the monarchy, the king sought to oppose it in England. If his foreign policy had been successful, he would have been forgiven for much of this. But his foreign policy was a failure from beginning to end.  Although he abandoned his friendship with France and became friends with Spain, the switch did not bring him any advantage. He attempted to intervene in the Thirty Years' War, but his intervention not only failed but also made him a laughingstock.

Charles I. Thus the throne, which James I had certainly weakened, was succeeded in 1625 by Charles I. A better man than his father, though not so eloquent, he was more capable. He was a man of genuine religious faith, and a friend of the Church of England, and had a strong sense of justice. But like his father he was firmly attached to the idea of ​​divine inheritance. Thus a political controversy and two opposing principles met. Parliament and the king clashed decisively, and the life and reign of Charles I ended in a most tragic manner.

Our main idea is to consider the circumstances under which Charles came into conflict with Parliament. They quarrelled over two matters, namely, religion and politics. Their struggle was chiefly based on two points. (1) By whom should England be governed? By the king alone?  Should Parliament alone? Or should the King and Parliament together? (2) Should the Church of England control the entire religious and moral life of the English people? Or should it be tolerated that other institutions be granted this right? Should the Church of England be "Anglican"? Or should it be "Puritan"?

1 The reign of Charles I began with the disastrous failure of a foreign campaign. Richelieu had sent a military expedition to support the French Huguenots who were besieging La Rochelle, but this campaign was a complete defeat. The enraged Parliament forced the king to accept a declaration of the rights of the English in opposition to it. This declaration became known as the Petition of Rights. It reiterated that taxes could not be levied without the consent of Parliament, and that no Englishman could be imprisoned without trial. This was a great advantage that Parliament had thus taken from the king. But the king was not at all inclined to obey Parliament completely. In 1629 the king refused to do so, and, despite his promises, he raised money by various means.  The most important of these was the famous "Ship Tax". This was a tax levied on the whole country to build ships for a hypothetical national emergency. The power and future of Parliament were in danger, especially due to the determination and intelligence of Charles's chief advocates, Lord and Stafford.

The Long Parliament. Parliament was rescued from a religious conflict that had its origins in Scotland. James I had gradually tried to break up the Presbyterian system in Scotland and establish a system of government similar to the bishopric in the Church of England. Taking this idea as an example, Charles I forced the introduction of a Book of Common Prayer in Scotland, which closely resembled the English Book of Prayer. The result was war (the Bishops' War). Here, without the support of Parliament,  The armies that the king was able to muster were defeated. Charles I could not wage war without money. He was unable to raise sufficient funds without Parliament. Thus, he was forced to call Parliament again after an interval of eleven years.

Thus, in 1640, the Long Parliament met. Although it was not a republican institution, it sought to oppose the king's right to govern himself as he pleased, both the church and the state. While the king initially complied, Parliament was emboldened by the victory it had won to make further demands. Parliament was also angered by the king's alleged collusion with the Roman Catholics in Ireland and by a rebellion by Catholics in Northern Ireland against the Protestants.

Civil War. Thus began a civil war. Initially, the king's forces were victorious. He was supported by the north and west of England, most of the aristocracy, and the peasantry of England. The Parliamentarian party, especially the large cities, including London, and the merchant classes in the south and east of England.  Until Cromwell came to the fore, the peasantry provided a cavalry force that Parliament could not match. Cromwell defeated the king at Marston Moor (1644) and raised the New Model Army to defeat Charles at Naseby (1645). But the victory was not only for Cromwell's new army. It was also important to persuade the Cots to join the Parliamentarian party. Parliament had to pay a high price for their support. The Parliamentarian party had to accept the Solemn League and Covenant and pledge to reform the Church of England on the model of Presbyterianism.

The assassination of the king. After the Battle of Naseby, the king became a prisoner of the Parliamentarian party.  But now the situation took a strange turn. The army was the most extraordinary that the world had ever seen. It was largely self-governing and independent in its ideas, but it was full of religious and political fervor. The ideas of the army were not the ideas of Parliament. Under its leaders, especially Cromwell and Depardieu, it became opposed to Parliament. The king saw this battle as an opportunity to save himself. He talked with the Scots, and this time they were persuaded to invade England against England. But the English army was in a position to face any danger. The Scots were defeated. Parliament was subdued, and after Pride's purge the discontented members were expelled by the generals. Acting on the orders of the army, Parliament brought Charles to court, who sentenced him to death, and executed him (1649).  After this, a commonwealth or commonwealth was established.

Cromwell. This had been done by the army. Now the army and its leader Cromwell had seized power. There were enemies on all sides. Ireland was in great turmoil, but everyone there was united in showing their dislike for Cromwell and his army. Scotland was hostile. The majority of the English people were horrified by the murder of the king and opposed the religious and political views of Cromwell and his supporters. Cromwell was not a public tyrant. He did not want to base his rule on the army. But the influence of facts was stronger than his will. If he had allowed the will of the people to act freely, he would have been overthrown. His power did not waver for a moment while he lived. But his son, who was weaker than him, failed to control the army, and his power soon disappeared.

 Cromwell oppressed Ireland with great force and cruelty. He seized large tracts of land and distributed them among his soldiers and supporters. He later invited his son Charles I to become king of Scotland. Cromwell was forced to go to Scotland to avoid the threat posed by that party. For a time he was in great danger, but by his steadfastness, skill, and a certain amount of luck he overcame all obstacles. He defeated the Scots at Dunbar and defeated Charles II at Worcester (1651). After that, no one dared to take the field against him. He succeeded in crushing military opposition.  He was not very good at dealing with his political opponents. His position was not tenable. He hoped to be a constitutional monarch, ruling by the will of the people under whatever title he might be called. But the people did not want him as a monarch, and he had to rely solely on the support of the armed forces. He was proclaimed Lord Protector. Parliament was elected on a reformed basis, somewhat similar to the methods used in the Reform Act of 1832. But Parliament was soon dissolved in a quarrel with Cromwell. When he took office in 1658, he had not found a way to resolve the political difficulties, but he had acted as a "good constable" to prevent further civil war.

Soon after his death, the whole organization collapsed. His virtuous and weak son, Richard Cromwell, soon resigned his post, unable to maintain his command of the army. The leaders of the army then quarreled. The nation as a whole rejoiced at the weakening of this great power, which had won the fear and respect of England on the Continent, but which had ruled against the will of the nation at home. Royalists, Presbyterians, Anglicans, Scots, Irish, English, etc., all began to have hopes. On some points they all agreed. The kingdom must be re-established and a genuine parliament elected. The first act was to recall Charles II from exile in Holland.

Charles II returned to power.  Charles II had led a life of great adventure. It had long seemed impossible that he would ever again become king. But now he was welcomed back with almost frenzied enthusiasm by a seemingly unanimous people. Patient and easy-going, he was determined not to "repeat his adventures": his somewhat cynical wit had enabled him to sail safely through the storms of his reign, and to leave his brother James II a secure throne.

He soon realized that if the monarchy was restored, Parliament was restored too. The confidence in Parliament, though not so vociferously declared, seemed to him to be as strong.  Soon, the competition between the king and parliament resumed, albeit in different forms, but just as fiercely as before. 

Many things were against Charles II. His court was immoral and costly. England was embroiled in a war with Holland, and suffered great humiliation. A fleet of Dutch ships entered the Thames and briefly besieged London. This defeat was believed to be due to the king's inadequate support for his ministers and the corruption of his finances. The plague in London and the fire of London (1666) during the Dutch War further displeased public opinion. Parliament did little more than impeach the king's chief minister, Clarendon.

2 Wars and Louis XIV. The king, who had been searching for help, had always hoped to secure it through an alliance with Louis XIV of France. He concluded the secret Treaty of Dover with Louis in 1670.  Thus Charles promised to join France in attacking Holland, and Louis XIV promised to help him establish a strong monarchy in England, and to declare himself a Roman Catholic and a follower of the Catholic Church. But little came of this agreement that was favorable to the aspirations of the two kings. The Dutch War did break out. But we have seen how the Dutch rallied after the first defeats. Parliament soon forced Charles to withdraw from the war. 2 Charles's religious and political ideas were not realized.

Not only that, but it had more serious consequences. The truth about it spread. Or rather, suspicions began to arise. As a result, a party called the Whigs soon arose. This party, opposed to the power of the crown, was anxious to submit the kingdom to Parliament.  The instigator and leader of the party was Lord Salisbury. That party came very near to victory. Rumours of a papal conspiracy were rife. It was alleged that the Catholics of England and Europe had conspired to assassinate Charles II and secure the throne for his brother, a true Catholic, James. There was great panic and widespread suspicion. Many were imprisoned. Some were put to death on evidence that was undoubtedly false. Meanwhile the Whigs were working in Parliament for a bill of disqualification. It was to be declared definitively that James could not succeed to the throne. Charles felt the danger. Not only was his brother's inheritance attacked, but his own power was also attacked. He dissolved parliaments one after another. But he did not meet with a single parliament that was not strongly opposed to him.  However, a twist of fate occurred. The Whig reaction had become too violent. The people realized that the charges against the Catholics were foolish and wicked. A great reaction arose in favor of the king. He succeeded in recalling his brother James from Scotland. In the last three years of his reign, the king, after his restoration to the throne, seemed more powerful and more popular than ever.

James II. When James II came to the throne in 1685, the opportunity seemed ripe for the monarchy to develop its power against Parliament. The Whigs had been completely discredited. The newly elected House of Commons was as enthusiastically partisan as the Parliament that had restored Charles II to the throne. England, following the example of many European states, including the Praetorians, sought to base its government on the king's unlimited power. The forces that supported the king were not as strong as they seemed. But the reign of James II was not ruined by any political force, but by the religious fervor of the English people, fueled by the fact that James II was a devout Roman Catholic.

 Although England's religious life had fallen into a deep decline in many respects, anything connected with Rome or the papacy was regarded with hatred and suspicion by the greater part of the nation. James II hoped to establish the crown as sovereign, and to permit the open expression of Roman Catholicism in England. The first object provoked a lasting opposition. But the second, which was better, safer, and more desirable, was the storm that drove James II from the throne.

In the first year of the new reign, rebellions broke out in Scotland and the south of England. But they were easily suppressed, and the suppression increased the king's power. He then revealed his plans.  He proposed to repeal the laws against all who did not worship according to the Church of England, whether Roman Catholic or Protestant. This would indeed give the Roman Catholics the freedom they had hoped for. Parliament rejected the king's proposals and was dissolved. He then set about carrying out his ideas without a parliament, or in the manner in which Louis XIV might be expected to act in France. He appointed Roman Catholics to the army. He also appointed them to the universities by force. He then issued a conciliatory proclamation, removing the penalties imposed on dissenters, whether Protestants or Roman Catholics. If the country accepted it, both his ideas would be achieved. Parliament would be humiliated and the freedom and influence of Roman Catholicism would be guaranteed.  James II hoped that if the country did not accept it, he would organize Ireland and Scotland to support him in order to crush all opposition. In the midst of the fighting, a son was born to him. Therefore, he hoped that if he won, his victory would be permanent and extend into the future.

Revolution. The unexpected intensity of the opposition frustrated all his efforts. The Whigs saw their opportunity. Seven of their leading men sent a letter signed by them, inviting William, the husband of James II's daughter and the Stathholder of the United Provinces, to come to England and defend the two inseparable principles of Protestantism and constitutional liberty. When he arrived, all the schemes of James II collapsed like a toy. In England he suffered no significant blow. He fled to France, where he was happily received by Love. The arrangements which James II had made in Scotland were likewise frustrated.  A sudden rebellion overthrew his political arrangements, and with them the Church of England, which had been so laboriously established in Scotland after the restoration of the monarchy. England and Scotland had been united at first by a common adherence to the Protestant Reformation, and although the English form of Protestantism was very different from the Scottish form, the two countries were now brought closer together by a common devotion to Protestantism and constitutional government. Ireland had a very different story. The English and Scots had abandoned King James II for a reason, and for the same reason, the Irish had supported him. Most of them were Roman Catholics, and the English government had become so oppressive to them that they gladly accepted every opportunity to overthrow it. They came close to victory.  The English and Protestant armies were besieged at Londonderry and Enniskillen. James II was enthusiastically welcomed in Dublin. Then fortunes changed dramatically. Londonderry was liberated. William III himself led an army to Ireland and decisively defeated James II's army at the Battle of the Boyne. This was followed by resistance. But after that battle the Stuart and Catholic cause in Ireland could not hope for lasting military success. The century that followed was a very sad one in Irish history, as English Protestant rule was re-established.

Thus was the Revolution of 1688 completed. It established the power of Parliament in England. From then on no tax could be levied without the consent of Parliament. Nor could an army be maintained. 3 It was Parliament that gave William the throne. It was Parliament that arranged who should succeed him. The Roman Catholics, suspected of having organized conspiracies which they had not themselves arranged, were excluded from political affairs and remained in the same unjust position as before. The universities and the learned professions were forbidden to them. Although Protestant dissenters were granted relief, it was by no means equal to the full equality which they should have enjoyed. It must be remembered that the Parliament thus established was not representative of the whole nation, but was controlled by the landed aristocracy.

The union of England and Scotland. A free parliament and a Presbyterian church were firmly established in Scotland. But Scotland was completely independent of England. The two countries were united by the fact that both had one king. Soon there were bitter disputes between the two countries over colonial and economic affairs. We must look forward to the reign of Queen Anne with interest. It should be noted that a union between the two countries was finally arranged in 1707. Thus, Scotland also took no less part in the colonial and commercial affairs of the country that was to be called Great Britain.

3 William. As a result of the revolution, a war broke out with France. 14 Louis, because he supported the cause of James II.  It was a long and tiring battle, fought with great determination by William III. But the English army did not gain a single victory in this battle. Even so, France was exhausted, and in 1697 it accepted the Peace of Ryswick and accepted the Protestant right to the throne of England.

William had no children. Parliament had decided that after his death Anne, Queen Mary's sister and daughter of 2nd Earl of Cornwall, should succeed to the throne, and that if Anne had no children, the throne should pass to the Protestant House of Hanover.

Queen Anne. Queen Anne's reign was mainly devoted to the War of the Spanish Succession. The origins and causes of that war are discussed in Chapter 9. Here we need only focus on domestic affairs. The main event was the union with Scotland, already mentioned.  The serious question arose in the latter half of the reign whether the throne should go to the House of Hanover, as provided for by the Act of Parliament in connection with the Settlement, or whether the Stuarts should be recalled. The Stuarts seemed to have a good opportunity. The Revolution of 1688, the political and religious aspirations of James II, had forced the country to accept it. But it was not really popular. The English Church still preached the doctrine of the divine inheritance of kings. If that doctrine were true, the rightful heir to the English throne was the son of James II, the man mentioned in English history as the former heir. The Tories took this view. The Queen also liked the idea of ​​her half-brother becoming the heir to the throne.  Bollingbrack and Oxford conspired fervently to secure the succession to the throne for the "old heir." Had he not been a Roman Catholic, it would have been impossible to prevent him from succeeding, but he was a Roman Catholic. He was unwilling to convert. Queen Anne died before all arrangements could be made. She was succeeded by George I of Hanover.

Culture and Religion. The period covered by this chapter is a very interesting and remarkable one in English history. We have considered the changes in it as a separate matter, as the best system of benevolence guides us through a very turbulent period. But important things were happening outside of politics and war. There was also a great change in the Church of England. Under the guidance of the Lord Bishop, it too was claiming more clearly and firmly the right of the medieval Christian Church, and was now claiming to be a Catholic Church. At the same time, the religious institutions which had separated from the Church of England were also developing in organization. The seventeenth century was their heroic age. Moderate Presbyterianism was represented by Baxter, an excellent preacher and theologian.  The Congregationalism or Independent Church was supported by Cromwell and Milton. William Penn was one of the members of the Quakers or Friends founded by George Joppa. Bunyan illuminated the Baptist community by his life and by his great allegorical poem The Pilgrim's Progress. It was a great age in literature too. Shakespeare was at work when James 1 came to the throne. Milton wrote Paradise Lost in the reign of Charles 2. In compensation for the neglect shown him during his lifetime, later generations have given his poems a growing respect. Great things happened in science also in this age. The greatest thing happened in science before the nineteenth century was in this age. It produced Newton.  His Principia, published in 1687, laid the foundation for modern astronomy.

The Colonization of America. One other feature of this period is to be noted. None is more important. The east coast of North America was filled with English colonies in the seventeenth century. This colonization was a decisive step in the movement which was to spread the influence of Europe throughout the world, including its systems of government, science, art, and religion, and to transform the world into a greater Europe. The process of colonization had been going on for much of the century. 1 Early in the reign of James I (1607), the colony of Virginia, planned in the reign of Queen Elizabeth, was actually established. This colony was established in 1632. The movement which gave North America its most characteristic character and which was to profoundly influence its destiny, had already begun in 1620.  In that year the ship Maple Leaf landed a group of settlers. Most of them had been residents of Holland for some time. They were religious independents who, dissatisfied with the English government, had come to America in search of a place where they could practice their religious beliefs. The colony they settled in was called Massachusetts, and its capital was named Boston, after the English residence of some of them. This Massachusetts colony was the first of a series of New England colonies, Puritan in religion, narrow in many ideas, but as powerful and courageous as their devout brothers in England. The society they established was democratic and egalitarian, socially and politically. The system of government and the way of life of the United States of America bear clear signs of this influence. It will continue to do so.

Under the restored Stuarts, the colony and its possessions developed rapidly. Carolina was established in 1663. As a result of the Dutch War, England acquired the Dutch colony, which had been separated from the New England colony of Virginia. The area was originally called New Amsterdam. Later, under King James II, the name of the colony was changed to New York, in honor of the king's brother, James, Duke of York. The only other colony worth mentioning here is Pennsylvania, established in 1681. Its founder was William Penn, a leading member of the League of Friends and a great influence on Charles II.  He, who remained true to the principles he had preached at home, gave his colony several special features. He established there a complete religious freedom, such as had not been seen in any other state. He established a very liberal democratic system of government. He established peace and friendship with the Indian tribes, with whom the other colonies were constantly at war. He gave the name Philadelphia to the capital, meaning the city of brotherly love.

Thus English strife and intolerance largely led to the establishment of the American colonies. In 1917, it was a strange twist of fate that led these American colonies, which were then united as the United States of America, to participate in the great European war to establish freedom among the nations of the world.
