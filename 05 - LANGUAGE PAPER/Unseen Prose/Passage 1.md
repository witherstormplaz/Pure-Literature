It was Jenny's first circus. A girl in purple tights, on a galloping horse, a clown, an elephant ripped through the middle. "And did you see the elephant?" she asked her brother Tom, who had found the piece of poster in the street when he was in town marketing the butter and eggs. "Was it really there? And the clown?"

But the ecstatic, eleven-year-old quiver in her voice, made him feel that perhaps in picking up the poster he had been unworthy of his own seventeen years; so with an offhand shrug he drawled, "Everybody said it wouldn't amount to much. A few ponies and an elephant or two—but what's an elephant?"

She wheeled from him, resenting his attempt to scoff away such wonders. It was supper-time, her father just in from the field, so off she sped to greet him, her throat too tight to cry out, passionate to communicate her excitement, to find response. But the old roan Billie took fright at the fluttering poster, and her father shouted for her to watch what she was doing and keep away from the horses. For a minute she stood quite still, impaled by the rebuff; then, as swiftly as before, ran to the house. "Look—" she pierced it shrilly— "what Tom brought me—a circus," and with the poster outstretched she sprang to the stove where her mother was frying pork. There was no rebuff this time. Instead, an incredible kind of pity. "Never mind, Jenny." A hot hand gentle on her cheek. "Your day's going to come. You won't spend all your life among chickens and cows or I'm not the woman I think I am!" And then, an angry clatter of stove lids that made her shrink away dismayed, in sudden dread of her father's coming and the storm that was to break. Not a word until he was sitting down at the table. Then as the platters were clumped in front of him he asked, "What's wrong?" and for answer, her mother hurled back, "Wrong? You—and the farm—and the debts—that's what's wrong. There's a circus in town, but do we go? Do we ever go anywhere?

Other children have things, and enjoy themselves, but look, look at it! That's how much of the circus my girl gets! Nothing ahead of her but chickens and cows! Another ten years—can't you just see the big, gawky know-nothing she's going to be?" Jenny gulped, startled. Ten years from now it was a quite different kind of young lady she intended to be. For a moment there was a clammy fear her mother might be right—and then she was furious. She wasn't gawky and she wasn't know-nothing. She was farther on in school than any other girl her age. She could do fractions and percentages and draw the map of North America with her eyes shut. Her mother to talk, who only last Sunday when she was writing a letter had to ask how to spell 'necessary'! But suddenly the din between her mother and father split apart, and it was Tom speaking. "Can't you hold on and let us eat in peace? We've heard all that before." Jenny shivered, it was so fine and brave of Tom. "Come on, Jenny, you're not eating anyway. We'll go out and leave them to it." "It's too bad all right you couldn't go to the circus," Tom consoled her, "but everybody said it wouldn't be worth the money."

She glanced up puzzled, impatient. Pity again! "There's Dad now, starting for the barn," Tom nudged her. "Better go and finish your supper." And she was afraid of her mother tonight. Afraid because all at once she felt defenseless. This sudden dilation of life—it was like a bubble blown vast and fragile. In time it might subside, slowly, it might even remain full-blown, gradually strengthening itself, but tonight she was afraid. Afraid that before the hack of her mother's voice it might burst and crumple.

So when she found the kitchen deserted, there was an isolated moment of relief. She glided across the kitchen, took down the poster from where it still hung over the calendar and fled with it to the barn. She lay limp in the hay, listening to her heartbeat subside. And there, in its dim, high stillness, she had her circus. Not the kind that would stop off at a little town. Not just a tent and an elephant or two. No—for this was her own circus; the splendid, matchless circus of a little girl who had never seen one. "You'll catch it," Tom said when he found her, "hiding up here instead of helping with the dishes." Catch it she did, but for once the threats of what would happen next time failed to touch her. The circus went on. All night long she wore her purple tights and went riding Billie round and round the pasture in them.

**Questions:**

1. What effect does the poster of the circus have on Jenny?
2. Bring out the contrast between Jenny's and her mother's vision on Jenny's future?
3. What does the passage suggest about Tom?
4. Bring out a literary device used in the passage and its purpose?
5. What is the ultimate source of conflict in the passage?

Answer 1:

The poster electrifies Jenny because it is all she gets of the circus she has never seen.
At first it produces ecstatic, physical excitement — the "ecstatic, eleven-year-old quiver in her voice," her "throat too tight to cry out," and her being "passionate to communicate her excitement." This drives her frantic movement through the passage: she "sped," "sprang" with the "poster outstretched," desperate to find response from Tom, her father and mother.

When that wonder is threatened — by Tom's scoffing or her father's shout — she fiercely protects it: she "wheeled from him, resenting his attempt to scoff away such wonders." The experience is described as a "sudden dilation of life — it was like a bubble blown vast and fragile," leaving her feeling defenseless and afraid it will "burst and crumple."

Finally, the poster pushes her into imagination as refuge. She "fled with it to the barn" and "there, in its dim, high stillness, she had her circus" — not a small-town tent, but "her own circus; the splendid, matchless circus of a little girl who had never seen one." So powerful is the effect that "All night long she wore her purple tights and went riding Billie round and round the pasture in them," untouched even by punishment.
