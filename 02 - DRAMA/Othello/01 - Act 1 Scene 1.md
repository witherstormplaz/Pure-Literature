### **Character Analysis**

**Iago:**

- He is established as a **deceptive and vengeful** figure who openly admits his duplicity: _"I am not what I am"_ and _"I must show out a flag and sign of love, / Which is indeed but sign."_
- Iago harbors deep resentment toward Othello for passing him over for a promotion in favor of Michael Cassio, a man he dismisses as a _"great arithmetician"_ who _"never set a squadron in the field"_ and whose soldiership is _"mere prattle without practice."_
- He explicitly states his philosophy of self-interest: _"I follow him to serve my turn upon him"_ and _"In following him, I follow but myself."_
- His language is frequently crude and provocative, especially when using animal imagery to incite others: _"an old black ram / Is tupping your white ewe."_

---

**Roderigo:**

- A wealthy but **weak-willed and easily manipulated** Venetian who has been paying Iago to help him win Desdemona's hand, he opens the play by complaining: _"thou, Iago, who hast had my purse / As if the strings were thine."_
- He is portrayed as gullible and driven entirely by infatuation: _"What a full fortune does the thick-lips owe, / If he can carry 't thus!"_ — revealing that even his racial contempt is filtered through personal jealousy.

---

**Brabantio:**

- A high-ranking Venetian Senator, he is initially dismissive of Roderigo, having forbidden him from _"haunting about my doors."_
- He represents the **established social order** of Venice and is deeply disturbed by what he calls the _"treason of the blood"_ represented by his daughter's secret marriage.
- He is quick to believe that his daughter has been _"abused, stolen from me, and corrupted / By spells and medicines bought of mountebanks,"_ because he cannot conceive of her choosing Othello of her own free will: _"For nature so preposterously to err / ... Sans witchcraft could not."_

---

**Othello (The Moor):**

- Though he does not appear in this scene, he is characterised through the biased perspectives of Iago and Roderigo, who use **derogatory and racialised language** such as _"Thick-lips," "old black ram,"_ and _"the devil."_
- Despite their personal hatred, Iago concedes the state needs him: _"Another of his fathom they have none / To lead their business"_ — referring to the _"Cyprus wars."_

---

### **Themes**

**Appearance vs. Reality:**

- Iago is the primary vehicle for this theme, explicitly describing his strategy of hiding his true intentions: _"I must show out a flag and sign of love, / Which is indeed but sign"_ and boasting _"I am not what I am."_
- He argues that those who appear devoted are merely self-interested: _"In following him, I follow but myself; / Heaven is my judge, not I for love and duty."_

---

**Racism and Xenophobia:**

- The scene is saturated with racial prejudice. Iago uses **provocative animal metaphors** to cast Othello as subhuman: _"an old black ram / Is tupping your white ewe"_ and _"you'll have your daughter covered with a Barbary horse."_
- He warns Brabantio that _"the devil will make a grandsire of you"_ — equating Othello with the devil through skin colour.
- Othello is cast as an outsider and categorised as _"an extravagant and wheeling stranger / Of here and every where."_

---

**Jealousy and Envy:**

- Iago's entire plot is initiated by professional envy: _"I know my price, I am worth no worse a place"_ — furious that Cassio, a _"great arithmetician,"_ was preferred over him.
- He resents Cassio's _"bookish theoric"_ against his own battlefield experience: _"Mere prattle without practice / Is all his soldiership."_
- Roderigo's jealousy is personal: _"What a full fortune does the thick-lips owe, / If he can carry 't thus!"_

---

**Order vs. Chaos:**

- The scene takes place at night, mirroring the **disruption of social and familial order.** Iago deliberately weaponises the darkness: _"Call up her father; / Rouse him, make after him, poison his delight."_
- Brabantio views Desdemona's elopement as an assault on natural law: _"For nature so preposterously to err ... Sans witchcraft could not."_
- Iago purposefully incites chaos with urgency and alarm: _"Thieves! thieves! thieves! / Look to your house, your daughter, and your bags!"_

---

### **Literary Techniques**

**Animal Imagery:**

- Iago dehumanises Othello through a cascade of animal comparisons designed to shock Brabantio: _"an old black ram / Is tupping your white ewe," "you'll have your daughter covered with a Barbary horse,"_ and the notorious _"making the beast with two backs."_

---

**Metaphor:**

- Iago uses an extended **garden metaphor** to articulate his philosophy of self-determination: _"Our bodies are our gardens, to the which our wills are gardeners."_
- He also frames his false service in mercenary, transactional terms: _"I follow him to serve my turn upon him"_ — as though loyalty is merely a coat to be worn for profit.

---

**Dramatic Irony:**

- The audience is immediately made aware of Iago's villainous nature: _"I am not what I am"_ — a direct inversion of God's self-declaration in Exodus (_"I am that I am"_), which signals Iago's Satanic quality.
- This creates tension as other characters trust him completely; Othello himself will later call him _"honest Iago"_ — a phrase made devastating by what we already know.

---

**Hyperbole:**

- Iago uses exaggerated, urgent language to rouse Brabantio, comparing the situation to a city on fire: _"what, ho, Brabantio! thieves! thieves!"_ and warning that Brabantio has been _"robbed"_ and lost _"half your soul."_

---

**Juxtaposition:**

- Shakespeare sharply contrasts Cassio's _"bookish theoric"_ with Iago's claimed practical _"proof"_ of military service, creating a tension between academic credentials and lived experience.
- There is also a stark juxtaposition between the **civility of Venice** — its senators, laws, and decorum — and the _"gross revolt"_ Iago describes happening in the night, associating Othello with the transgression of every social boundary.
