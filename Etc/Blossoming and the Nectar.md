In Mahinda’s family, everyone regarded his father’s elder sister as a very good and generous woman. Mahinda thought so too; in those days he had great respect for the opinion of his elders. His father said, “Now try to be like Loku Naenda, Mahinda. She’s an example to us all.” His mother said, “Loku Naenda has more shradda than all of us.” Loku Naenda never killed anything, not even a mosquito. Once, Mahinda saw her saving some ants that had fallen into a basin of water; even the most insignificant creature benefited from Loku Naenda’s attentions. Loku Naenda never stole; she had a large house and garden, a lot of jewellery, and a small coconut property in Matara. She had everything she wanted. She never lied. She often said she never did, and of course everyone believed her. Loku Naenda’s conduct was always irreproachable. She was a broad woman, a bit on the short side and very dark; her nose and lips were thick, her skin coarse. She had a large mole on the tip of her nose and another with a hair in it on her chin. At the back of her head was a very small konde (bun). Unless they were her relatives, Loku Naenda kept all men at a safe distance; and they kept Loku Naenda at an equally safe distance. She had never married. As for drinking or smoking—even the thought of her doing either of these things made Mahinda want to laugh.

Once, Punchi Naenda caught Siripala and Mahinda sharing a cigarette in the back garden. The first thing she said was, “How disappointed Loku Naenda will be, Mahinda. You’re only fifteen, but you’re already chasing these bad habits!” She wasted no time telling his father who delivered a stern lecture: “There will be no smoking or drinking in this house. Not under my roof”. 

When Loku Naenda was informed, she fixed Mahinda with a long heavy silence. Finally she spoke, her voice and certain. “Mahinda, there’s no need for me to say much. Your own Karma will deal with you. Smoke as much as you like. When you get lung cancer, when your lungs rot. This gratification of the senses brings only disease, death and *sansara*. Mark my words”

Punchi Naenda nodded vigorously beside her. “You hear that, Mahinda? We are only advising you for your own good. No need to look the other way!”

Mahinda kept his gaze lowered, saying nothing. Inside, a quiet displeasure burned. He was tired of their endless sermons about sin and karma, especially when the words came from people who seemed to take such satisfaction in controlling others.

He continued to look the other way, though lately, his thoughts had been drifting toward other kinds of forbidden gratification. Ones that had nothing to do with cigarettes.

Punchi Naenda was also unmarried and had no household of her own. Though she constantly sang Loku Naenda’s praises, she seemed to prefer living in Mahinda’s house. At the time of the cigarette incident, she could talk of nothing but yet another example of Loku Naenda’s generosity.

This time, the meritorious deed was especially admirable. Loku Naenda was bringing a girl from Matara to her house, the daughter of a toddy tapper. Not as a daughter, of course. That would have been unthinkable. A girl of such low birth could never become part of their family. Loku Naenda was far too practical for that. Instead, Kusuma was to come as a servant.

Everyone agreed it was a great kindness. In one stroke, the girl would be lifted from a crowded, half-starved hut into a proper house with good food, decent clothes, and regular wages. What more could anyone ask for?

Nangi and Mahinda were at Loku Naenda’s house the morning Kusuma arrived. Her father, a thin, weathered toddy tapper, had brought her himself. He said she was eighteen, though her small, skinny frame and wide, nervous eyes made her seem younger. Lice crawled in her thick, curly black hair, and a raw sore marked one knee. She stood quietly, clutching a small bundle of clothes, clearly overwhelmed.

She had grown up in a cramped hut as one of eight brothers and sisters, half-starved and often beaten. After handing her over, the man left quickly, clearly uncomfortable in such a fine house.

Once he was gone, Father shook his head and said, “These toddy tapper families… I’ve heard how some of the girls from those huts turn out. Running wild as they get older, lifting their cloths for any boy who looks at them. No discipline at all.” He gave a short, knowing chuckle. “But your Kusuma seems like a quiet one. She’s very lucky to come here. A proper house like Akka’s will teach her how to behave.”

Mother nodded in agreement. “It must feel like heaven to her. She’s not bad looking under all that dirt. With regular food she’ll soon fill out nicely. I just hope she doesn’t turn greedy or start stealing. That has to be stopped right from the start.”

Punchi Naenda was quick to add, “Akka knows exactly how to handle these village girls. She gives them so much. If they misbehave anyway, it’s nothing but raw wickedness. As she always says, no one can escape the karmic law.”

Father agreed firmly. “Exactly. She’ll learn discipline here.”

A week later, Kusuma had begun to settle into the rhythms of Loku Naenda’s large two-story wooden house. Her own room was upstairs, small and simple, with just enough space for a narrow bed, a wooden cupboard, and a small window overlooking the garden. There was a modest washroom on the same floor shared by the upstairs rooms. Loku Naenda was strict about the rules: all lights upstairs, including in Kusuma’s room, had to be out by nine every night. “Discipline starts with small things,” she had said firmly.

Loku Naenda kept her busy from before dawn until after dark, sweeping, dusting the heavy ebony furniture, polishing brass lamps and trays, washing clothes, and helping in the kitchen. Mahinda was sometimes puzzled by Loku Naenda’s way of talking about the karmic law. Of course they all knew about karma. He remembered very well what the monks in the temple used to say: everybody had to take the consequences of his actions in one way or another. If you wanted too many things your desires would make you linger in *sansara*; you would be a prisoner of your desires. That’s what the monk said. But Mahinda wasn’t sure that he understood. Because Loku Naenda, who was so wise, seemed to want a lot in return for whatever she did. But in those days he didn’t bother too much about such things. He had so many more important things to think about like how to dodge Pali classes, or ways and means of smoking without being caught and lectured to.

  

Loku Naenda was pleased with Kusuma. She was intelligent and learnt quickly. She soon learnt to be neat and clean. She was very helpful in the house. She dusted the furniture—all Loku Naenda’s carved ebony chairs and couches in the sitting room. She cleaned all the brass trays, lamps and vases. She was very good at fetching and carrying. Loku Naenda wondered whether she should teach Kusuma to read and write. She thought about it a bit. Then she told them that to teach Kusuma how to crochet would be far more useful. Lace table mats were in great demand and fetched a very good price. Loku Naenda was a very practical woman.

  

After Kusuma’s arrival, Nangi began to visit Loku Naenda almost every day. Kusuma knew very little. So Nangi began to feel very wise, though she knew very little herself. Mahinda was, of course, the really wise one among the younger lot. In those days, they all thought themselves very wise. But everyone acknowledged Loku Naenda to be the wisest. This was her own opinion as well—naturally.

  

It seemed to Mahinda that Nangi liked showing off a bit. She would sit with Kusuma on the verandah steps and tell her all about the wonders of the world. Had Kusuma ever been to Colombo? No. Then she wouldn’t have ever been in a lift, would she? No. Had she ever been on an escalator? No. Kusuma’s ignorance was so satisfying to Nangi! Had she ever been to the Zoo? No. What was a Zoo? Nangi was in her element. She told Kusuma all about the Zoo; the tigers, the lions, the bears, the giraffes, the kangaroos, the zebras, the red-black baboon, the elephants. Kusuma had seen an elephant! Oh! Nangi was quite disappointed. Where had Kusuma seen an elephant? Dragging logs on the road. That wasn’t so bad. The Zoo elephants didn’t do anything as silly and foolish as dragging logs. They balanced on little stools or skipped round the arena. And then all the people laughed and clapped. Kusuma longed to go to Colombo to see all those marvels. She asked Nangi a thousand and one questions. Nangi brought her picture books. Kusuma had never held a book in her hands before. She turned over the pages carefully. Nangi lent her the books for a few days. She couldn’t read, of course, but she loved looking at the pictures. Then Loku Naenda ordered Nangi to take the books away. Kusuma looked at the pictures too often. That very afternoon she was looking at pictures when she should have been polishing the brass.
