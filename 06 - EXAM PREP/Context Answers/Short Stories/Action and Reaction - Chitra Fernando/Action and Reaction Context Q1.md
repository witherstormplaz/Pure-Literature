>[!quote] "I have found money for it. It's a very meritorious deed. No one should interfere with such good things." "When will it be completed?" "The building will be completed in about... would you like to donate...?" 

==These lines are taken from the short story, Action and Reaction written by Chitra Fernando. This excerpt appears towards==**(Introduction/Context, title, author, set the scene where the excerpt belongs)** the end of the story, where ==Mahinda, the narrator, visits== Loku Nanda's house after spending some time in England. At the lunch table, ==Kusuma proudly speaks==**(Plot placement/situation, summarizes what is happening)** about the shrine room she is building in the temple, even asking Mahinda to contribute to what she calls a meritorious deed.

Though ==Loku Nanda is seen as a generous person== at the beginning of the story, in reality, she had treated Kusuma, the adopted servant, ==with cruelty and hypocrisy==, even denying the chance to learn to read and write. The story gradually reveals how Loku Nanda restricted Kusuma's freedom of choice. Towards the end, the reader sees how ==Kusuma has become a replica of Loku Nanda==**(1st Character analysis, shows about characters and developments)**, which is a repetition of the same behaviors, blinding following Buddhist rituals and principles without any deeper understanding of the ultimate goal of Buddhism, which is enlightenment of Nibbana. 

In this situation, it is clearly shown how Kusuma exploits Loku Nanda, taking the produce from her garden, selling antique ebony furniture, and jewelry to finance her "meritorious deeds". This brings out the ==theme of piety versus inequity==, where the outward ==appearance of devotion hides selfish motives==**( 1st Theme/message, linking to main ideas and implications)**. ==Kusuma's== dual role, constructing a shrine while simultaneously ==exploiting== Loku Nanda, reveals both her piousness and her wickedness. Her ==piety is self-centered==**(1st Critical comment, give judgment, interpretation)**, since she believes that such a deed will help her to be born as a rich woman in her next life.

(2nd theme/message, linking to main ideas and implications) Another theme highlighted through these lines is the way religion becomes twisted into a tool for personal gain. By placing Kusuma’s act of constructing a shrine room beside the chief monk’s sermon, the writer shows how people perform “meritorious deeds” not out of genuine spiritual intention, but for selfish returns. Fernando underscores how distorted religious practice becomes when merit is treated like a transaction rather than an act of faith.

(3rd theme/message combined with character analysis, deepening implications) This excerpt also exposes Kusuma’s rise in material comfort alongside her fall in moral integrity. She now has enough wealth to build an entire shrine room, an expensive undertaking, yet she funds it through exploiting and neglecting Loku Nanda. The contrast demonstrates that she has surpassed even her former mistress in hypocrisy and manipulative piety. Still, the story leaves space for a sliver of sympathy, since Kusuma’s lack of literacy and her lifelong exposure to flawed role models have shaped her into what she becomes.

(2nd Character analysis, showing developments and consequences) Through Kusuma’s authoritative voice and her subtle manipulation, the narrative presents her growing dominance in the household. At the same time, Loku Nanda’s decline becomes a kind of poetic justice — the result of her own past cruelty returning to her through the very person she oppressed. The shift in power echoes the story’s exploration of karma, where actions inevitably circle back to confront their creators.

(2nd Critical comment, giving judgment and interpretation) Viewed together, these lines reveal the story’s sharp satire on religious misunderstanding and social power. Kusuma and Loku Nanda both embody the dangers of mistaking ritual for righteousness and prestige for piety. Their dynamic exposes how moral decay hides beneath polite devotion, and how, in this world, “merit” becomes less about spiritual liberation and more about the ego’s relentless hunger for status and reward.
