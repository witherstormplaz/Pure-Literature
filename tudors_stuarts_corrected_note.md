# Tudor and Stuart England — corrected point-form study note

## 1) Big picture: what changed from Tudors to Stuarts?

- The **Tudor period** is usually remembered as a time when:
  - the monarchy became stronger,
  - England became more stable,
  - Parliament and the Crown generally worked together,
  - literature and the arts flourished.
- The **Stuart period** is remembered as a time of:
  - growing conflict between king and Parliament,
  - religious tension,
  - civil war,
  - the execution of a king,
  - the rise of Parliament’s power after the Revolution of 1688.

## 2) The Tudors

### Henry VII
- Henry VII began the Tudor dynasty after the Wars of the Roses.
- His marriage arrangement helped strengthen the Tudor claim.
- He helped bring more peace and stability after years of conflict.

### Henry VIII
- Henry VIII made the monarchy stronger.
- He broke with the Roman Catholic Church and created the Church of England under royal control.
- This increased royal authority, but it also created long-term religious tension.

### Elizabeth I
- Elizabeth I ruled during a successful and comparatively stable period.
- England gained:
  - stronger national unity,
  - greater respect in Europe,
  - cultural brilliance,
  - growth in literature and the arts.
- Her reign is often seen as a high point of Tudor England.

## 3) James I and the start of Stuart problems

### James VI of Scotland becomes James I of England
- James VI of Scotland also became James I of England.
- This united the crowns of Scotland and England under one ruler.
- However, the two kingdoms were not fully united as one state yet.

### Divine Right of Kings
- James strongly believed in the **divine right of kings**.
- This means:
  - kings receive power directly from God,
  - subjects should obey them,
  - resisting the king is morally and religiously wrong.
- This idea clashed with Parliament, which wanted a voice in government.

### Problems under James I
- James often needed money.
- Parliament did not always agree to give him what he wanted.
- He tried to raise money in ways Parliament disliked.
- He also had religious conflicts:
  - he opposed strong Presbyterian influence,
  - he wanted the Church of England to remain under royal control.
- His foreign policy was widely seen as weak and unsuccessful.
- He failed to achieve major success in the Thirty Years’ War.

## 4) Charles I and the clash with Parliament

### Charles I’s character
- Charles I was more capable than his father in some ways.
- He was serious and religious.
- He strongly believed in royal authority and the divine right of kings.
- His beliefs brought him into conflict with Parliament.

### Main causes of conflict
- The struggle between Charles I and Parliament was mainly about:
  - **who should govern England**,
  - **how much power the king should have**,
  - **what kind of church England should have**.

### Religion
- Charles supported the Church of England in a more Anglican direction.
- Many people feared he was too friendly to Catholic-like practices.
- Many Puritans wanted a more Protestant and stricter church.

### Politics
- Parliament wanted:
  - no taxes without Parliament’s consent,
  - protection from arbitrary imprisonment,
  - a greater role in government.
- Charles wanted to rule more independently.

## 5) Early conflict under Charles I

### Foreign policy failure
- Charles’s early foreign policy was a failure.
- He failed in his war efforts and lost prestige.

### Petition of Right (1628)
- Parliament forced Charles to accept the **Petition of Right**.
- It stated important limits on royal power:
  - no taxation without Parliament,
  - no imprisonment without cause,
  - no arbitrary military rule.
- This was a major victory for Parliament.

### Personal Rule / Eleven Years’ Tyranny
- In 1629, Charles dissolved Parliament and ruled without it for 11 years.
- During this time he raised money in unpopular ways.
- One important method was **Ship Money**:
  - originally a levy for naval defence,
  - later extended more broadly,
  - widely resented as unfair and illegal in spirit.

### Charles’s advisers
- Important royal supporters included:
  - **William Laud**,
  - **Thomas Wentworth, Earl of Strafford**.
- Laud supported a more ceremonial Church of England.
- Strafford supported strong royal authority.

## 6) Scotland and the road to civil war

### Religious conflict in Scotland
- Charles tried to impose religious changes on Scotland.
- He introduced a prayer book similar to the English one.
- This angered many Scots, especially Presbyterians.

### Bishops’ Wars
- The Scots resisted.
- Charles’s army was not strong enough.
- He needed money, so he was forced to recall Parliament.

### The Long Parliament (1640)
- Parliament returned in 1640 and became known as the **Long Parliament**.
- It aimed to limit royal power more firmly.
- It was not republican at first, but it opposed the king’s attempt to govern alone.
- It also reacted against Charles’s rule in church and state.

## 7) Civil War

### Why civil war broke out
- Conflict became too intense to settle peacefully.
- England divided into:
  - **Royalists / Cavaliers**: supported the king,
  - **Parliamentarians / Roundheads**: supported Parliament.

### Where support lay
- Royalists were stronger in:
  - the north and west,
  - among many nobles and traditional supporters.
- Parliamentarians were stronger in:
  - London,
  - the south and east,
  - the merchant classes,
  - many towns.

### Cromwell’s role
- Oliver Cromwell rose to prominence on the Parliament side.
- He helped create an effective cavalry.
- Major victories included:
  - **Marston Moor (1644)**,
  - **Naseby (1645)**.

### New Model Army
- Cromwell helped build the **New Model Army**.
- It was disciplined, effective, and strongly motivated.
- This army became one of Parliament’s greatest strengths.

### Scotland and the Solemn League and Covenant
- Parliament needed Scottish support.
- In return, it agreed to the **Solemn League and Covenant**.
- This meant a promise to reform the Church of England in a Presbyterian direction.

## 8) Trial and execution of Charles I

### The king becomes a prisoner
- After defeat, Charles I was captured.

### Army vs Parliament
- The army and Parliament then began to quarrel with each other.
- The army was radical and deeply religious.
- It did not fully trust Parliament.

### Pride’s Purge
- Army leader **Colonel Pride** removed many MPs who opposed the army.
- This is called **Pride’s Purge**.

### Regicide
- Charles I was put on trial.
- He was found guilty and executed in **1649**.
- This is called **regicide**: the killing of a king.

### Commonwealth
- After the execution, England became a **Commonwealth**.
- This meant there was no king.

## 9) Cromwell and the Protectorate

### Cromwell takes power
- Cromwell became the dominant figure.
- He ruled with army support.

### Problems Cromwell faced
- He had enemies in:
  - Ireland,
  - Scotland,
  - many English royalists,
  - many people who disliked military rule.
- He did not want to rule as a simple tyrant, but the situation pushed him toward strong rule.

### Ireland
- Cromwell’s campaign in Ireland was harsh and destructive.
- He took land from many Irish people and gave it to soldiers and supporters.
- This remains one of the most controversial parts of his rule.

### Scotland
- Cromwell defeated the Scots at:
  - **Dunbar (1650)**,
  - **Worcester (1651)** against Charles II.

### Lord Protector
- Cromwell became **Lord Protector**.
- He ruled with a constitutionally shaped but still highly controlled system.
- He tried to keep order and avoid another civil war.
- His rule was strong, but not fully stable.

### After Cromwell’s death
- Cromwell died in 1658.
- His son **Richard Cromwell** was unable to keep control.
- The regime quickly collapsed.

## 10) The Restoration

### Charles II returns
- The monarchy was restored in 1660.
- **Charles II** returned from exile.
- He was welcomed enthusiastically.

### Charles II’s character
- He was clever, patient, and politically cautious.
- He tried not to repeat the mistakes of his father.

### But Parliament was also back
- The Restoration did not mean the old monarchy returned unchanged.
- Parliament’s power also remained important.
- The old struggle between king and Parliament continued.

## 11) Problems under Charles II

### Court and politics
- Charles II’s court was seen as:
  - morally loose,
  - expensive,
  - politically unreliable.

### War with the Dutch
- England fought wars with the Dutch.
- Some results were humiliating for England.
- The Dutch even attacked English waters in one bold raid.

### The Great Plague and Great Fire
- London suffered:
  - the **Great Plague (1665)**,
  - the **Great Fire (1666)**.
- These disasters damaged public confidence.

### Clarendon
- Charles’s chief minister **Clarendon** was eventually impeached.
- Parliament remained suspicious of the king.

## 12) Charles II and France

### Secret Treaty of Dover (1670)
- Charles secretly agreed with **Louis XIV of France**.
- He promised to help France against the Dutch.
- In return, Louis promised support for Charles’s stronger rule.

### Religious issue
- Charles also promised to move toward Catholicism in private terms.
- This caused fear in England.

### Why this failed
- The agreement became suspicious and damaging when people learned of it.
- Parliament and public opinion grew more alarmed.

## 13) The Exclusion Crisis and the Whigs

### Fear of a Catholic succession
- People feared Charles’s brother **James**, who was Catholic, would become king.
- This led to the **Exclusion Crisis**.

### Whigs and Tories
- The political conflict helped create the parties:
  - **Whigs**: wanted to exclude James and strengthen Parliament,
  - **Tories**: supported hereditary succession and the king’s right.

### Shaftesbury
- The leading figure of the Whig movement was **Lord Shaftesbury**, not Salisbury.
- He opposed the Catholic succession.

### Charles II resists
- Charles dissolved Parliament several times.
- The exclusion movement eventually failed.

## 14) James II

### James II becomes king
- James II came to the throne in **1685**.
- At first, he seemed strong because the Whigs had been weakened.

### The real issue: religion
- James was openly Catholic.
- Many English people feared Catholic power and remembered old conflicts with Rome.

### His aims
- He wanted:
  - more freedom for Catholics,
  - stronger royal power,
  - less dependence on Parliament.

### Why people resisted him
- His policies looked like an attempt to reverse the Protestant settlement.
- He appointed Catholics to important positions.
- He also tried to influence the universities and army.

### Declaration of Indulgence
- James issued a declaration easing penalties on Catholics and dissenters.
- Many people saw this not as tolerance, but as a way to weaken Parliament and promote Catholic influence.

## 15) The Glorious Revolution of 1688

### Invitation to William
- Seven leading politicians invited **William of Orange** to intervene.
- William was married to James II’s daughter Mary.

### Collapse of James’s rule
- James lost support quickly.
- He fled to France.
- William came to England and took power with little fighting.

### Why it happened
- The main reason was fear of:
  - Catholic domination,
  - arbitrary royal power,
  - a break from Protestant government.

### What it changed
- The Revolution of 1688 made Parliament stronger.
- It ended the idea that the king ruled absolutely.

## 16) William III and Mary II

### Settlement of the throne
- William and Mary became joint rulers.
- Parliament controlled the succession.

### Key constitutional result
- No king could rule independently of Parliament.
- Taxes and standing armies needed Parliament’s approval.
- Parliament became central to government.

### Religious result
- Protestant rule was secured.
- Catholics remained excluded from many political rights.

## 17) Ireland and Scotland after 1688

### Scotland
- Scotland also experienced major changes.
- Presbyterianism gained strength there.
- But Scotland still had its own separate political structure for a time.

### Ireland
- Ireland remained a major site of conflict.
- James II was supported by many Irish Catholics.
- William defeated James at the **Battle of the Boyne (1690)**.
- This confirmed Protestant political dominance in Ireland.

## 18) Union of England and Scotland

- England and Scotland were later united politically in **1707**.
- This created **Great Britain**.
- Before that, they shared a monarch but were still separate kingdoms.

## 19) Queen Anne

### Main issue
- Anne’s reign was heavily shaped by:
  - the War of the Spanish Succession,
  - the union with Scotland,
  - the question of who would succeed her.

### Succession problem
- There was anxiety over whether the Stuarts might return.
- Since the Stuart heir was Catholic, many opposed him.
- The settlement eventually favoured the Protestant House of Hanover.

### Hanover succession
- When Anne died, **George I of Hanover** succeeded her.

## 20) Religion and culture in the seventeenth century

### Church of England
- The Church of England became more clearly organized and self-conscious.
- It increasingly claimed continuity with the older Catholic tradition while remaining Protestant.

### Other religious groups
- Several Protestant groups developed strongly:
  - **Presbyterians**,
  - **Independents / Congregationalists**,
  - **Quakers**,
  - **Baptists**.
- Important figures included:
  - **Richard Baxter**,
  - **Oliver Cromwell**,
  - **John Milton**,
  - **William Penn**,
  - **John Bunyan**.

### Literature
- This was a great literary age.
- Important names include:
  - **Shakespeare**,
  - **Milton**,
  - **Bunyan**.

### Science
- The century also produced major scientific progress.
- The most important figure was **Isaac Newton**.
- His **Principia** was published in **1687** and changed science permanently.

## 21) Colonization of America

### Why it matters
- English colonization of North America was one of the most important developments of the period.
- It helped spread English language, institutions, religion, and culture across the Atlantic.

### Virginia
- **Virginia** was established in **1607**.
- It was the first permanent English colony in North America.

### Plymouth / Mayflower
- In **1620**, the **Mayflower** brought Pilgrims to North America.
- They settled at **Plymouth**, not “Massachusetts” in the careless way the original note suggests.
- These settlers were religious separatists seeking freedom to worship.

### Massachusetts Bay
- Later Puritan settlement developed in Massachusetts.
- Boston became an important center.

### Other colonies
- **Carolina** was founded in **1663**.
- **New York** was taken from the Dutch in **1664** and renamed after the Duke of York.
- **Pennsylvania** was founded in **1681** by **William Penn**.

### Pennsylvania
- William Penn was a Quaker.
- He promoted:
  - religious freedom,
  - fair government,
  - peaceful relations with Native Americans.
- Philadelphia became its capital.

## 22) Final summary

- The Tudor era brought:
  - stronger monarchy,
  - stability,
  - cultural growth.
- The Stuart era brought:
  - conflict between monarchy and Parliament,
  - religious division,
  - civil war,
  - execution of a king,
  - restoration,
  - revolution,
  - Parliament’s lasting supremacy.
- The biggest turning point was the **Glorious Revolution of 1688**.
- After that, England moved toward a constitutional monarchy rather than absolute royal rule.
