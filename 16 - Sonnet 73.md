Analysis of the lines First Quatrain [Lines 1-4]

That time of year thou mayst in me behold When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang Upon those boughs which shake against the cold, Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang.

Time of year-one of the seasons of the year

thou-you

mayst-may

behold see - /observe/view

boughs - main branches of a tree

shake - tremble/shiver

Bare - void/empty/vacant

ruin'd - broken/damaged

In the first quatrain, the speaker tells his lover that the lover may be able to observe that time of the year' within him. What is that time of the year? It may be late autumn because yellow leaves begin hanging upon the boughs and falling down during this time of the year. Perhaps, the trees may look naked without the leaves on boughs. The boughs also begin to shiver with due to the advancing winter season. Thus, the metaphor of late autumn and advancing winter season has been employed to reveal the old age with harshness, desolation and emptiness. The yellow leaves symbolize the remaining grey hair on the speaker's head and the boughs without leaves symbolize the boldness of the speaker as the signs of his old age. The phrase "Shake against the cold' symbolizes the shivering of the other parts of the body like limbs due to their frailty being unable to withstand the advancing winter and it again implies the signs of old age and approaching death. "Choir" is a part of a church where the choristers sing. The phrase "ruin'd choir' symbolizes the vacant and voiceless boughs without once melodious singing of the birds of spring and summer. Thus the speaker looks back and compares his vitality of the young age in the past life with the void present in the old age on the verge of death.

Second Quatrain [Lines 5-8]

In me thou see'st the twilight of such day

As after sunset fadeth in the west, Which by and by black night doth take away, Death's second self, that seals up all in rest.

see'st - see

twilight-dusk/evening sunset

such day - a day of autumn

fadeth - fade / gradually grow faint and disappear

doth - does

Death's second's self - sleep

In the second quatrain, the speaker tells his lover that the lover can observe the twilight of a day of late autumn in his life after the sunset.. "The twilight' is a metaphor employed for the speaker's evening time of life which is the old age. This is the time that the day dies. The day time is very bright and attractive and it symbolizes the speaker's prime youth. The sunset is taken over by the black night and brings Death's second self which is sleep. Then sleep is sealed up just like in a coffin for rest which means death. Thus, now it is the sunset on the speaker's life and his bright youth disappears letting black night; which is death to take away.

Third Quatrain [Lines 9-12]

In me thou see'st the glowing of such fire That on the ashes of his youth doth lie, As the death-bed whereon it must expire, Consum'd with that which it was nourish'd by.

Expire - come to the end

Consum'd - eaten

Nourished - fed

In the third quatrain the speaker tells his lover to observe the glowing fire in his life. The speaker's life time is likened to fire. Fire eats away at wood until there is none left and it also dies in making it ashes. Wood is the fuel that provides energy for fire. Similarly, youth is the fuel which provides energy for life. When there is less and less wood for burning, even fire becomes less and less gradually and leads to its own extinguishment in the ashes it creates. So these ashes can be interpreted as the ashes of his youth The last glowing fire is the climax of aging and ashes are beneath the fire just as youth has passed to develop the adulthood. The deathbed of a person is the cold embers of the fire which at one stage was solid wood that nourished the fire flames. Thus, when youth and strength dwindles gradually, life span also becomes less and less and leads to death. It is ironic that life creates its own deathbed eventually.

When analyzing this quatrain deeply, it is evident that the speaker is expressing not only about his impending physical death, but the death of his youth and subsequently his youthful desires - those very things which sustained his relationship with the young man whom he loves.

Couplet [Lines 13-14]

This thou perceiv'st, which makes thy love more strong.

To love that well which thou must leave ere long.

perceiv'st- understand

ere long before long / soon

It is quite obvious that the entire poem is written to someone, probably a lover or a loved one. The poet seems to make a link between the three quatrains and the final couplet juxtaposing death and love.

In the first line, "This" refers to the speaker's aging and approaching death as described in the three quatrains of the sonnet. In the same line, the word 'which' refers to the lover's knowledge that the speaker is old and approaching death. The concluding line is more significant in the sonnet because it intensifies the idea brought out in the penultimate line regarding the strength of love 'love more strong".

Accordingly, the knowledge that the two will eventually be parted by the speaker's death makes the lover's love for the speaker much more stronger. In that sense, the couplet conveys the overall point that the imminence of growing old and dying as introduced in the main body of the poem affects the quality of love. This kind of love exists even beyond the death of the speaker so that it is eternal and spiritual.

Sonnet 73 is written in typical Shakespearean or English sonnet form. It consists of three quatrains and one couplet at the end, altogether 14 lines written in iambic pentameter with a regular rhyme scheme.

Development of the structure

Each quatrain describes the same issue, the speaker's aging and approaching death in familiar and but different terms. The multiple expressions of the same view offer an opportunity for the reader to get a more obvious and complete understanding of the issue.

1st Quatrain

The speaker talks about fall when trees of late autumn get prepared for winter by shedding of their leaves and there is no more sweet singing of birds. They have migrated to warmer climates. The speaker is referring to himself that he has reached the finality of aging. He is no longer in the spring time of his youth, but is nearing winter, death.

2nd Quatrain

The speaker talks about the end of the day. "The sunset' is taken over by the dark night, and death's second self [sleep]. The speaker is at the twilight of his life and death will soon take him.

3rd Quatrain

The speaker talks about the extinguishing of fire by comparing to his life. The fire is consumed by what it was first nourished; the fuel wood [youth]. After extinguishing of fire, what remains beneath is cold embers just like the speaker's cold dead body.

Couplet

The turn or the volta in this sonnet comes in the final couplet. The poet is associated with the strength of his lover's love vs. his aging and approaching his death. Having perceived the speaker's finality of life, the lover makes his love much more stronger at the hour of need before parting.

Use of metaphors, Conceits and symbols to highlight the themes

Poetry is a popular common medium for people to express love. It is obvious that sonnets have been composed to express love since its origin in Italy.. William Shakespeare's Sonnet 73 is no exception in this regard. Accordingly, passions of love along with death are expressed through the use of figurative language. The poet has employed metaphors as a poetic device in all the three quatrains to convince the reader about the speaker's aging and approaching death. The extended metaphors also contribute to build up powerful imagery as well as conceits to bring more sense and sensibility over the issues highlighted. 

In the first quatrain, "time of year," is the metaphor of late autumn, which emphasizes the harshness and emptiness of old age. The poet extends this metaphor in the lines. 

"when yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang upon the boughs which shake against the cold" (lines 2-3).

Those metaphors clearly indicate that winter, which usually symbolizes loneliness and desolation, is coming. The leaves that are falling off the branches symbolize the old man's loss of hair, and "the boughs shaking against the cold" symbolize the frailty of his physical organs as very obvious signs of old age and imminent death. The speaker also uses another metaphor in the season of autumn.

"bare ruined choirs where late the sweet birds sang" to express a feeling of old age. The speaker says that autumn is a dull season without the melodious songs of the sweet birds of spring. The poet thereby, compares it to his person's old life without joy and vitality that he had during his spring youth.

In the second quatrain, the metaphor shifts, when the speaker says that his age is like late twilight,

"In me thou seest the twilight of such day

As after sunset fadeth in the west"

This is the time in which the day dies, the same way, the sun is setting on the speaker's life.

Here sunset represents dying. The next metaphor compares black night which occurs after sunset, to death.

"Which by and by black night doth take away

Death's second self that seals up all in rest"

It is important to note that the poet has changed his focus from aging to death, and narrowed his scope to the close of one day.

In the final quatrain the poet speaks of a deathbed of ashes.

These ashes can be interpreted as the ashes of his youth. Those ashes had once been the fuel of the man's youth that provided his youthful energy. But now, they are at the place where the dying fire of his youth and strength dwindles to nothingness. This fire image can be considered a conceit because the metaphor is extended and elaborated to series of events such as youth, life and finality.

This poem is not simply a procession of interchangeable metaphors; it is the story of the speaker slowly realizing the finality of his life and his impermanence in time. It is the final couplet of the Sonnet that the strength of love is highly stressed. Accordingly, if one loves during the life time, such love can live forever in spite of short span of life.

Variations and contrasts

There is a variation and a contrast of the tone between the three quatrains and the couplet.

In the quatrains tone is sad and tragic but it is happy in the couplet.

In the quatrains, mood is gloomy but it is lovable in the couplet

In the quatrains, the speaker has a negative and pessimistic attitude towards life, but it is positive and optimistic in the couplet.

In the quatrains, theme is death, but love is the theme in the couplet.

Paradox / irony

"Consumed with that which it was nourish'd by."

According to this line the poet conveys that fire is fed by the fuel wood but the same fire kills wood and make it ashes and nothing lives at the end. In the same way it is ironic and paradoxical that life is given all the energy it needs by the period of one's youth hood, but with the passage of time life consumes youth and transforms it to old age and kills on the deathbed in the end.
