
## **Song: Go and catch a falling star By John Donne [1572-1631/Elizabethan and Jacobean Period.]**


### **Note on John Donne**

  

Donne was born on January 22, 1572 in London, into a recusant Roman Catholic family when practice of that religion was illegal in England. Donne was the third of six children. His father, also named John Donne, was of Welsh descent and a warden of the Ironmongers Company in the City of London. Donne's father was a respected Roman Catholic who avoided unwelcome government attention out of fear of persecution.

  

His father died in 1576, when Donne was four years old, leaving his son fatherless and his widow. Elizabeth Heywood, with the responsibility of raising their children alone. Heywood was also from a recusant Roman Catholic family, the daughter of John Heywood, the playwright, and sister of the Reverend Jasper Heywood, a Jesuit priest and translator. She was a great-niece of the Roman Catholic martyr Thomas More. This tradition of martyrdom would continue among Donne's closer relatives, many of whom were executed or exiled for religious reasons. Donne was educated privately, however, there is no evidence to support the popular claim that he was taught by Jesuits. Donne's mother married Dr. John Syminges, a wealthy widower with three children, a few months after Donne's father died. Donne thus acquired a stepfather. Two more of his sisters, Mary and Katherine, died in 1581. Donne's mother lived her last years in the Deanery after Donne became Dean of St Paul's, and died just two months before Donne, in January 1631. In 1583, the 11-year-old Donne began studies at Hart Hall, now Hertford College, Oxford. After three years of studies there, Donne was admitted to the University of Cambridge, where he studied for another three years. However, Donne could not obtain a degree from either institution because of his Catholicism, since he refused to take the Oath of Supremacy required to graduate. [12]

  

In 1591 Donne was accepted as a student at the Thavies Inn legal school, one of the Inns of Chancery in London. On 6 May 1592 he was admitted to Lincoln's Inn, one of the Inns of Court. [2] In 1593, five years after the defeat of the Spanish Armada and during the intermittent Anglo-Spanish War (1585-1604), Queen Elizabeth issued the first English statute against sectarian dissent from the Church of England, titled "An Act for restraining Popish recusants". It defined "Popish recusants" as those "convicted for not repairing to some Church, Chapel, or usual place of Common Prayer to hear Divine Service there, but forbearing the same contrary to the tenor of the laws and statutes heretofore made and provided in that behalf". Donne's brother Henry was also a university student prior to his arrest in 1593 for harbouring a Catholic priest, William Harrington, whom he betrayed under torture. Harrington was tortured on the rack, hanged until not quite dead, and then subjected to disembowelment. Henry Donne died in Newgate Prison of bubonic plague, leading Donne to begin questioning his Catholic faith.

  

During and after his education, Donne spent much of his considerable inheritance on women, literature, pastimes and travel. Although no record details precisely where Donne travelled, he did cross Europe and later fought with the Earl of Essex and Sir Walter Raleigh against the Spanish at Cadiz (1596) and the Azores (1597), and witnessed the loss of the Spanish flagship, the San Felipe. According to Izaak Walton, who wrote a biography of Donne in 1658:

  

By the age of 25 he was well prepared for the diplomatic career he appeared to be seeking. He was appointed chief secretary to the Lord Keeper of the Great Seal, Sir Thomas Egerton, and was established at Egerton's London home, York House, Strand close to the Palace of Whitehall, then the most influential social centre in England.

  

During the next four years Donne fell in love with Egerton's niece Anne More, and they were secretly married just before Christmas in 1601, against the wishes of both Egerton and George More, who was Lieutenant of the Tower and Anne's father. Upon discovery, this wedding ruined Donne's career, getting him fired and put in Fleet Prison, along with minister Samuel Brooke, who married them ve tine hian who acted as a witness to the wedding. Donne was released shortly thereafter when the mariage was proven valid, and he soon secured the release of the other two. Walton tells us that when Donne wrote to his wife to tell her about losing his post, he wrote after his name: John Donne, Anne Donne, Un-done. It was not until 1609 that Donne was reconciled with his father-in-law and received his wife's dowry.

  

After his release, Donne had to accept a retired country life in a small house in Pyrford, Surrey, owned by Anne's cousin, Sir Francis Wooley, where they resided until the end of 1604 In spring 1605 they moved to another small house in Mitcham, London, where he scraped a meager living as a lawyer, while Anne Donne bore a new baby almost every year. Though he also worked as an assistant pamphleteer to Thomas Morton writing anti-Catholic pamphlets, Donne was in a constant state of financial insecurity.

  

Anne bore John 12 children in 16 years of marriage, (including two stillbirths their eighth and then, in 1617, their last child); indeed, she spent most of her married life either pregnant or nursing. The 10 surviving children were Constance, John, George, Francis, Lucy (named after Donne's patron Lucy, Countess of Bedford, her godmother), Bridget, Mary, Nicholas, Margaret, and Elizabeth Three (Francis, Nicholas, and Mary) died before they were ten. In a state of despair that almost drove him to kill himself, Donne noted that the death of a child would mean one mouth fewer to feed, but he could not afford the burial expenses. During this time, Donne wrote but did not publish Biathanatos, his defense of suicide. His wife died on 15 August 1617, five days after giving birth to their twelfth child, a still-born baby. Donne mourned her deeply, and wrote of his love and loss in his 17th Holy Sonnet.

  

In 1602 John Donne was elected as Member of Parliament for the constituency of Brackley, but this was not a paid position. [2] Queen Elizabeth I died in 1603, being succeeded by King James I of Scotland. The fashion for coterie poetry of the period gave Donne a means to seek patronage, and many of his poems were written for wealthy friends or patrons, especially MP Sir Robert Drury of Hawsted (1575-1615), whom he met in 1610 and became Donne's chief patron, furnishing him and his family an apartment in his large house in Drury Lane.

  

In 1610 and 1611 Donne wrote two anti-Catholic polemics: Pseudo-Martyr and Ignatius His Conclave for Morton. He then wrote two Anniversaries, An Anatomy of the World (1611) and Of the Progress of the Soul[19] (1612) for Drury. Although James was pleased with Donne's work, he refused to reinstate him at court and instead urged him to take holy orders. [8] At length, Donne acceded to the king's wishes, and in 1615 was ordained into the Church of England. [13]

  

In 1615 Donne was awarded an honorary doctorate in divinity from Cambridge University, and became a Royal Chaplain in the same year, and a Reader of Divinity at Lincoln's Inn in 1616, where he served in the chapel as minister until 1622. In 1618 he became chaplain to Viscount Doncaster, who was on an embassy to the princes of Germany. Donne did not return to England until 1620. [16] In 1621 Donne was made Dean of St Paul's, a leading and well-paid position in the Church of England, which he held until his death in 1631. During his period as dean his daughter Lucy died, aged eighteen. In late November and early December 1623 he suffered a nearly fatal illness, thought to be either typhus or a combination of a cold followed by a period of fever. During his convalescence he wrote a series of meditations and prayers on health, pain, and sickness that were published as a book in 1624 under the title of Devotions upon Emergent Occasions. One of these meditations, Meditation XVII, later became well known for its phrases "No man is an Iland" (often modernised as "No man is an island") and "...for whom the bell tolls". In 1624 he became vicar of St Dunstan-in-the-West, and 1625 a prolocutor to Charles 1.[2] He earned a reputation as an eloquent preacher and 160 of his sermons have survived, including the famous Death's Duel sermon delivered at the Palace of Whitehall before King Charles I in February 1631.

  

His works are noted for their strong, sensual style and include sonnets, love poems, religious poems, Latin translations, epigrams, elegies, songs, satires and sermons. His poetry is noted for its vibrancy of language and inventiveness of metaphor, especially compared to that of his contemporaries. Donne's style is characterised by abrupt openings and various paradoxes, ironies and dislocations. These features, along with his frequent dramatic or everyday speech rhythms, his tense syntax and his tough eloquence, were both a reaction against the smoothness of conventional Elizabethan poetry and an adaptation into English of European baroque and mannerist techniques. His early career was marked by poetry that bore immense knowledge of English society and he met that knowledge with sharp criticism. Another important theme in Donne's poetry is the idea of true religion, something that he spent much time considering and about which he often theorized. He wrote secular poems as well as erotic and love poems. He is particularly famous for his mastery of metaphysical conceits.

  

Donne's earliest poems showed a developed knowledge of English society coupled with sharp criticism of its problems. His satires dealt with common Elizabethan topics, such as corruption in the legal system, mediocre poets, and pompous courtiers. His images of sickness, vomit, manure, and plague reflected his strongly satiric view of a world populated by all the fools and knaves of England. His third satire, however, deals with the problem of true religion, a matter of great importance to Donne. He argued that it was better to examine carefully one's religious convictions than blindly to follow any established tradition, for none would be saved at the Final Judgment, by claiming "A Harry, or a Martin taught [them] this."

  

Donne's early career was also notable for his erotic poetry, especially his elegies, in which he employed unconventional metaphors, such as a flea biting two lovers being compared to sex. In "Elegy XIX: To His Mistris Going to Bed" he poetically undressed his mistress and compared the act of fondling to the exploration of America. In "Elegy XVIII" he compared the gap between his lover's breasts to the Hellespont. Donne did not publish these poems, although he did allow them to circulate widely in manuscript form.

  

Some have speculated that Donne's numerous illnesses, financial strain, and the deaths of his friends all contributed to the development of a more somber and pious tone in his later poems. [13] The change can be clearly seen in "An Anatomy of the World" (1611), a poem that Donne wrote in memory of Elizabeth Drury, daughter of his patron, Sir Robert Drury of Hawstead, Suffolk. This poem treats Elizabeth's demise with extreme gloominess, using it as a symbol for the Fall of Man and the destruction of the universe.[13]

  

The poem "A Nocturnal upon S. Lucy's Day, Being the Shortest Day", concerns the poet's despair at the death of a loved one. In it Donne expresses a feeling of utter negation and hopelessness, saying that "I am every dead thing... re-begot/Of absence, darkness, death." This famous work was probably written in 1627 when both Donne's friend Lucy, Countess of Bedford, and his daughter Lucy Donne died. Three years later, in 1630, Donne wrote his will on Saint Lucy's Day (13 December), the date the poem describes as "Both the year's, and the day's deep midnight".

  

The increasing gloominess of Donne's tone may also be observed in the religious works that he began writing during the same period. His early belief in the value of scepticism now gave way to a firm faith in the traditional teachings of the Bible. Having converted to the Anglican Church, Donne focused his literary career on religious literature. He quickly became noted for his sermons and religious poems. The lines of these sermons and devotional works would come to influence future works of English literature, such as Ernest Hemingway's For Whom the Bell Tolls, which took its title from a passage in Meditation XVII of Devotions upon Emergent Occasions, and Thomas Merton's No Man is an Island, which took its title from the same source.

  

Towards the end of his life Donne wrote works that challenged death, and the fear that it inspired in many men, on the grounds of his belief that those who die are sent to Heaven to live eternally. One example of this challenge is his Holy Sonnet X, "Death Be Not Proud", from which come the famous lines "Death, be not proud, though some have called thee / Mighty and dreadful, for thou art not so." Even as he lay dying during Lent in 1631, he rose from his sickbed and delivered the Death's Duel sermon, which was later described as his own funeral sermon. Death's Duel portrays life as a steady descent to suffering and death, yet sees hope in salvation and immortality through an embrace of God, Christ and the Resurrection.

  

His work has received much criticism over the years, especially concerning his metaphysical form. Donne is generally considered the most prominent member of the metaphysical poets, a phrase coined in 1781 by Samuel Johnson, following a comment on Donne by John Dryden. Dryden had written of Donne in 1693: "He affects the metaphysics, not only in his satires, but in his amorous verses, where nature only should reign, and perplexes the minds of the fair sex with nice speculations of philosophy, when he should engage their hearts, and entertain them with the softnesses of love." In Life of Cowley (from Samuel Johnson's 1781 work of biography and criticism Lives of the Most Eminent English Poets), Johnson refers to the beginning of the seventeenth century in which there "appeared a race of writers that may be termed the metaphysical poets". Donne's immediate successors in poetry therefore tended to regard his works with ambivalence, with the Neoclassical poets regarding his conceits as abuse of the metaphor. However he was revived by Romantic poets such as Coleridge and Browning, though his more recent revival in the early twentieth century by poets such as T. S. Eliot and critics like FR Leavis tended to portray him, with approval, as an anti-Romantic. [26]

  

Donne is considered a master of the metaphysical conceit, an extended metaphor that combines two vastly different ideas into a single idea, often using imagery. An example of this is his equation of lovers with saints in "The Canonization". Unlike the conceits found in other Elizabethan poetry, most notably Petrarchan conceits, which formed clichéd comparisons between more closely related objects (such as a rose and love), metaphysical conceits go to a greater depth in comparing two completely unlike objects. One of the most famous of Donne's conceits is found in "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning" where he compares two lovers who are separated like the two legs of a compass.

  

Donne's works are also witty, employing paradoxes, puns, and subtle yet remarkable analogies. His pieces are often ironic and cynical, especially regarding love and human motives. Common subjects of Donne's poems are love (especially in his early life), death (especially after his wife's death), and religion.[18]

  

John Donne's poetry represented a shift from classical forms to more personal poetry. Donne s noted for his poetic metre, which was structured with changing and jagged rhythms that closely resemble casual speech (it was for this that the more classical-minded Ben Jonson commented that "Donne, for not keeping of accent, deserved hanging"). [18]

  

Some scholars believe that Donne's literary works reflect the changing trends of his life, with love poetry and satires from his youth and religious sermons during his later years. Other scholars, such as Helen Gardner, question the validity of this dating most of his poems were published posthumously (1633). The exception to these is his Anniversaries, which were published in 1612 and Devotions upon Emergent Occasions published in 1624. His sermons are also dated, sometimes specifically by date and year.

  

Donne died on 31 March 1631 and was buried in old St Paul's Cathedral, where a memorial statue of him by Nicholas Stone was erected with a Latin epigraph probably composed by himself. The memorial was one of the few to survive the Great Fire of London in 1666 and is now in St Paul's Cathedral. The statue was claimed by Izaac Walton in his biography to have been modelled from the life by Donne in order to suggest his appearance at the resurrection; it was to start a vogue in such monuments during the course of the 17th century. In 2012 a bust of the poet by Nigel Boonham was unveiled outside in the cathedral churchyard. 

  

## **Metaphysical Poetry**

  

As far as the term 'Metaphysics' is concerned, 'Meta' means 'beyond' and 'physics' 'physical nature Metaphysical poetry means poetry that goes beyond physical world of the senses and explores the spiritual world. Metaphysical poetry began early in the Jacobean age in the last age of Shakespeare. John Donne was the pioneer and founder of the metaphysical school of poetry. Dryden used the this term first and said that Donne 'affects the metaphysics". The other poets who followed John Donne was Abraham Cowley, Henry Vaughan, Richard Crashaw, Andrew Maerwel, George Herbert, Robert Herrick etc.

  

### **Characteristics of Metaphysical Poetry**

  

- Dramatic manner and direct tone of speech

- Style-concise, succinct, epigrammatic

- A fusion of passionate feelings and logical arguments

- A blend of passion and thought

- Use of conceits [A conceit is a kind of metaphor that compares two very unlike things in a surprising, clever and dramatic way. Often, conceits are extended metaphors that dominate an entire passage or poem.]

- Wit [A form of intelligent humour]

- Mixture of sensual and spiritual experience.

- Usage of satire and irony.

- Imagery related to contemporary science, mathematics, geography, astronomy, mythology. legendry etc.

- Use of colloquial speech.

- Affectation and hyperbolic expressions.

- Lyrical and musical

  

### **Meanings of Words**

  

- falling star - meteor or shooting star

- mandrake root a narcotic, short-stemmed European plant, Mandragora officinarum, ofthe nightshade family, having a fleshy, often forked root somewhatresembling a human form.

- cleft - split, divided, or partially divided into two.

- mermaid - a mythical sea creature with the head and trunk of a woman and the tail of a fish, conventionally depicted as beautiful and with long flowing golden hair.

- envy - jealousy

- stinging - pricking

- befell - happened / occurred

- fair-beautiful

- wonders-great surprises

- swear-affirm

- ere before [in time]

  

## **Analysis of stanzas**

  

### **Stanza -1**

  

Go and catch a falling star,

Get with child a mandrake root,

Tell me where all past years are,

Or who cleft the devil's foot,

Teach me to hear mermaids singing,

Or to keep off envy's stinging,

And find

What wind

Serves to advance an honest mind.

  

In the first stanza, the poet addresses an imaginary listener. Accordingly, he asks the listener to perform a list of impossible tasks. The first task is to go and catch a falling star. Here the falling star is most probably a meteor or a shooting star. It is an impossible task because a falling star becomes a destructive object like a heated fiery ball due to the friction with the earth's atmosphere. On the other hand it is impossible to decide the time of falling. Therefore, it is absurd to wait for catching a falling star.

  

The other task is related to a mandrake root. Mandrake is is a type of plant from which drugs may be made, especially for causing sleep. It is also considered a poisonous plant as well as a plant with magical power. The root of mandrake splitting in two parts was often thought to resemble a man's lower part with two legs. Sometimes in some cases it is believed that it also has head and hands. Consequently a mandrake root represents male human form that could impregnate a woman. So "Get with child" means to make a woman pregnant. This idea is related to the false belief that a barren woman could become fertile by eating the root of mandrake plant. Therefore, the wish for female to be pregnant on a mandrake root can only be heard in fairy tales and mythology. 

  

In the third line the poet orders his listener to tell him where the past years are. It is really an absurd command because past remains only in our memory as something abstract.

  

In the fourth line, the poet asks the listener to find out who cleft the devil's foot. Devil is often depicted as an evil being that has toes joined by a tissue like hooves of the ox and the horse. When it is hard to know about the facts of true living beings, how can one find out the facts about the creation of the physical features of the invisible mythical creatures.

  

In the fifth line, the poet asks his listener to teach him how to listen to mermaid's singing. Mermaids are mythical sea creatures with the head and trunk of a woman and the tail of a fish, conventionally depicted with beautiful long flowing golden hair.

  

According to Greek mythology, mermaids live in the sea and they enchant the sailors by sitting on the rocks and singing for them. When they reach the rocks to listen to the songs, the ships get crashed on the rocks and the mermaids disappear. Thus the sailors face death. Therefore, it is impossible to teach the speaker how to listen to mermaid's songs.

  

In a nutshell. envy is a feeling of discontent or covetousness with regard to another person's advantages, success, possessions, etc. Envy is a sin listed in Ten Commandments 'You shall not covet your neighbour's wife or his male servant or his female servant or his ox or his donkey or anything that belongs to your neighbor" Even the Saint Clement of Alexandria in his writing "One Who Knows God' states that envy is inflamed by the passions that attack it like stinging insects. Therefore, what Donne says as 'envy's stinging' is associated with religion. However, it is impossible to prevent from stinging from envy.

  

At the end of the stanza, there is another impossible task given to the poet's listener. The poet asks the listener to teach him how to find a wind that helps an honest man improve in his social life. The underlined idea is that people who never lie and cheat cannot improve and progress in life like unscrupulous men.

  

Thus, there are a number of imperatives [with verbs like go, catch, find, and teach] put forward before the listener to be performed as quite impossible challenges. Why are there such impossible imperatives beyond the bounds of possibility and the capability of the ordinary humans? The answer can be found out when reading the next stanza.

  

### **Stanza - 2**

  

If thou be'st born to strange sights, 

Things invisible to see, 

Ride ten thousand days and nights, 

Till age snow white hairs on thee, 

Thou, when thou return'st, wilt tell me, 

All strange wonders that befell thee, 

And swear,

No where

Lives a woman true, and fair.

  

The poet who has already given some challenges to his listener in the first stanza is shifting towards something imaginative in the second stanza. Accordingly, the poet considers the listener as a person who was born to strange sights. What do the 'strange sights' mean? This phrase is used for a very specific group of people with a far more different intellectual capacity than the other normal people. Consequently, the things which are commonly invisible in the world to the normal human beings would be visible to the listener because he was born with such strange sights like a psychic. The poet thereby, asks the listener to travel a long period of time like ten thousand days and nights to explore the unfamiliar lands far and wide in the world till he gets snow white colour hair due to aging. When the listener comes back, he will tell the poet about the great wonders in the world. But there is one thing that he will not be able to find. That is the greatest wonder of the world. What is it? It is finding out a woman who is true to her husband and beautiful as well. Thus this stanza ends up in a pessimistic and cynical tone and mood that it is impossible to seek a woman with both beauty and truthfulness.

  

### **Stanza - 3**

  

If thou find'st one, let me know, 

Such a pilgrimage were sweet; 

Yet do not, I would not go, 

Though at next door we might meet;

Though she were true, when you met her,

And last, till you write your letter,

Yet she

Will be

False, ere I come, to two, or three.

  

In the third stanza, Donne further continues his argument based on the hypothesis even though there is a true and fair woman, she will be true only for a very short time and she will be false with two or three men. Let's take a look at the stanza how the argument is built up and put forward.

  

The poet asks the listener to let him know if he finds a true and beautiful woman during his long discovery of the world far and wide. At this moment, the poet seems to have a ray of hope about the existence of a true and fair woman. If the traveler found one, he would visit her and consider the journey as a sweet pilgrimage. What is a pilgrimage? It is a journey to a sacred place. The poet thereby, regards that such a fair and beautiful woman is holy like an angel or a goddess. Nevertheless, suddenly, the poet back paddles insisting that he would not go to see her even though she was found at the next door. Why does Donne change his mind at once? That is because of his deep rooted attitude towards women in his memory. The poet says that the woman would have appeared to be true when the listener encountered her. But the poet is quite sure that her honesty will last only until the listener puts the pen to paper to write the letter to the poet to come and see her. She will thereby, seduce two or three men before the poet manages to go there.

  

## **Themes of the poem**

  

The poem 'Goe and catch a Falling Starre' falls within the category of 'love poems' Some critics hold the view that it was posthumously published in 1633 in the volume entitled 'Songs and Sonnets'. Donne wrote it during his youth perhaps at the age of twenty. Woman's inconsistency or infidelity would have been a popular subject among the young poets who met in the literary circles in London life. Even his contemporaries like William Shakespeare expressed his cynical attitude towards women in his sonnets. For example, his sonnet 138 exposes the deceitfulness and dishonesty of women.

  

"When my love swears that she is made of truth

I do believe her, though I know she lies"

  

Donne could enliven the subject with his personal experience with metaphysical flavour. His joyful life in London and his close company with different young women justified his attitude about woman's faithlessness and fickleness.

  

According to the Petrarchan courtly love poetry, woman was highly idealized and idolized as a heroine and a goddess, worthy of love and admiration. But the metaphysical poets poked fun at woman's fashions, frailties and faithlessness. That was why Shakespeare's maxim 'Frailty thy name is woman' became quite popular during Donne's period

  

In the poem "Goe and catch a Falling Starre 'Donne quite vividly highlights the fact that it is impossible to find a loyal and chaste woman. The poet, through irony and exaggeration suggests the impossibility of the undertaking to discover a true and fair woman. He builds up the argument and proves his conviction.

  

"And swear

Nowhere

Lives a woman true and fair'

  

These lines further confirm that there is a strong possibility for fair women to have many lovers because men are naturally enticed by their physical beauty. Therefore, we cannot expect such beautiful women to be honest, chaste and faithful when there is a warm atmosphere of love and romance all around. On the contrary, an ugly woman or an uninviting woman can remain truthful and honest throughout her life because she does not have a big demand from men according to the argument. Nobody will flirt with them and compete to take undue amorous advantages from them. They can preserve their chastity and morality without any trouble. In that sense, Donne seems to suggest that fair women are insincere whereas ugly women are sincere. At the same time, he concludes if there were at least one true and fair woman, she would be holy. Nevertheless, they cannot achieve holiness, as they never become true and faithful.

  

We wonder whether Donne experienced the rejection of his proposal to a beautiful girl at his young age or had melancholic experience owing to his broken love affair for having this kind of bitter cynicism towards women. If it is so, Donne tries to generalize the fact that all fair women are false and faithless because of the fault and betrayal of one girl.

  

When analyzing the poem, we feel like to pay our attention to another aspect of the poem. It is male chauvinism and prejudice brought out via the poet's voice. Though he is highly critical and satirical about women who are beautiful, he is very meticulous not to make any comment on handsome men whether they are true or not. On the other hand, the poet seems to be hypocritical because he deliberately keeps quiet of the men who destroy the chastity of fair women. Men themselves give fair women value, worth and fame to outshine in the society and get the demand from the opposite gender. But when they become victims of men, same men devalue and defame them in a callous and hypocritical manner exposing their prejudice and selfishness.

  

"Yet she

Will be

False, ere I come, to two and three"

  

Again it is realistic that immature and inexperienced young men develop a cynical and pessimistic attitude towards such fair women. But when they gain maturity, they change their attitude and talk about spiritual love, reciprocated love, consummated love etc. Even John Donne who categorically denied the existence of true love and inconsistency of women. tends to believe in perfect love through his own life. As far as Donne's personal life is concerned, it is evident that he experienced and enjoyed true love with his beautiful wife Anne More in spite of the obstacles from Anne's family. They also had eleven children. Although Anne died in 1617, Donne continued his life with the memory of his beloved dead wife. He also decided to remain as a widower till he died.

  

Moreover, Donne's poem The Good Morrow witnesses his maturity gained in both his life and poetry.

  

"If our two loves be one. Or thou and I

Love so alike, that none can slacken, none can die"

  

Thus, there is a contrast between his early poems and later poems. The cynical attitude to the fair sex in the early poems of Donne, is in contrast with the rational attitude to love and sex to be found in his later poems.

### **Dramatic Monologue**

  

The persona in the poem speaks to an imaginary listener. We feel that the listener is before the persona. So the poem is a fine dramatic monologue. It makes the poem more live and interesting.

  

### **Meter / Rhythm**

  

The meter of the poem is close to tetrameter, using mostly iambs (an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed syllable). In addition to iambic iambic tetrameter, the poet employs some other metrical feet as well. In the first stanza we can notice how the imperative verbs go, catch, get are stressed neglecting iambic feet. The following line is one of the example and its stressed syllables are bold:

  

"**Go** and **catch** a **fall**ing **star**..."

  

The rhythm of the poem is similar to the rhythm of speaking. It changes according to the needs of the device that highlights the tension rather than irregularity. It also indicates the stress that one would use in a dramatic reading. The poet constantly disconnects the accepted rhythms unexpectedly to argument Changing the tetrameter in lines seven and eight (with two syllables each) is a dramatic give us pleasant surprises.

  

### **Conceits**

  

"Go and Catch a Falling Star" is a metaphysical conceit of the fair and virtuous women in the world. Donne uses the fantastic and impossible examples of catching falling stars, pregnancies with mandrake roots and hearing mermaid's singing to emphasize the fact how hard it is to find a fair woman who will be faithful and loval to a husband. Donne points out in the second and the last stanza how if his listener were to search the world for a thousand days and nights seeing many strange and wonderful things, he would still not find a single faithful woman. The poet even goes so far as to mention in the last stanza that if he were to know where that perfect woman was, even if she was next door as a neighbor, she would already be false with several men before he even managed to walk the few steps to reach her.

  

Thus the entire poem is presented as a metaphysical conceit.

  

### **Terseness**

  

Terseness is another characteristic of metaphysical poets. Terseness means using words economically to convey something deep. In this poem the poet says

  

**"No where Lives a woman true and fair"**

  

In this compact idea Donne intends to show that just as it is impossible to catch a falling star in the sky, a woman with both honesty and fairness is rare to find out as they first seem to be honest

  

but later they are found to be dishonest.

  

### **Metaphors / Symbols / Imagery**

  

The poet employs several metaphors, symbols and imagery related to astronomy, mythology, legendry etc Stars are used in the conventional love poems to compare women's beauty. But in this poem it is a falling star which suggests the chastity of a fair woman is in a state of deterioration. She is falling from grace similar to devil who was a fallen angel. So beautiful women are just like fallen angels.

  

Past years are referred to the fair woman's virtuous qualities like chastity, innocence, faithfulness. etc. The poet asks the listener to tell him where the past years are. It is just like asking about the woman's good moral qualities. But it is utterly impossible to find and bring them back.

  

Women are like mermaids. They are treacherous and deceptive. They lure men with their melodious singing and outward beauty. They are not what they seemed. So the poet asks to teach him how to avoid misleading, enchantment of beautiful women.

  

Man's envy is compared to stinging of an insect like scorpion. Here, the envy that the poet speaks lover. The poet conveys that it is impossible to avoid such sexual jealousy and it is the most harmful of is a kind of jealousy developed in a person's mind when he has lust for another man's wife or a and painful like the stinging of a scorpion.

  

The word 'pilgrimage is used as a metaphor. A woman who is true and fair is like a deity. Her residence is a holy shrine and the journey to visit her is compared to a pilgrimage. Thus the poet creates a holy image in the line" Such a pilgrimage were sweet"

  

Snow white hair symbolizes man's old age. It also implies the long period that the listener takes for his discovery of the strange lands. Moreover, it implies how much it is hard to search for a true and fair woman.

  

### **Hypothesis**

  

In the third stanza, the poet puts forward his argument on the hypothesis even though there is such a true and fair woman, she will be true only for a very short time and soon she will be false losing her chastity.

  

### **Language**

  

When considering the language used by the poet, there are several metaphysical features. One of them is the logical and argumentative quality of the language. In the first stanza, the poet puts forward some challenges to the listener in an argumentative, rational and imperative form. The use of imperative verbs like Go, Catch, Get, Tell, Teach boosts the logical effect. All the impossible and impractical tasks given to the listener help the poet prove his main argumentative point that it is impossible to find out a true and fair woman.

  

Moreover, the phrases, clauses and the questions in the lines with words like **If, or, and, no, what, though, yet etc. add logical tone and mood to the poem.**

  

The poet uses colloquial language from the beginning to the end of the poem for imperatives, questions, statements, imaginations and so on.

  

There are several archaic English words in the poem such as thou, be'st, thee, ere, wilt etc. In the original poem, we get the extra letter 'e' for the words as follows:

  

Goe/catche / starre/finde /roote/yeares/heare/mermaids/ winde/minde / borne/haires/sweare/doore/doe/faire/mee/ shee/ bee

  

Further we get unfamiliar spellings of the following words when comparing with modern English

  

envies- envy's

daies - days

retorn'st/return'st-return

findst/find'st-find

wilt-will

  

In the line "If thou be'st born to strange sights". the word be'st means beest and it is a second person singular simple present tense archaic subjunctive of 'be.' It is usually used in a clause beginning with 'If", "beest' can be replaced with 'art' which means 'are' in modern English.

  

* **"And find**

**What wind**

**Serves to advance an honest mind"**

  

In this extract the abstract phrase "honest mind' is a Synecdoche and it stands for an honest person. It is also an Innuendo to convince the true nature of the present society. Accordingly, an honest person has no avenue to rise in the society. Only dishonest and insincere people are rewarded and well-recognized in the society.

  

### **Hyperbole**

  

The poet vastly exaggerates the period of possible travel of the listener to ten thousand days and nights. It is one of the hyperbole used in the poem

  

"Ride ten thousand days and nights,

Till age snow white hairs on thee;"

  

There is another hyperbole at the end of the poem

  

"Though she were true when you met her, And lat, till you write your letter, Yet she

Will be

False, are 1 come, to two or three,"

  

The poet exaggerates the possibility of faithlessness of the fair woman in so short a span of time.

  

### **Sound Devices**

  

As far as the sound devices are concerned, many monosyllabic words, vowels and liquid consonants like L, M and N are used in the first stanza to draw the attention and concentration of the listener

  

### **Humour**

  

Humour is brought out in the poem through the above hyperboles. On the other hand Donne generalizes the inconsistency and infidelity of all women through his arguments. He has nothing to prove his logic except his own imagination and perhaps his own experience. But it is unfair to run down all fair women. Even women who read the poem would also laugh at this baseless allegation against them without being serious. They may think that it is poet's fantasy.
