Money By Philip Larkin [1922 – 1985 / Post -Modernism]  
  
Note on the poet  
  
Philip Larkin, an eminent writer in postwar England. He was born on August 9, 1922, in Coventry. He was the second child, and only son, of Sydney and Eva Larkin. He attended the City's King Henry VIII School between 1930 and 1940. After leaving King Henry college, he went to St. John's College, Oxford, despite the war. Larkin had a poor eyesight. But, he was able to complete his degree without interruption, graduating in 1943 with First Class Honours in English. His closest friends at Oxford were Kingsley Amis and Bruce Montgomery.  
  
The first of his poems to be published in a national weekly was 'Ultimatum', which appeared in the Listener, November 28, 1940. Then in June 1943, three of his poems were published in Oxford Poetry (1942-43). These were 'A Stone Church Damaged By A Bomb', 'Mythological Introduction', and 'I dreamed of an out-thrust arm of land'.  
  
After graduating, Larkin lived with his parents for a while, before being appointed Librarian Wellington, Shropshire, in November of 1943. Here, he studied to qualify as a professional librarian but continued to write and publish. In 1945, ten of his poems appeared in Poetry from Oxford in Wartime.  
  
Two novels, Jill and A Girl in Winter were published in 1946 and 1947 respectively.  
  
In 1946, Larkin became the assistant Librarian at the University College of Leicester. He completed his professional studies and became an Associate of the Library Association in 1949. In October 1950, he became the Sub-Librarian at Queen's University, Belfast. It was in Belfast that he applied fresh vigour to his poetry activities, and, in 1951, had a small collection, XX Poems, privately printed in an edition of 100 copies. Also, in 1954, the Fantasy Press published a pamphlet containing five of his poems.  
  
Larkin took up the position of Librarian at the University of Hull on March 21, 1955, and it was in October of that year that The Less Deceived was published. It was this collection that would be the foundation of his reputation as one of the foremost figures in 20th Century poetry.  
  
It wasn't until 1964 that his next collection, The Whitsun Weddings was published. Again, the collection was well received, and widely acclaimed, and the following year, Larkin was awarded the Queen's Gold Medal for Poetry.  
  
During the years 1961-71 that Larkin contributed monthly reviews of jazz recordings for the Daily Telegraph, and these reviews were brought together and published in 1970 under the title All What Jazz: a record diary 1961-1968. He also edited the Oxford Book of Twentieth Century English Verse, which was published in 1973.  
  
His last collection High Windows was published in 1974, and confirmed him as one of the finest poets in English Literary history. 'Aubade', his last great poem, was published in The Times Literary Supplement in December 1977. If this had been the only poem Larkin had ever written, his place in English poetry would still be secure.  
  
A collection of his essays and reviews was published in November 1983 as Required Writing: miscellaneous pieces 1955-1982, and won the W.H. Smith Literary Award for 1984.  
  
Larkin received many awards in recognition of his writing, especially in his later years. In 1975 he was awarded the CBE, and in 1976 was given the German Shakespeare-Prize. He chaired the Booker Prize Panel in 1977, was made Companion of Literature in 1978, and served on the Literature Panel of the Arts between 1980 and 1982. He was made an Honorary Fellow of the Library Association in 1980. In 1982 the University of Hull made him a Professor.  
  
In 1984 he received an honorary D.Litt. from Oxford University, and was elected to the Board of the British Library. In December of 1984 he was offered the chance to succeed Sir John Betjeman as Poet Laureate but declined, being unwilling to accept the high public profile and associated media attention of the position.  
  
In mid 1985 Larkin was admitted to hospital with an illness in his throat, and on June 11 an operation was carried out to remove his esophagus. His health was deteriorating, and when he was awarded the much prized Order of the Companion of Honour he was unable, because of ill health, to attend the investiture, which was due to take place at Buckingham Palace on November 25. He received the official notification courtesy of the Royal Mail.  
  
Philip Larkin died of cancer at 1.24 a.m. on Monday December 2 1985. He was 63 years old.

Analysis of the stanzas  
  
Stanza - 1  
  
Quarterly, is it, money reproaches me:  
'Why do you let me lie here wastefully?  
I am all you never had of goods and sex.  
You could get them still by writing a few cheques.'  
  
In the first stanza, money is speaking to the persona reproachfully. The persona represents all the people who use money as the medium of transaction. Accordingly, money questions the persona critically why he lets money lie there wastefully. Money seems to be worried as it is not spent by the persona. The persona appears to be very stingy and frugal.  
  
Money boasts and feels proud of giving his purchasing power to people, Money says that persona is never able to buy goods and sex without him. It is evident that money commodifies all the needs of people including even one of the basic human needs: sex. in the modern capitalism. There are easy ways of purchasing goods and services by writing cheques instead of hard cash. At present, even some more facilities are available for transactions like credit cards., E-commerce and so on.  
  
Stanza-2  
  
So I look at others, what they do with theirs:  
They certainly don't keep it upstairs.  
By now they've a second house and car and wife:  
Clearly money has something to do with life  
  
The second stanza focuses on the people's interest in saving money and spending money.  
  
Accordingly, the persona seems to be interested in saving money. Therefore, the persona represents the people who get contentment by having large deposits in the banks or keeping money with themselves.. Nevertheless, the persona observes and notices some other people who may belong to the middle class, have purchased 'a second house, car and wife.'  
  
It is true that a person's purchasing power, economic and social status are highly considered in the modern society. Therefore, the mundane possessions like luxurious houses, vehicles, land property, mobile phones, etc. are some of the visible and tangible things that decide and display the sound wealth. and ideal life of a modern man. Thus, they think money has a lot to do with life. But, the poet has a sarcastic and satirical attitude towards such people. He points out that modern man has a strong desire to outshine and out do others by possessing even a second house and a car. In addition, they are in need of a second wife and ready to spend money to buy such a second wife. In that sense it is quite obvious that such a 'second wife' is like another commodity available at the market. Thus the sublime marital relationships are also based on power of money at present and people tend to have even extra- marital relationships because of their monetary power. Although it looks immoral and inhuman in the civilized society, it is normal and fashionable in the modern society.  
  
Stanza - 3  
  
In fact, they've a lot in common, if you enquire:  
You can't put off being young until you retire,  
And however you bank your screw, the money you save Won't in the end buy you more than a shave.  
  
There is a link between the last line of the second stanza and the first line of this stanza. The poet points out that both money and life have a lot in common. As you cannot save up your youth for your old age, your savings in the bank will not have any value by the time you retire. Here, "bank our screw" refers to the money earned by somebody from a day's job and then saving it. your  
  
People grow old with the passage of time losing the physical and mental strength and power. Therefore, you cannot postpone your young age in order to get all its benefits like attraction, strength, and vitality at the old age. In other words, you must spend your youth joyfully and meaningfully because you will not get that valuable youth again. So the things you are expected to do at the young age, should be done without wasting it. In the same way, your earned money should be spent meaningfully and fruitfully without depositing them for future because the buying power of money will eventually decrease as a result of its depreciation. That is why the speaker says "the money you save won't in the end buy you more than a shave".  
  
The "shave" is here referred to the final shave that an undertaker gives a corpse in the casket.  
  
Stanza - 4  
  
I listen to money singing. It's like looking down From long French windows at a provincial town, The slums, the canal, the churches ornate and mad In the evening sun. It is intensely sad.  
  
At the beginning of the poem, money was speaking to the persona reproachfully. But now, the persona listens to singing of money. Money has ensconced in a room with French windows looking down far below at people madly pursuing life. What does the place where money is occupying at this moment suggest? It could be an upstairs luxurious residence fixed with French windows.  
  
Larkin draws our attention to a socio-economic aspect of the social system. Accordingly, "French windows' symbolize the affluent class whereas the slums symbolize the deplorable condition and the absolute poverty of the majority of people in the town being unable to fulfill even one of their basic human needs like a proper house with better living condition. This class difference between haves and have nots is the visible reality in modern capitalism which is based on the power of money.  
  
The poet is also critical about the hypocritical role of the church. The Bible professes that "the meek shall inherit the earth;" However, in reality, churches are often in mad pursuit of gaining amass great wealth and power while ignoring the needs of the poor.  
  
Thus, money very vividly witnesses the obvious contrast brought out through the scenes of the slums and the ornate churches and laments by singing a melancholic song in the evening expressing the theme of man's greed of money and power of money verses the sublime qualities like morality and humanity.  
  
Themes of the poem

- The relationship between money and happiness.\
    
- Socio-economic disparity brought out by money.\
    
- Human behavior in the modern capitalism.\
    
- Immorality and inhumanity brought out by money.\
    
- Power of money in the modern social system.\
    
- Frustration brought out by money.\
    
- Materialism vs. spiritualism\
    
- Modern values and life\
    
- Criticism of consumerism


Poetic Techniques  

Structure / Form - There are four stanzas in quatrain form  

Narrative point of view  

In the first stanza, the persona tells how money is speaking to him reproachfully. Then we can listen to what money says. In the second stanza, the persona reports us what he observes form his point of view. In the third stanza, the persona makes analysis based on his observation and examination about money and human life. In the last stanza, the persona makes a critique based on the singing of money.

Third Stanza  
Double E

In each stanza, the tone of the final couplet creates a mock effect and the words bring out deep sense. Accordingly, the first rhyming pair; Sex/ cheques intensifies the fact that even sex has become a commodity and sexual relationships are based on the buying power of man.  
   
In the second stanza, the rhyming couplet; wife/life suggests that a second wife is a part of modern man's fashionable and carnal life and power of money.  
  
In the third stanza, the rhyming pair save/ shave brings out an ironic sense that savings of man depreciate having hardly enough money even for a shave in the end.  
   
In the last stanza, the rhyming couplet mad / sad brings out another irony that man's mad pursuit of money brings out nothing in the end but only frustration and sorrow.
