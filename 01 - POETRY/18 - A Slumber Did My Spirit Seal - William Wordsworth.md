# A Slumber Did My Spirit Seal
**By William Wordsworth**

*[1770 - 1850 / Romantic Age]*

---

## Poem Text

> A slumber did my spirit seal;  
> I had no human fears:  
> She seemed a thing that could not feel  
> The touch of earthly years.  
> No motion has she now, no force;  
> She neither hears nor sees;  
> Rolled round in earth's diurnal course,  
> With rocks, and stones, and trees.

---

## The Lucy Poems

The Lucy poems are a series of five poems composed by the English Romantic poet William Wordsworth between 1798 and 1801. The poems were written during a short period while the poet lived in Germany. Wordsworth examines the poet's unrequited love for the idealized character of Lucy, an English girl who has died young. The idea of her death weighs heavily on the poet throughout the series, imbuing it with a melancholic, elegiac tone.

Whether Lucy was based on a real woman or was a figment of the poet's imagination has long been a matter of debate among scholars. Wordsworth never revealed the details of her origin or identity. Some scholars speculate that Lucy is based on his sister Dorothy, while others see her as a fictitious or hybrid character. Most critics agree that she is essentially a literary device upon whom he could project, meditate and reflect.

The "Lucy poems" consist of:

1. "Strange fits of passion have I known"
2. "She dwelt among the untrodden ways"
3. "I travelled among unknown men"
4. "Three years she grew in sun and shower"
5. "A slumber did my spirit seal"

Although they are presented as a series in modern anthologies, Wordsworth did not conceive of them as a group, nor did he seek to publish the poems in sequence. He described the works as "experimental" in the prefaces to both the 1798 and 1800 editions of *Lyrical Ballads*, and revised the poems significantly — shifting their thematic emphasis between 1798 and 1799. Only after his death in 1850 did publishers and critics begin to treat the poems as a fixed group; anthologies since have generally presented them as a series.

## Note on the Poem 'A Slumber Did My Spirit Seal'

"A slumber did my spirit seal" is a poem written by William Wordsworth in 1798 and published in the 1800 edition of *Lyrical Ballads*. It is usually included as one of his Lucy poems, although it is the only poem of the series not to mention her name. The poem is a mere eight lines long: two stanzas.

During the autumn of 1798, Wordsworth travelled to Germany with his sister Dorothy and fellow poet Samuel Taylor Coleridge. From October 1798, Wordsworth worked on the first drafts for his "Lucy poems", which included "A slumber Did My Spirit Seal". It was published in the 1800 edition of *Lyrical Ballads*.

This poem is about the death of a loved one, perhaps Lucy, and the poet's feeling about his beloved when he thinks about her death. The poem also describes his imagination about his beloved after death. This poem is a kind of elegy. In this poem, the poet seems to be immortalizing her death by saying that she had no human fears. Now earthly years were no longer a matter of concern for her because they cannot make her older now. In the second stanza he is describing her dead body. She is not able to perform any of the physical movement or activity now. In the last two lines the poet describes that she is now under the surface of the earth revolving along with it on its path. He tells us that like other stones, rocks and trees, she also revolves with the earth now.

## Meanings of Words

- **slumber** - to sleep, esp. peacefully / to be in a state of inactivity, quiescence
- **spirit** - soul / mind
- **seal** - close off
- **earthly years** - time
- **motion** - the action or process of moving
- **diurnal course** - relating to or occurring in a 24-hour period; daily

## Analysis of the Lines

### First Stanza

> A slumber did my spirit seal;  
> I had no human fears:  
> She seemed a thing that could not feel  
> The touch of earthly years.

At the beginning of the stanza, the poet declares that his spirit or mind slumbered, not his body. The poet uses the word *slumber* instead of *sleeping* and *dying*. Slumber is a creative state of near sleep when soul and imagination are very much alive and awake. Therefore, it implies that the poet was in a dream-like state, devoid of any common fears which usually humans experience. The word 'seal' signifies closing off completely. Therefore, it is obvious that his mind's eye was sealed. Consequently, he does not have human fears. What are these human fears? They may be the fears of death, mutilation, losing autonomy, separation, rejection, humiliation, worthlessness, aging and so on. Therefore, he was in a state of bliss.

He is saying that he was brave and strong enough to face the untimely death of his beloved without the human fears of separation and aging.

In the third line, the poet uses the pronoun 'She' for his unnamed female love. Perhaps, she could be Lucy. When she died, her body was in last slumber lacking the ability and strength to move. "She seemed a thing..." suggests that the poet's beloved seemed a thing of non-sensate so that she could not be touched by time. In other words, she is not subject to the matters regarding time in physical life like aging.

Thus, it is quite obvious that both the poet and his lover gain eternity without exposing to human fears and the touch of earthly years.

### Second Stanza

> No motion has she now, no force;  
> She neither hears nor sees;  
> Rolled round in earth's diurnal course,  
> With rocks, and stones, and trees.

In the second stanza Wordsworth offers peculiar description of the beloved's present situation. We can learn that she has died. She is blind and deaf, being incapable of perceiving the world around her. The poet also mentions that she is now without motion or force. It implies that she had been more energetic and dynamic before her death.

In the last two lines the poet reveals that his beloved has decomposed and become an integral part of the earth, rolling with it as it turns day to day.

> 'Rolled round in earth's diurnal course'

The poet is so meticulous not to use the word 'die' for Lucy's fate. Instead, the Latin term 'diurnal' [diurnus] is used and it refers to the world of nature. Lucy's lack of motion mirrors the poet's gentle 'slumber' in the first stanza, suggesting that death is a peaceful sleep. Accordingly, the word 'diurnal' implies that the girl is now back amongst the world of nature, and has become a part of something greater. That is why the poet's response to her death lacks bitterness or emptiness. Instead, he gets eternal happiness from the fact that she is now beyond the human fears and earthly years.

## Themes

- Death of a beloved one brings pain and sorrow. Nevertheless, we can get relief and eternal happiness because the dead person becomes an integral part of the earth, merging with all other things.
- Death makes a person a part and parcel of nature, devoid of earthly pains and feelings.
- Man comes from dust and returns to dust after death, proving the maxim 'dust to dust, earth to earth'
- Nature is permanent through the process of recycling. One's death takes another form of nature.
- The best way to escape the fear of death is to forget the notion of death, being in a state of slumber.

## Poetic Techniques

### Form

The poem has only eight lines divided into two stanzas. It is a lyrical ballad. The lyric is a short musical poem that can be sung. The poem also becomes an elegy because it expresses sorrow for someone's death. In this poem, the poet laments over the death of Lucy, his beloved. But we do not feel he is really lamenting because he has made his mind with the thought that she has become a part of nature after death, free from human fears and earthly years.

### Rhyme Scheme

Rhyme scheme used in the poem is ABAB / CDCD

> A slumber did my spirit seal; - **A**  
> I had no human fears: - **B**  
> She seemed a thing that could not feel - **A**  
> The touch of earthly years. - **B**  
> No motion has she now, no force; - **C**  
> She neither hears nor sees; - **D**  
> Rolled round in earth's diurnal course, - **C**  
> With rocks, and stones, and trees - **D**

### Meter

The circular shape of the rhythm reflects the idea that life is a vicious circle. Man comes from dust and turns to dust after death. The poet uses alternate or interchanging rhythm. The reader finds that the first and third lines, in each stanza, are in iambic tetrameter and the second and fourth lines are in iambic trimeter. Each foot consists of two syllables; the first is unstressed and the second is stressed. But note that in the 5th line and the 7th line the iambic foot is reversed to give much stress on "No" and "Rolled".

#### 1st Stanza

> U / U / U / U /  
> A slumber did my spirit seal;  
> U / U / U /  
> I had no human fears:  
> U / U / U / U /  
> She seemed a thing that could not feel  
> U / U / U /  
> The touch of earthly years.

#### 2nd Stanza

> S / U / U / S /  
> No motion has she now, no force;  
> U / U / U /  
> She neither hears nor sees;  
> S / U / U / U /  
> Rolled round in earth's diurnal course,  
> U / U / U /  
> With rocks and stones and trees.

### Caesura

Caesura is a break between words within a metrical foot to recite the lines. The purpose of using a caesura is to create a dramatic pause, which has a strong impact. The pause helps to add an emotional, often theatrical touch to the sentence and conveys a depth of sentiment in a short phrase. In the poem, we get caesura in the second stanza as follows:

> "No motion has she now, // no force"  
> "With rocks, // and stones, // and trees"

### Language / Tone / Style

Descriptions and statements are made in very plain manner. The language of the poem is very simple. "Slumber" is used in the sense of inactivity referring to the shock or dazed condition of the poet. The word "diurnal" refers to Lucy's everlasting union with earth in its daily routine of life and movement.

The tone is solemn throughout the poem. The poet uses appropriate diction to achieve this solemnity. So the selected words and phrases are more poetic rather than colloquial.

Examples:

- **Slumber** - sleep
- **Seal** - close
- **Earthly years** - time
- **Diurnal course** - daily routine

Further, the 'r', 'd' and 'o' sounds in the three 'no's and in the words *motion, force, rolled, round, diurnal course, rocks, stones* have strong effect to boost the solemn tone.

We also can notice that the last two lines of the poem reflect the last two lines in the first stanza. Both refer to the idea of mortality and the circular nature of life. The first stanza is written in the past tense. In the first stanza, the poet reminisces the sight of the beloved's body after her demise. Yet, the second stanza is written in the present tense and it refers to her burial and the natural phenomenon that she faces.

### Alliteration / Assonance / Repetition

In the first line of the poem, the sound 's' (*slumber, spirit, seal*) is repeated and this repetition of a consonant sound is a figure of speech called 'alliteration' and it creates the effect of sleeping and dreaming. 'R' sound in the line 'Rolled round in earth's diurnal course' is another example of alliteration and it reflects the idea of recycling and constant cycle of life after death.

In the second stanza, "O" sound is repeated to make the effect of rolling and rotating. The repetition of the word "and" in the last line suggests the continuous recycling process of nature. Moreover, the repetition of "no" and "nor" stresses the idea that Lucy has become a part of nature without sensory perception and motion.

### Imagery

An image is a verbal representation of a sensory experience or of an object that can be known by one or more of the senses like sight, smell, touch and taste.

Imagery is vivid descriptive language that appeals to one or more of the senses. The description of the speaker's dead beloved as being:

> "Rolled round in earth's diurnal course  
> With rocks, and stones, and trees"

is an example of imagery. The visual picture in our mind of earth revolving around the sun, and consequently the body and soul of the speaker's beloved revolving along with rocks, stones and trees suggests her as becoming one with Nature after her death.

### Metaphor / Symbol

A symbol is an image which suggests or represents something other than itself. In poetry, a symbol represents both what it is, and additionally, a concept or an idea. In this poem *rocks, and stones, and trees* symbolize nature. *Diurnal course* also symbolizes the cycle of nature. 'The touch of earthly years' is a metaphor in the poem. It is a metaphor for time and aging.

### Paradox

The second stanza is paradoxical because the girl Lucy who has no motion after death rolls round in the earth's diurnal course. Though this idea looks absurd, it is true because the girl who has become a part of nature may take different forms in the recycling process of nature.
