# Batter my heart, three-person'd God
**By John Donne**

*[1572 - 1631 / Elizabethan and Jacobean Period]*

---

## Poem Text - Modern Version

> Batter my heart, three-person'd God, for you  
> As yet but knock, breathe, shine, and seek to mend;  
> That I may rise and stand, o'erthrow me, and bend  
> Your force to break, blow, burn, and make me new.  
> I, like an usurp'd town to another due,  
> Labor to admit you, but oh, to no end;  
> Reason, your viceroy in me, me should defend,  
> But is captiv'd, and proves weak or untrue.  
> Yet dearly I love you, and would be lov'd fain,  
> But am betroth'd unto your enemy;  
> Divorce me, untie or break that knot again,  
> Take me to you, imprison me, for I,  
> Except you enthrall me, never shall be free,  
> Nor ever chaste, except you ravish me.

---

## The Holy Sonnets of John Donne

There are nineteen poems written by John Donne as the Holy Sonnets or Divine Sonnets during the period from 1572-1631. These sonnets were first published in 1633, two years after Donne's death. Donne followed the style and form prescribed by Renaissance Italian poet Francesco Petrarca (1304-1374). Accordingly, these sonnets consist of an octave including eight lines and a sestet with six lines. Nevertheless, several rhythmic and structural patterns as well as the inclusion of couplets are elements influenced by the sonnet form developed by English poet and playwright William Shakespeare (1564-1616).

Donne's religious poems also belong to the Metaphysical school of poetry. The nineteen poems that constitute the collection were not published during Donne's lifetime although they did circulate in manuscript. Many of the poems are believed to have been written in 1609 and 1610, during a period of great personal distress and strife for Donne who suffered a combination of physical, emotional, and financial hardships during this time. This was also a time of personal religious turmoil as Donne was in the process of conversion from Roman Catholicism to Anglicanism. He took holy orders in 1615 despite profound reluctance and significant self-doubt about becoming a priest.

In Holy Sonnets, Donne addresses religious themes of mortality, divine judgment, divine love, and humble penance while reflecting deeply personal anxieties.

19. "Oh, to vex me, contraries meet in one"

## Note on the Sonnet 'Batter my heart, three-person'd God'

Batter my Heart is one of the beautiful religious sonnets of Donne. After the death of his wife in 1617, Donne fell more and more under the shadow of a terrible spiritual gloom. As his life drew near its close, Donne devoted his talent to write religious sonnets. Batter my Heart is one of the products of this period of his life. Donne gave up sensuous life completely behind him and was highly involved in fierce anxiety for the right relationship with the eternal. The poet felt guilty conscious of his life spent and had a strong yearning to be with God's infinite greatness.

Donne's religious sermons and poems reached astounding heights of subtlety and intensity. The searching of soul and the horrified fascination to God can be seen in "Batter my Heart" with amazing sincerity, intensity and earnestness. Donne's grand style of expressing noble thought in this poem deserves admiration.

## Summary of the Sonnet

The speaker asks God to intensify the effort to restore the speaker's soul. Knocking at the door is not enough; God should overthrow him like a besieged town. His own reason has not been enough either, and he has engaged himself to God's enemy. He asks God to break the knots holding him back, imprisoning him in order to free him, and taking him by force in order to purify him.

## Analysis of the Lines

### Octave

#### Lines 1-4

> Batter my heart, three-person'd God, for you  
> As yet but knock, breathe, shine, and seek to mend;  
> That I may rise and stand, o'erthrow me, and bend  
> Your force to break, blow, burn, and make me new.

**Vocabulary:**

- **Batter** - strike repeatedly with hard blows / a battering ram which was used to break down doors to a castle during this time period
- **Three-person'd God** - the Trinity: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit in Christian religion

At the beginning of the sonnet, the poet employs the image of a blacksmith for God. In Christian theology, there is only one God. But, that one God [Father] is actually made up of three along with Jesus [Son] and the Holy Ghost [Holy Spirit]. They are the trinity that makes up the Christian *Three-personed* God.

God is thereby compared to a blacksmith and the speaker himself to an object. The word 'batter' refers to a battering ram which was used to break down doors to enter a castle during that period. He asks God to "batter" his heart to mend it, as opposed to what God has been doing so far: just knocking, breathing, shining, and trying to help the speaker heal. Why does he expect mending? Perhaps, he may feel that he has committed a lot of sins and he wants to get his heart purified through harsh suffering. There is also the metaphor or image of a door used in the second line. His heart is compared to a door and he asks God not to gently knock but batter it to enter his heart.

Therefore, instead of the divine actions like knocking, breathing and shining, the speaker asks God to treat him more violently in order to make him a new person to rise and stand, using very hard and harsh actions like breaking, blowing and burning. Why is the speaker in need of such severe suffering?

Probably it is a reference to the Christian idea that true happiness and salvation come only after death. Earthly life must be a continual act of suffering to enter heaven. That is why the speaker wants to be abused and broken in the earthly world. Then he will be worthy for the afterlife. Thus, he requests, "Batter my heart" (line 1), metaphorically indicating that he wants God to use force to assault his heart, like battering down a door, as God's divine actions have not worked successfully and sufficiently.

This request indicates that the speaker considers his soul or heart too badly damaged or too sinful to be renewable, so that God must re-create him using his absolute mighty power.

#### Lines 5-8

> I, like an usurp'd town to another due,  
> Labor to admit you, but oh, to no end;  
> Reason, your viceroy in me, me should defend,  
> But is captiv'd, and proves weak or untrue.

**Vocabulary:**

- **usurp'd town** - a captured or seized town by force
- **due** - belong
- **Labour to admit** - strive to let God in
- **viceroy** - a ruler exercising authority in a colony on behalf of a sovereign
- **captiv'd** - captured person

In this quatrain, the speaker compares himself to a town that is captured or "usurped." The phrase "to another due" suggests that the town belongs to someone else. It has been forcibly taken from his rightful owner. The sigh and exclamation "oh" in line 6 reveals the speaker's honest sad feelings at this present situation.

Reason is personified as God's viceroy and it is the faculty given to man by God as a help for him to think logically and live without exposing to evils. In other words, man's rationality protects and guards him from Satan and temptation.

But now the speaker's reason seems to be incapable of warding off evil. Therefore, the speaker has got embarrassed in showing his faith in God. Reason has been imprisoned by the devil and has become weak. This is a fine metaphysical conceit employed by the poet.

### Sestet

#### Lines 9-12

> Yet dearly I love you, and would be lov'd fain,  
> But am betroth'd unto your enemy;  
> Divorce me, untie or break that knot again,  
> Take me to you, imprison me, for I,

**Vocabulary:**

- **Fain** - glad / pleased / happy
- **betroth'd** - engaged to marry

As far as the traditional sonnet form is concerned, the ninth line marks the 'turn' in the poem because the problem developed in the octave begins to move towards a solution. On the contrary, the ninth line of this sonnet does not function as a turn at all. It further develops the speaker's strong yearning to be with God. He says he would be happy to be loved. Nevertheless, it is ironic that the speaker is engaged to marry God's enemy. Who is God's enemy? Definitely, the speaker may be referring to Satan. What caused the speaker to have such a close relationship with Satan instead of God? It is nothing else but losing his rationality as pointed out in the previous lines.

However, now he seems to be guilty-conscious of his engagement with God's enemy. He asks God to divorce him to break that engagement knot with Satan again. Here the word 'again' implies that he would have had such engagements with the devil in the past or is referring to a time when God had to break the tie with Satan related to the Biblical story 'Adam and Eve' in the Garden of Eden.

However, the speaker asks God to take him and imprison him using God's power. The speaker thereby links the metaphor 'usurp'd town' to convey again his wish for God to imprison him, bringing back the idea that Christians must endure pain and struggle in order to get into Heaven. On the other hand, the last line is paradoxical because the speaker has to be imprisoned to be free. Can one become free if he is imprisoned? Literally, it is impossible. But paradoxically, we have to consider that it is God who captivates and imprisons him to make him free from the devil and sin.

#### Lines 13-14

> Except you enthrall me, never shall be free,  
> Nor ever chaste, except you ravish me.

**Vocabulary:**

- **enthrall** - capture / enslave
- **chaste** - abstaining from extramarital, or from all, sexual intercourse
- **ravish** - seize or carry off by force / rape

In the final lines, the poet uses another paradox. The speaker asks God to capture and ravish him so that he can be chaste. Here, if the word 'enthrall' is defined as 'excite' it creates a sexual image and has sexual connotation. Otherwise it can be taken as 'enslave' to give the meaning of 'captured.' On the other hand the word 'ravish' has multiple literal meanings as seizing, carrying off or having sex forcibly [rape]. If these words are regarded as sexual connotation, the speaker may appear in the female role of betrothal and ravishment.

Again the question arises whether one can be chaste or preserve chastity if she is raped. Once again the paradox is resolved because if it is God who takes possession of him entirely and violently, he will be chaste or pure without sin.

All these sexual allusions should be defined, analyzed and concluded with theological knowledge. Otherwise, it is absurd to look at them from the human point of view. As the poet does not have any other option, he may be using paradox as a medium of expression.

## Poetic Techniques

### Structure

Donne usually combines the elements of both Petrarchan and Shakespearean style in his sonnets. In this sonnet, he mostly follows Petrarchan style. Accordingly, in the octave, the persona expresses his longing to be reborn afresh. He asks God to use his violence and mighty power to renew him by liberating his sinful heart. In the sestet, the persona affirms his love for God and need for breaking his bond with God's enemy, devil. Finally the sestet ends with the persona's pleading for God's salvation. Donne uses the Shakespearean style by ending the poem with a rhyming couplet that encapsulates the key idea of his poem.

### Rhyme Scheme

The sonnet form used by Donne in Batter my heart is actually very complex. The octave form of the first part, with the rhyming scheme of ABBA ABBA, definitely suggests the Petrarchan form.

> Batter my heart, three person'd God; for, you - **A**  
> As yet but knocke, breathe, shine, and seeke to mend; - **B**  
> That I may rise, and stand, o'erthrow mee, and bend - **B**  
> Your force, to breake, blowe, burn and make me new - **A**  
> I like an usurpt towne, to another due, - **A**  
> Labour to admit you, but Oh, to no end, - **B**  
> Reason your viceroy in mee, mee should defend, - **B**  
> But is captiv'd, and proves weake or untrue - **A**

The sestet is somewhat complex due to its mixed form of Petrarchan and Shakespearean sonnets. Donne tries to get the clinching effect of the sonnet at the end using a couplet following the Shakespearean sonnet form.

Accordingly, the sestet consists of CDCD EE rhyming scheme. But, the words like *enemie* and *I* do not have sound rhyme.

> Yet dearely I love you, and would be loved faine, - **C**  
> But am betroth'd unto your enemie: - **D**  
> Divorce mee, untie, or breake that knot againe, - **C**  
> Take mee to you, imprison mee, for I - **D**  
> Except you enthrall mee, never shall be free, - **E**  
> Nor ever chast, except you ravish mee. - **E**

### Meter

John Donne employs a number of metrical variations strategically with a variety of stress levels to realize his beats and off beats.

Donne uses an inversion in the first foot of the first line to stress the key verb, "batter", and then sets up a clear iambic pattern with the rest of the line. In the second and fourth lines he uses strongly-stressed off beats that can be interpreted as spondees. Donne also uses enjambment between lines three and four to speed up the flow as he confirms his desire to be made new. Donne puts an extra syllable in the final foot of the line and this can be read as an anapest.

Examples:

> That I may rise, and stand, o'erthrow me, and bend

When it should read something like this:

> That I may rise, and stand, o'erthrow me'nd bend

### Narrative Point of View

The entire sonnet is presented in the first person narrative form. It makes a personal plea. Therefore, the reader becomes very much closer to the speaker. Consequently, the repetitive use of "mee" and "I" throughout the whole poem is probably used to create sympathy in the reader's mind for the persona.

### Use of Diction and Language Functions

Donne uses imperatives in an unusual manner specially when addressing God. It suggests the sense of immediacy, urgency and desperation of the persona because he is a sinner and in need of taking the attention of God.

Examples:

> "Batter my heart."  
> "Divorce mee, untie, or breake that knot againe,"  
> "Take mee to you, imprison mee, for I"

Plosive sounds in the words 'breake', 'blowe', 'burn' mimic the actions of battering the heart of the persona to make him re-created. These words also convey the energetic emotional conviction behind his rhetoric - anger, contempt, desperation, etc.

A synalepha or synaloepha /sɪnəˈliːfə/ is the merging of two syllables into one, especially when it causes two words to be pronounced as one. The apostrophes indicate the use of synaloepha, a form of elision.

Examples:

> Batter my heart, three person'd God; for, you  
> As yet but knocke, breathe, shine, and seeke to mend;  
> That I may rise, and stand, o'erthrow mee, and bend  
> Your force, to breake, blowe, burn and make me new  
> I like an usurpt towne, to another due,  
> Labour to admit you, but Oh, to no end,  
> Reason your viceroy in mee, mee should defend,  
> But is captiv'd, and proves weake or untrue.  
> Yet dearely I love you, and would be loved faine,  
> But am betroth'd unto your enemie:  
> Divorce mee, untie, or breake that knot againe,  
> Take mee to you, imprison mee, for I  
> Except you enthrall mee, never shall be free,  
> Nor ever chast, except you ravish mee.

The poet has used parallelism as well. All the actions mentioned in line 2 are amplified in line 4. This portrays the increasing sense of emergency that the persona feels.

> "As yet but knocke, breathe, shine, and seeke to mend;"  
> "Your force, to breake, blowe, burn and make me new"

Parallel series of verbs are used to explore the different facets of the three-person'd God:

- God (representing power): 'knocks' and 'breaks'
- Holy Spirit (breathes life): 'breathe' and 'blow'
- Son (play on the word 'sun'): 'shines' and 'burns'

When comparing the original text and the modern text, you can notice some differences in spellings of the words.

For example, Donne spells *usurped* as *usurpt*. He wants the word to be treated as bi-syllabic, not tri-syllabic. In other words, it shouldn't be pronounced *usurpèd*. He apostrophizes *betroth'd* for the same reason. He doesn't want us to pronounce it as *betrothed*. What is the reason for this peculiar type of pronunciation by economizing spellings? The reason is that you will miss the words and phrases that he does want us to pronounce tri-syllabically like "beloved fain".

> Yet dearely I love you, and would belovèd faine, [tri-syllabic]

In modern editions, the accent grave over the è is used for this purpose.

On the other hand monosyllabic words like *knocke, blowe, burn* add to the violence of the tone and mood.

The caesuras [a break between words within a metrical foot marked by commas] at the beginning of the poem slow down the pace and add to the force which makes each word sound almost like a harsh blow.

> Batter my heart, three person'd God; for, you  
> As yet but knocke, breathe, shine, and seeke to mend;  
> That I may rise, and stand, o'erthrow mee, and bend

There are several examples for word play in the lines.

- The word 'enthrall' implies the extent to which the persona is captivated by God.
- The word 'imprisoned' has a negative connotation but when reading in context in the poem, it implies the persona's desperation to be one with God.
- The word 'ravish' has a sexual connotation. While asking God to 'ravish' him the persona expresses his need to experience religious ecstasy rather than sexual contentment.

### Metaphors / Similes / Imagery / Conceit / Personification / Symbolism

- `Batter my heart, three person'd God; for, you` >> conceit
- `As yet but knocke, breathe, shine, and seeke to mend;` >> conceit
- `That I may rise, and stand, o'erthrow mee, and bend` >> kinesthetic imagery / conceit
- `Your force, to breake, blowe, burn and make me new` >> kinesthetic imagery / conceit
- `I like an usurpt towne, to another due,` >> simile >> conceit
- `Labour to admit you, but Oh, to no end,`
- `Reason your viceroy in mee, mee should defend,` >> personification
- `But is captiv'd, and proves weake or untrue.`
- `Yet dearely I love you, and would be loved faine,`
- `But am betroth'd unto your enemie:` >> metaphor
- `Divorce mee, untie, or breake that knot againe,` >> kinesthetic imagery / symbol
- `Take mee to you, imprison mee, for I`
- `Except you enthrall mee, never shall be free,`
- `Nor ever chast, except you ravish mee.`

- Blacksmith conceit is used to compare the re-creation of man by God to the purification and re-moulding of metal. In the analogy the actions related to kinesthetic imagery, "knocke", "breathe", "shine", "breake", "blowe", "burn" are usually used by a blacksmith to transform metal and make it something usable. Similarly the persona asks God to use these actions to transform him into a virtuous person.

- "Usurpt towne" is a conceit to build up the situation of the persona being under the control of Satan just like a town being captured by an enemy.

- "Like an usurpt town" can be considered as a simile to describe the persona's pitiable sinful condition of slavery to sin and how his conscience and reason have been completely overwhelmed by Satan and hence cannot set him free. He can be saved if the Trinity God "batter" and smash through the gates of the captive town. It symbolically suggests that his heart must be released from the clutches of Satan and save his soul. He wants God to completely smash him, rebuild his life anew all over again.

- In the sestet Donne uses the metaphor of "betrothal" to indicate that he is enslaved to Satan and that only if God divorces him and "breaks that knot again" he will be really free. The knot is a symbol of the persona's engagement to Satan. This also can be taken as a conceit based on a metaphor and a symbol.

- Donne personifies "reason" as a 'viceroy' of God. It implies how the persona has become a captive under Satan, because his reason is suppressed without proper function.

- Donne uses marriage imagery to show how he is bound to Satan. So God has to break his soul (which was considered to be feminine) from the shackles of Satan.

- The words "break" and "knock" symbolise the harsh and hard work of God the Father; "blow" and "breathe" symbolise the fiery Holy Spirit; and "burn" and "shine" symbolize the dazzling quality of Jesus the Son of God.

### Paradoxes

In line 3 the persona asks God to 'o'erthrow' him in order for him to 'rise'. This paradox comes back to the Christian idea that one has to undergo pain and suffering in order to gain salvation and become one with God.

The three concluding paradoxes - "imprison," "enthrall" and "ravish" - forcefully emphasize the idea that only if God imprisons and enslaves and ravishes him, he will become free and pure and holy once again.

### Tone

The speaker begins with a soft loving tone as he describes God's ability to batter his heart. As the poem progresses the tone seems to become more desperate and yearning. In the last lines of the poem the persona wants to feel religious ecstasy. The words "ravish mee" have a sexual connotation and might be almost blasphemous. In the third stanza the tone becomes slightly softer as the persona expresses his love for God. The poem ultimately ends on a high tone due to words such as 'ravish mee'.

### Dramatic Quality

The sonnet becomes very live because of its dramatic quality. The persona addresses God throughout the poem to make him a virtuous person for his salvation. So the poem is presented in the form of dramatic monologue. All the actions and imagery also boost the dramatic quality of the poem.
