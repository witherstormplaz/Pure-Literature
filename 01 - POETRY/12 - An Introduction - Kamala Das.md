# An Introduction By Kamala Das 
*[1934 -2009 / Post –Modern Period/ Indian Poetry - Post Colonial Era]*

## The Poem

I don't know politics but I know the names  
Of those in power, and can repeat them like  
Days of week, or names of months, beginning with Nehru.  
I am Indian, very brown, born in Malabar,  
I speak three languages, write in  
Two, dream in one.

Not your mother-tongue. Why not leave  
Me alone, critics, friends, visiting cousins,  
Every one of you? Why not let me speak in  
Any language I like? The language I speak,  
Becomes mine, its distortions, its queernesses  
All mine, mine alone.  
It is half English, half Indian, funny perhaps, but it is honest,  
It is as human as I am human, don't  
You see? It voices my joys, my longings, my  
Hopes, and it is useful to me as cawing Is to crows or roaring to the lions, it  
Is human speech, the speech of the mind that is  
Here and not there, a mind that sees and hears and  
Is aware. Not the deaf, blind speech  
Of trees in storm or of monsoon clouds or of rain or the  
Incoherent mutterings of the blazing  
Funeral pyre. I was child, and later they  
Told me I grew, for I became tall, my limbs  
Swelled and one or two places sprouted hair.  
When I asked for love, not knowing what else to ask  
For, he drew a youth of sixteen into the  
Bedroom and closed the door, He did not beat me  
But my sad woman-body felt so beaten.  
The weight of my breasts and womb crushed me.  
I shrank Pitifully.  
Then I wore a shirt and my

Be a quarreller with servants. Fit in. Oh,  
Belong, cried the categorizers. Don't sit  
On walls or peep in through our lace-draped windows.  
Be Amy, or be Kamala. Or, better  
Still, be Madhavikutty. It is time to  
Choose a name, a role. Don't play pretending games.  
Don't play at schizophrenia or be a  
Nympho. Don't cry embarrassingly loud when  
Jilted in love I met a man, loved him.  
Call Him not by any name, he is every man  
Who wants. a woman, just as I am every Woman who seeks love. In him... the hungry haste  
Of rivers, in me... the oceans' tireless  
Waiting. Who are you, I ask each and everyone,  
The answer is, it is I. Anywhere and,  
Everywhere, I see the one who calls himself I  
In this world, he is tightly packed like the  
Sword in its sheath. It is I who drink lonely  
Drinks at twelve, midnight, in hotels of strange towns,  
It is I who laugh, it is I who make love  
And then, feel shame, it is I who lie dying  
With a rattle in my throat. I am sinner,  
I am saint. I am the beloved and the  
Betrayed. I have no joys that are not yours, no  
Aches which are not yours. I too call myself I.

## Note on the Poet

Kamala Surayya, popularly known by her one-time pen name Madhavikutty and Kamala Das was one of the most prominent feminist writers in the postcolonial era. She was born on 31st March 1934. She spent her childhood in her ancestral home in Malabar, Kerala and in Calcutta. Kamala Das belonged to a literary family. Her mother Balamani Amma was a famous poet and her grand uncle Nalapat Narayana Menon was also a respected writer. Her interest in writing began at a young age while watching her elders' work. 

Kamala Das was married to Madhava Das, an employee at the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) at the age of 15 and moved to Bombay with her husband. She said that a woman had to prove herself to be a good wife, a good mother, before she could become anything else. Kamala Das had three sons - MD Nalapat, Chinnen Das and Jayasurya Das. Her husband appreciated her writing and he allowed her to write at night and even till morning. At the same time, she attended all the household duties like feeding the children, cleaning up the kitchen and so on. She wrote in her mother tongue Malayalam as well as in English. She was popular among her Malayalam readers as Madhavi Kutty and to her English readers, she was Kamala Das. On account of her extensive contribution to the poetry in India, she was honoured as 'The Mother of Modern Indian English Poetry'. She is compared to the English poetess Sylvia Plath because of the confessional style of her writing.

In her poems she tried to focus on women who were confined to their households, and considered a commodity to be exchanged through marriage. She portrayed the women in her poems as human; with desires, pain and emotions just like men. Accordingly, her writing consisted of vivid descriptions of menstruation, puberty, love, lust, lesbian encounters, child marriage, infidelity and physical intimacy. She introduced her readers to the concept of female sexuality, a notion that was nonexistent until then.

In her poem titled 'A Widow's Lament' she describes feeling isolated in a male-dominated society that she could never call her own, because it had no place for its women:

> In one of her earliest works, in the poem 'An Introduction' she expresses her resentment in being confined to gender roles that she did not choose for herself and her desire to break out of them.

Her literary work earned her a lot of recognition and won her numerous accolades. She won the P.E.N.'s Asian Poetry Prize in 1963, the Kerala Sahitya Academy Award in 1969 for the short story Thanuppu (Cold) and the National Sahitya Academy Award in 1985. She was also shortlisted for the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1984. So wide was her reach that much of her work has been translated into numerous foreign languages including French and German. English was the language she chose for all six of her published poetry collections.

Although she was born in a conservative Hindu Nair (Nalapat) family having royal ancestry, she converted to Islam on December 11, 1999, at the age of 65 and assumed the name Kamala Surayya. 

She died, on 31st May 2009, at the age of 75. She died at a hospital in Pune. Her body was flown to her home state of Kerala. She was interred at the Palayam Jama Masjid at Thiruvananthapuram with full state honour.

## Analysis of the Lines

### Lines 1-3

> I don't know politics but I know the names  
> Of those in power, and can repeat them like  
> Days of week, or names of months, beginning with Nehru.

At the beginning of the poem, the poetess says that she does not know politics. Nevertheless, she knows the names of the politicians from the first prime minister Jawaharlal Nehru onwards who came to power in India. She has a good memory of them just like the days of the week and names of months. She seems to state the fact that the Indian politics is confined to a few rulers who belong to elite and Indian democracy means the male domination. 

### Lines 4-6

> I am Indian, very brown, born in Malabar,  
> I speak three languages, write in  
> Two, dream in one.

In these lines, the poetess reveals her national identity and the linguistic capabilities. She thereby, says that she is an Indian and brown in complexion. She was born in Malabar. She can speak three languages, write in two and dreams in one. Like most of the citizens of India, she is also capable of speaking three languages and writing in two probably English and her native language, Malayalam. She also may dream in one universal language that everyone can communicate with each other in the word irrespective of linguistic, racial, national and cultural barriers and disparities. When considering Kamala Das's abilities and capabilities, she excels men in such a patriarchal society by possessing all the qualities, status required to make her superior. 

### Lines 7-13

> Don't write in English, they said, English is  
> Not your mother-tongue. Why not leave  
> Me alone, critics, friends, visiting cousins,  
> Every one of you? Why not let me speak in  
> Any language I like? The language I speak,  
> Becomes mine, its distortions, its queernesses  
> All mine, mine alone.

The speaker is well-familiar with English and she therefore, writes in English. On the contrary, her relatives, friends and critics ask her not to write in English because it is not her mother-tongue. Perhaps, those who are dear and near to her may have several reasons of their own to have such a protest and stance. They may hate the British colonists who occupied in India suppressing and oppressing the Indian culture through their gun and language and the Bible. They also could be jealous of her language competence in speaking and writing. Nevertheless, Kamala Das, like Derek Walcott in 'A Far Cry from Africa' loves English language so that she asks them why they do not let her speak in any language. They actually, do not have sound logical reasons to justify their protest and dislike. 

As far as the use of language is concerned, Kamala Das does not like to define and interpret it in patriotic, racial and extreme point of view. It is only her medium of communication and expression of creativity. She exploits and utilizes the English language in writing as she wants in spite of its distortions and queerness. Thus, it is quite obvious that her English looks Indian and it is another variety of English as Indian English. Accordingly, if anyone criticizes and satirizes of her use of English, it is his ignorance, jealousy and domination of patriarchy. As well, she seems to be critical towards the hypocritical nature of the society that ignores the mistakes made by men and highlights even the trivial mistakes of women by forgetting the fact that no one in the world is perfect.

### Lines 24-32

> I was a child and later they  
> Told me I grew, for I became tall, my limbs

> When I asked for love, not knowing what else to ask

Here 'they' may be the parents of the speaker or the other people of society who are conservative and patriarchal in their attitude specially towards women and existing social norms and practices. They think that women have little freedom to voice their views. They told her that she had grown up by just considering her physical growth with height, swelled limbs and sprouted hair around genitals brought out by the changes of her puberty. The age of puberty is full of new joys, emotions, imaginations and romantic thoughts. It is an age which has a strong desire for love and freedom. On the contrary, at the age of sixteen, her parents forced her to enter a traditional marriage as a child-bride with an elder man according to the marital customs and traditions in rural areas in India. Actually Kamala Das married Madhava Das, a bank officer who was 35 years at the time of their marriage. Although her physical growth was visible outwardly, mentally she did not have enough maturity to understand the matters associated with marriages such as love and sex. After marriage, she would have asked love from her husband as any innocent teenager has a strong desire for love rather than sex. The line "not knowing what else to ask" suggests that she had not known what marriage meant and what she should expect from married life. In place of love and affection, the speaker reminisces how her husband responded her at that moment.

> "he drew a youth of sixteen into the Bedroom and closed the door"

The closing of the bedroom door is euphemistic and suggestive. It implies the sexual copulation between the husband and the wife. The 'door' also symbolizes freedom so that closing of the door can be interpreted as losing freedom of the child bride. In other words, thus juvenile marriage proved the saying 'wedlock is padlock'.

After she was taken into the room, she felt like she was beaten on the bedroom. The word 'beaten' in this line has been connotatively used not for physical beating but for the sexual and mental torture. Therefore, it suggests how she felt painful physically in her sexual life. She used the phrase 'My sad woman body' to show how she was very young and her body was not strong enough to bear up sex of a physically matured husband.

The line 'The weight of my breasts and womb crushed me' implies how she felt while her husband was satisfying his lust. This also could be interpreted connotatively as her pregnancy. Such a teen girl has to bear the responsibility of giving birth to children. Then she has to nourish them with breast feeding. It is clear that immature age is not suitable for conceiving a child and delivery of a child. But it is pity that as most of the victims of the social practice and patriarchy, the poetess also had to experience the bitter reality being unable to bear the weight of her breasts and crushed womb. This situation made her to shrink in a corner.

We get more details about the love and marriage of Kamala Das in her autobiographical book 'My Story'. Accordingly, her first encounter with her husband was an emotional and physical shock for her. She had expected very romantic love and care from her husband such as taking her into his arms and caressing her face, her hair, her hands and whispering soothing words. Thus she wanted conversation, companionship and warmth. She also expected that he would get rid of her loneliness of life. She further expresses that she never expected 'rough hands rising up her skirts and tearing up her brassiere' and "Whenever he found me alone in a room, he began to plead with me to bare my breasts and if I did not he turned brutal and crude." His hands bruised her body and left blue and red marks on the skin. Thus her life became miserable in the company of her nonchalant and lustful husband. He was also addicted to homo-sexuality. In her autobiography, Kamala Das writes: "At this time my husband turned to his old friend for comfort. They behaved like lovers in my presence. To celebrate my birthday they shoved me out of the bedroom and locked themselves in. I stood for a while wondering what two men could possibly do together to get some physical rapture." He also boasted of his knowledge about 'sluts and nymphomaniacs'. Consequently, his contact with his wife was usually cruel and brutal.

It is quite evident that what Kamala Das mentioned in her autobiographies reflects in the above lines regarding her love, marriage and sexuality.

The so-called conservatives are called 'categorizers' or the extremists of patriarchy. They were asking Kamala Das not to sit on walls and peep through the lace-draped windows. Without being kind and polite, they cried to prohibit her non-conformist behavior vehemently.

## Themes

Kamala Das is recognized as a confessional poetess. What does confessional poetry mean? Confessional poetry was a reaction to the depersonalized poetry of the 1920s and 1930s. Poets of that era believed that a poem was different from the poet himself, which confessional poets did not adhere to. It was only in the 1950s that confessional poetry was introduced. This style of writing could be seen throughout the literary history. Italian poet Petrarch had used first person in his poems to express his feelings and thoughts. In the sixteenth century, William Shakespeare's sonnets exposed different aspects of a poet's personality. A great romantic English poet, William Wordsworth has also written poems based on his feelings and experiences. His magnum opus The Prelude can be considered his autobiography. The same can be said for Wordsworth's contemporary, S. T. Coleridge. In 1943, American poet and short story writer Delmore Schwartz's confessional poem Genesis was published. However, it was in the late 1950s that "confessional poetry" gained full recognition.

According to the Academy of American Poets, confessional poetry is "Poetry of the personal or 'I'." It means that all the poems of this school are written from the first-person point of view. This allows readers to identify emotionally with the speaker of a poem.

**Intimate and Personal Subject** can be the most defining characteristic of confessional poetry. The earlier norm of impersonal poetry dealing with social issues was broken by this type of poetry. It dealt with taboo subjects such as mental illness, sexuality, suicide, alcoholism, depression, drug abuse, gender roles, family life, infidelity, etc. Things that are deemed as embarrassing and shameful were discussed frankly in confessional poetry. Confessional poems always have an autobiographical touch to them.

The other feature of confessional poetry is the **Lyrical Workmanship**. Rather than just pouring your heart out on a paper, confessional poets maintained a level of craftsmanship. They paid attention to rhyme and prosody in their poems. Use of allusions, metaphors, etc., can be seen in such kind of poetry.

When focusing on Kamala Das's poems, Kamala Das is considered the most significant and controversial writer in post-colonial Indian English poetry. She is very frank and open when exposing her personal experiences as a confessional poetess in her poetry. Nevertheless, she depicts them universal through her poetic knack. Her poetry mirrors modern woman's strong yearning for freedom. Being a confessional poet, she unveils even the bedroom secrets going beyond the Indian norms and ethics. She reveals her dissatisfaction in life through many of her poems.

As far as her poem 'An Introduction' is concerned, it is really a confessional poem. She refused the advices and protests of the critics and the friends who asked her not to write in English because it is a foreign language. But she highly adheres to English Language and confirms her strong attachment as follows.

> "The language I speak, Becomes mine, its distortions, its queernesses All mine, mine alone. It is half English, half Indian, funny perhaps, but it is honest, It is as human as I am human, don't You see?"

Thus one of the themes brought out in the poem is the use of language to express humanity and universality without being over extreme, bias, racial and patriotic. Humanity surfaces all such petty and narrow minded attitudes and opinions. On the other hand, she points out that a language should not be considered something rigid and a heritage of a nation or a person. It can be moulded to make the communication easy and delicate. In that sense Kamala Das created one variety of English as Indian English 'half English, half Indian.'

Kamala Das also expresses her dissatisfaction in her marital life. When most of the women hide their suffering due to the fear of patriarchy, she divulges all her personal matters related to her love, sex and marriage being the voice of Indian women who become victims of culture. Accordingly, she conveys her protest and disgust towards child-marriages deprived of the pleasure of teenage period and longing for romantic type of love rather than hard and harsh sex of a mature husband. 

Later she reveals and justifies her extra-marital relationship changing even her religion.

> "I met a man, loved him. Call Him not by any name, he is every man Who wants, a woman, just as I am every Woman who seeks love"

Kamala Das generalizes male-ego or the male identity and discloses what men seek mainly from women. It is quenching their lust. At the same time women expect love. Kamala Das's attitude towards love and sex is based on her experiences with men and therefore, she had a good understanding about both love and sex from her marriage at the age of sixteen. Consequently, the poetess has been able to bring out the theme of male sex verses female love in this poem.

Another theme brought out in the poem is **non-conformism verses conformism**. Who is a conformist?

A conformist is a person who behaves in accordance with prevailing standards or customs and typically dislikes or avoids unconventional behavior. On the contrary, non-conformist is a person who does not conform to prevailing ideas or practices in their behaviour or views. In that sense, it is quite obvious that Kamala Das used to go against the stereotype role of a Hindu-woman. In other words, she did like to abide by the assigned conventional feminine role.

> "Then ... I wore a shirt and my Brother's trousers, cut my hair short and ignored My womanliness."

Then the conformists who are in favour of patriarchal norms and practices could be seen coming forward to protesting and criticizing her tom-boyish look and behavior.

> "Dress in sarees, be girl  
> Be wife, they said. Be embroiderer, be cook,  
> Be a quarreler with servants. Fit in. Oh,  
> Belong, cried the categorizers. Don't sit  
> On walls or peep in through our lace-draped windows."

Thus, Kamala Das's non-conformist and unconventional traits can be identified and detected not only from the yearning to get rid of her feminine look but also from the other matters related to her literary work, love, marriage and so on.

**Searching for Identity** is another theme in the poem. Identity has many aspects and dimensions - it is a personal, socio-cultural, national, sexual, spiritual and creative construct. When self is hampered in its fulfillment through various roles, it generates an identity crisis. This crisis brings alienation and it further aggravates the crisis. Kamala Das had to search for her identity in a male-dominated society where women are marginalized from socio-economic, political, religious fields and denied the conjugal and domestic rights. Therefore, she can be considered as a rebel against the male dominated society as it voices for the liberation of women's self and tries to create their own identity in such a society. At the end of the poem, she realizes that there is no much difference between male identity and female identity because both have positive and negative qualities.

> ...I am sinner, I am saint. I am the beloved and the Betrayed..

Thus, she argues and proves the fact that even female-ego is not inferior to the such a so-called supreme male-ego.

## Poetic Techniques

### Form / Structure
* **Irregular length of lines / Unrhymed lines.**

### Rhythm
There is a control over rhythm. The poetess displays a strong feeling for rhythm although the poem is non-metrical. Specially, conversational speech creates rhythm.

### Narrative Form
The poetess has employed first person narrative form. She seems to be addressing an imagined audience. Therefore, we get the features of a dramatic monologue. 
> "It is as human as I am human, don't  
> You see? It voices my joys, my longings, my" 

This also allows readers to identify emotionally with the speaker of the poem.

### Language
Colloquial lines and run-on-lines [enjambment] create natural speech rhythm:
> "I am Indian, very brown, born in Malabar,"  
> "Not your mother-tongue. Why not leave Me alone, critics, friends, visiting cousins,"  
> "It is half English, half Indian, funny perhaps, but it is honest,"

Rhetorical questions and imperatives help the speaker emphasize the facts and issues forwarded by her:
> "Why not leave Me alone, critics, friends, visiting cousins, Every one of you? Why not let me speak in Any language I like?"  
> "Dress in sarees, be girl Be wife, they said. Be embroiderer, be cook, Be a quarreller with servants. Fit in. Oh, Belong, cried the categorizers. Don't sit On walls or peep in through our lace-draped windows."

The poet uses very long sentences divided by several commas. They suggest the speaker's mental confusion, hesitation and disturbance:
> "Woman who seeks love. In him... the hungry haste Of rivers, in me. the oceans' tireless"  
> "It is half English, half Indian, funny perhaps, but it is honest,"

The poet has used many "ing" verbs in the poem. They are "cowing", "roaring", "muttering", "blazing", "knowing", "dying" etc.

Repetition of words is one of the stylistic devices employed in the poem. The frequent repetition of words, lines and even sections of the poem help the poetess emphasize an idea or an emotion. The repetition of "be" and "don't" shows the restrictions imposed on the poetess by the patriarchal society.
> "Be Amy, or be Kamala. Or, better Still, be Madhavikutty. It is time to Choose a name, a role. Don't play pretending games. Don't play at schizophrenia or be a Nympho. Don't cry embarrassingly loud when"

### Sound Devices

* **Alliteration:**
  * `But my sad woman - body felt so beaten.` 
  * `hungry haste` 
* **Onomatopoeia:**
  * `The weight of my breasts and womb crushed me` 
  * `Swelled and one or two places sprouted hair.`

### Metaphors
The lover of the poetess is compared to the 'hungry rivers' and the poetess herself is compared to the 'tireless river oceans'.
> "of river, in me... the oceans tireless waiting..."

### Simile
The simile 'sword in its sheath' expresses man's inner nature of personality which is hidden and it seldom comes out in words and deeds.
> "In this world, he is tightly packed like the Sword in its sheath"

### Symbolism
* The line `"The weight of my breasts and womb crushed me."` symbolizes sexual oppression as well as the signs of pregnancy.
* Wearing shirts, brother's trousers and having short hair symbolize masculinity whereas wearing sarees, embroidering and cooking symbolize femininity.
  > "Then ... I wore a shirt and my Brother's trousers, cut my hair short and ignored My womanliness. Dress in sarees, be girl Be wife, they said. Be embroiderer, be cook"
* In the line `"Bedroom and closed the door, He did not beat me"`, bedroom symbolizes copulation and closing the door symbolizes losing freedom of the speaker.
* > "Don't play at schizophrenia or be a Nympho. Don't cry embarrassingly loud when"

  In this extract, the word 'schizophrenia' suggests mental disorder and abnormal social behavior. The word 'Nympho' implies the strong sexual desire of a girl.
* The line `"Drinks at twelve, midnight, in hotels of strange towns,"` also symbolizes the male behaviour.
* > "Belong, cried the categorizers. Don't sit On walls or peep in through our lace-draped windows"

  In this extract, "categorizers" symbolize conservatives or those who are strongly in favour of male chauvinism.

### Paradox / Antithesis
> "a rattle in my throat. I am sinner, I am saint. I am the beloved and the Betrayed. I have no joys that are not yours, no"

### Imagery
* **Auditory Imagery:**
  * `Hopes, and it is useful to me as cawing,`
  * `Is to crows or roaring to the lions,`
* **Visual Imagery:**
  * `Swelled and one or two places sprouted hair,` (Visual Imagery for a girl's physical changes because of attaining puberty)
  * `"Bedroom and closed the door, He did not beat me But my sad woman - body felt so beaten. The weight of my breasts and womb crushed me."` (Visual Imagery for copulation and woman's pregnancy)
