# Ode to a Nightingale
**By John Keats**

*[1795 - 1821 / Romantic Period]*

---

## Poem Text

### Stanza 1

> My heart aches, and a drowsy numbness pains  
> My sense, as though of hemlock I had drunk,  
> Or emptied some dull opiate to the drains  
> One minute past, and Lethe-wards had sunk:  
> 'Tis not through envy of thy happy lot,  
> But being too happy in thine happiness, —  
> That thou, light-winged Dryad of the trees  
> In some melodious plot  
> Of beechen green, and shadows numberless,  
> Singest of summer in full-throated ease.

### Stanza 2

> O, for a draught of vintage! that hath been  
> Cool'd a long age in the deep-delved earth,  
> Tasting of Flora and the country green,  
> Dance, and Provençal song, and sunburnt mirth!  
> O for a beaker full of the warm South,  
> Full of the true, the blushful Hippocrene,  
> With beaded bubbles winking at the brim,  
> And purple-stained mouth;  
> That I might drink, and leave the world unseen,  
> And with thee fade away into the forest dim:

### Stanza 3

> Fade far away, dissolve, and quite forget  
> What thou among the leaves hast never known,  
> The weariness, the fever, and the fret  
> Here, where men sit and hear each other groan;  
> Where palsy shakes a few, sad, last gray hairs,  
> Where youth grows pale, and spectre-thin, and dies;  
> Where but to think is to be full of sorrow  
> And leaden-eyed despairs,  
> Where Beauty cannot keep her lustrous eyes,  
> Or new Love pine at them beyond to-morrow.

### Stanza 4

> Away! away! for I will fly to thee,  
> Not charioted by Bacchus and his pards,  
> But on the viewless wings of Poesy,  
> Though the dull brain perplexes and retards:  
> Already with thee! tender is the night,  
> And haply the Queen-Moon is on her throne,  
> Cluster'd around by all her starry Fays;  
> But here there is no light,  
> Save what from heaven is with the breezes blown  
> Through verdurous glooms and winding mossy ways.

### Stanza 5

> I cannot see what flowers are at my feet,  
> Nor what soft incense hangs upon the boughs,  
> But, in embalmed darkness, guess each sweet  
> Wherewith the seasonable month endows  
> The grass, the thicket, and the fruit-tree wild;  
> White hawthorn, and the pastoral eglantine;  
> Fast fading violets cover'd up in leaves;  
> And mid-May's eldest child,  
> The coming musk-rose, full of dewy wine,  
> The murmurous haunt of flies on summer eves.

### Stanza 6

> Darkling I listen; and, for many a time  
> I have been half in love with easeful Death,  
> Call'd him soft names in many a mused rhyme,  
> To take into the air my quiet breath;  
> Now more than ever seems it rich to die,  
> To cease upon the midnight with no pain,  
> While thou art pouring forth thy soul abroad  
> In such an ecstasy!  
> Still wouldst thou sing, and I have ears in vain —  
> To thy high requiem become a sod.

### Stanza 7

> Thou wast not born for death, immortal Bird!  
> No hungry generations tread thee down;  
> The voice I hear this passing night was heard  
> In ancient days by emperor and clown:  
> Perhaps the self-same song that found a path  
> Through the sad heart of Ruth, when, sick for home,  
> She stood in tears amid the alien corn;  
> The same that oft-times hath  
> Charm'd magic casements, opening on the foam  
> Of perilous seas, in faery lands forlorn.

### Stanza 8

> Forlorn! the very word is like a bell  
> To toll me back from thee to my sole self!  
> Adieu! the fancy cannot cheat so well  
> As she is fam'd to do, deceiving elf.  
> Adieu! adieu! thy plaintive anthem fades  
> Past the near meadows, over the still stream,  
> Up the hill-side; and now 'tis buried deep  
> In the next valley-glades:  
> Was it a vision, or a waking dream?  
> Fled is that music: — Do I wake or sleep?

## Note on the Poet

John Keats was born in Moorgate, London, on 31 October 1795 to Thomas Keats and his wife, Frances Jennings. Although Keats and his family seem to have marked his birthday on 29 October, baptism records give the date as the 31st. He was the eldest of four surviving children; his younger siblings were George (1797–1841), Thomas (1799–1818), and Frances Mary “Fanny” (1803–1889), who eventually married Spanish author Valentín Llanos Gutiérrez. Another son was lost in infancy. His father first worked as a hostler at the stables attached to the Swan and Hoop Inn, an establishment he later managed, and where the growing family lived for some years. Keats believed that he was born at the inn, a birthplace of humble origins, but there is no evidence to support his belief.

His parents were unable to afford Eton or Harrow, so in the summer of 1803 he was sent to board at John Clarke’s school in Enfield, close to his grandparents’ house. The small school had a liberal outlook and a progressive curriculum, more modern than the larger, more prestigious schools. In the family atmosphere at Clarke’s, Keats developed an interest in classics and history, which stayed with him throughout his short life. The headmaster’s son, Charles Cowden Clarke, also became an important mentor and friend, introducing Keats to Renaissance literature, including Tasso, Spenser, and Chapman’s translations. The young Keats was described by his friend Edward Holmes as a volatile character, “always in extremes”, given to indolence and fighting. However, at 13 he began focusing his energy on reading and study, winning his first academic prize in midsummer 1809.

In April 1804, when Keats was eight, his father died. The cause of death was a skull fracture suffered when he fell from his horse while returning from a visit to Keats and his brother George at school. Thomas Keats died intestate. Frances remarried two months later, but left her new husband soon afterwards, and the four children went to live with their grandmother, Alice Jennings, in the village of Edmonton. In March 1810, when Keats was 14, his mother died of tuberculosis, leaving the children in the custody of their grandmother. She appointed two guardians, Richard Abbey and John Sandell, to take care of them. That autumn, Keats left Clarke’s school to apprentice with Thomas Hammond, a surgeon and apothecary who was a neighbour and the doctor of the Jennings family.

Having finished his apprenticeship with Hammond, Keats registered as a medical student at Guy’s Hospital. He lodged near the hospital, at 28 St Thomas’s Street in Southwark, with other medical students. However, Keats’s training took up increasing amounts of his writing time, and he was increasingly ambivalent about his medical career. He felt that he faced a stark choice. He had written his first extant poem, “An Imitation of Spenser”, in 1814, when he was 19. Now, strongly drawn by ambition, inspired by fellow poets such as Leigh Hunt and Lord Byron, and beleaguered by family financial crises, he suffered periods of depression. In 1816, Keats received his apothecary’s licence, which made him eligible to practise as an apothecary, physician, and surgeon, but before the end of the year he announced to his guardian that he was resolved to be a poet, not a surgeon.

Although he continued his work and training at Guy’s, Keats devoted more and more time to the study of literature, experimenting with verse forms, particularly the sonnet. In May 1816, Leigh Hunt agreed to publish the sonnet “O Solitude” in his magazine, *The Examiner*. It was the first appearance in print of Keats’s poetry.

The first volume of Keats’s verse, which included “I stood tiptoe” and “Sleep and Poetry”, both strongly influenced by Hunt, was a decisive turning point for Keats, establishing him in the public eye as a figure in what Hunt termed “a new school of poetry”. At this time Keats wrote to his friend Bailey:

> “I am certain of nothing but the holiness of the Heart’s affections and the truth of the imagination. What imagination seizes as Beauty must be truth.”

This passage would eventually be transmuted into the concluding lines of “Ode on a Grecian Urn”:

> “Beauty is truth, truth beauty, — that is all / Ye know on earth, and all ye need to know.”

In early December 1816, under the heady influence of his artistic friends, Keats told Abbey that he had decided to give up medicine in favour of poetry.

In June 1818, Keats began a walking tour of Scotland, Ireland, and the Lake District with his friend Charles Armitage Brown. Keats’s brother George and his wife Georgina accompanied them as far as Lancaster and then continued to Liverpool, from where the couple emigrated to America. They lived in Ohio and Louisville, Kentucky, until 1841, when George’s investments failed. Like Keats’s other brother, they both died penniless and racked by tuberculosis, for which there was no effective treatment until the next century. In July, while on the Isle of Mull, Keats caught a bad cold and “was too thin and fevered to proceed on the journey”. After his return south in August, Keats continued to nurse Tom, exposing himself to infection. Some biographers suggest that this is when tuberculosis, his “family disease”, first took hold. “Consumption” was not identified as a disease with a single infectious origin until 1820, and there was considerable stigma attached to the condition, as it was often associated with weakness, repressed sexual passion, or masturbation. Keats “refuses to give it a name” in his letters. Tom Keats died on 1 December 1818.

John Keats moved to the newly built Wentworth Place, owned by his friend Charles Armitage Brown. It was on the edge of Hampstead Heath, ten minutes’ walk south of his old home in Well Walk. The winter of 1818–19, though a difficult period for the poet, marked the beginning of his *annus mirabilis* in which he wrote his most mature work. He composed five of his six great odes at Wentworth Place in April and May and, although it is debated in which order they were written, “Ode to Psyche” opened the published series. According to Brown, “Ode to a Nightingale” was composed under a plum tree in the garden. Brown wrote:

> “In the spring of 1819 a nightingale had built her nest near my house. Keats felt a tranquil and continual joy in her song; and one morning he took his chair from the breakfast-table to the grass-plot under a plum-tree, where he sat for two or three hours. When he came into the house, I perceived he had some scraps of paper in his hand, and these he was quietly thrusting behind the books. On inquiry, I found those scraps, four or five in number, contained his poetic feelings on the song of our nightingale.”

“Ode on a Grecian Urn” and “Ode on Melancholy” were inspired by sonnet forms and probably written after “Ode to a Nightingale”. In 1819, Keats wrote “The Eve of St. Agnes”, “La Belle Dame sans Merci”, “Hyperion”, “Lamia”, and a play, *Otho the Great*. The poems “Fancy” and “Bards of passion and of mirth” were inspired by the garden of Wentworth Place. In September, very short of money and in despair, considering taking up journalism or a post as a ship’s surgeon, he approached his publishers with a new book of poems. They were unimpressed with the collection, finding the presented versions of “Lamia” confusing, and describing “St Agnes” as having a “sense of pettish disgust” and “a ‘Don Juan’ style of mingling up sentiment and sneering”, concluding it was “a poem unfit for ladies”. The final volume Keats lived to see, *Lamia, Isabella, The Eve of St. Agnes, and Other Poems*, was eventually published in July 1820. It received greater acclaim than *Endymion* or *Poems*, finding favourable notices in both *The Examiner* and *Edinburgh Review*. It would come to be recognised as one of the most important poetic works ever published.

Keats befriended Isabella Jones in May 1817, while on holiday in the village of Bo Peep, near Hastings. She is described as beautiful, talented and widely read, not of the top flight of society yet financially secure, an enigmatic figure who would become a part of Keats’s circle. Throughout their friendship Keats never hesitates to own his sexual attraction to her, although they seem to enjoy circling each other rather than offering commitment. He writes that he “frequented her rooms” in the winter of 1818–19, and in his letters to George says that he “warmed with her” and “kissed her”. The meetings may have been a sexual initiation for Keats according to Bate and Gittings. Jones inspired and was a steward of Keats’s writing. The themes of “The Eve of St. Agnes” and “The Eve of St Mark” may well have been suggested by her, the lyric “Hush, Hush! [O sweet Isabel]” was about her, and that the first version of “Bright Star” may have originally been for her. In 1821, Jones was one of the first in England to be notified of Keats’s death.

Letters and drafts of poems suggest that Keats first met Frances (Fanny) Brawne between September and November 1818. It is likely that the 18-year-old Brawne visited the Dilke family at Wentworth Place before she lived there. She was born in the hamlet of West End (now in the district of West Hampstead). Her father kept a London inn, and both families lost several members to tuberculosis. She shared her first name with both Keats’s sister and mother, and had a talent for dress-making and languages as well as a natural theatrical bent. During November 1818 she developed an intimacy with Keats, but it was shadowed by the illness of Tom Keats, whom John was nursing through this period.

In one of his many hundreds of notes and letters, Keats wrote to Brawne on 13 October 1819:

> “My love has made me selfish. I cannot exist without you — I am forgetful of every thing but seeing you again — my Life seems to stop there — I see no further. You have absorb’d me. I have a sensation at the present moment as though I was dissolving — I should be exquisitely miserable without the hope of soon seeing you... I have been astonished that Men could die Martyrs for religion — I have shudder’d at it — I shudder no more — I could be martyr’d for my Religion — Love is my religion — I could die for that — I could die for you.”

Tuberculosis took hold and he was advised by his doctors to move to a warmer climate. In September 1820 Keats left for Rome knowing he would probably never see Brawne again. After leaving he felt unable to write to her or read her letters, although he did correspond with her mother. He died there five months later on 23 February 1821 and was buried in the Protestant Cemetery, Rome. His last request was to be placed under a tombstone bearing no name or date, only the words:

> “Here lies One whose Name was writ in Water.”

John Keats was an English Romantic poet. He was one of the main figures of the second generation of Romantic poets, along with Lord Byron and Percy Bysshe Shelley, despite his work’s having been in publication for only four years before his death.

Although his poems were not generally well received by critics during his lifetime, his reputation grew after his death, and by the end of the 19th century, he had become one of the most beloved of all English poets. He had a significant influence on a diverse range of poets and writers. Jorge Luis Borges stated that his first encounter with Keats’s work was the most significant literary experience of his life.

The poetry of Keats is characterised by sensual imagery, most notably in the series of odes. This is typical of Romantic poets, as they aimed to accentuate extreme emotion through the emphasis of natural imagery. Today his poems and letters are some of the most popular and most analysed in English literature.

## Analysis of the Stanzas

### Stanza 1

> My heart aches, and a drowsy numbness pains  
> My sense, as though of hemlock I had drunk,  
> Or emptied some dull opiate to the drains  
> One minute past, and Lethe-wards had sunk:  
> 'Tis not through envy of thy happy lot,  
> But being too happy in thine happiness, —  
> That thou, light-winged Dryad of the trees  
> In some melodious plot  
> Of beechen green, and shadows numberless,  
> Singest of summer in full-throated ease.

**Vocabulary**

- **ache** — suffers from continuous pain
- **drowsy** — sleepy and lethargic
- **numbness** — the state of feeling numb [lack of feeling]
- **hemlock** — a poisonous herb of the carrot family having finely cut leaves and small white flowers. A drug or lethal drink is prepared from the poison hemlock
- **opiate** — a drug derived from or related to opium
- **Lethe** — in Greek mythology, the river of forgetfulness, which flows through Hades. Drinking its water results in loss of memory
- **light-winged** — flying very quickly and lightly
- **Dryad** — in Greek mythology, a forest nymph [minor goddess]
- **beechen green** — green colour of beech trees
- **plot** — piece of land
- **melodious** — mellifluous
- **full-throated ease** — singing lightly, loudly and heartily

At the beginning of the poem, the poet says that his heart hurts as if he has just drunk the poison hemlock, a poisonous herb of the carrot family. It is believed that the Greek philosopher Socrates took the drink of hemlock when he was put to death for corrupting the youth. So hemlock reminds us of death. The poet says he feels drowsy and numb as if he had just drunk poison hemlock.

In the next two lines, the poet says he feels as though he has drunk some powerful drug, an opiate, which is produced from the poppy flower. The opiate causes him to “sink” into a kind of oblivion. In Greek mythology, Lethe was a river in Hades (the Underworld) that made people forget all their memories if they drank from it.

He also wants to express that the pain he feels is not because he is jealous of the bird’s happiness. Instead, he feels excessively happy for the bird’s joy. Why is the nightingale so happy? Because it sits in the trees all day and sings about summer. Now it is obvious that the poet is addressing the nightingale. He compares the bird to a “Dryad of the trees”. In Greek mythology, a Dryad is a nymph [a female spirit] that lives in the trees. The phrase “light-winged” suggests that the nightingale is small so that it can fly quickly and lightly. The bird makes any space or plot it inhabits melodious. At this moment, the bird seems to be on a plot of green beechen trees and shady places. The nightingale continues singing about summer loudly and heartily without much effort. So we can speculate that the poem was written at the peak of summer.

### Stanza 2

> O, for a draught of vintage! that hath been  
> Cool'd a long age in the deep-delved earth,  
> Tasting of Flora and the country green,  
> Dance, and Provençal song, and sunburnt mirth!  
> O for a beaker full of the warm South,  
> Full of the true, the blushful Hippocrene,  
> With beaded bubbles winking at the brim,  
> And purple-stained mouth;  
> That I might drink, and leave the world unseen,  
> And with thee fade away into the forest dim:

**Vocabulary**

- **draught of vintage** — glass [sip / drink] of high-quality wine
- **deep-delved** — deep inside
- **Flora** — plants of a particular region; also the Roman goddess of flowers and spring
- **Provençal song** — a song relating to, denoting, or characteristic of Provence, its inhabitants, their dialect
- **mirth** — merriment / joy
- **blushful** — reddish, like blushing cheeks; having a red face from emotion
- **Hippocrene** — in Greek mythology, a fountain on Mount Helicon, sacred to the Muses and regarded as a source of poetic inspiration
- **beaded bubbles** — bubbles like beads
- **purple-stained** — purple-coloured
- **brim** — edge of a cup, full to the top
- **fade / dissolve** — disappear

At the beginning of this stanza the poet expresses his wish for wine, “a draught of vintage that has been kept cool deep in the earth”. Vintage wine is made from grapes from the same harvest, and people often refer to a particular year at a winery as a “vintage”. We have no explanation at this point for his sudden desire to drink. Perhaps he wishes to take alcohol to escape from his painful reality. Good wine needs to be kept cool. That is why people often store it in their cellars. According to Keats, the earth is like a giant wine cellar.

Flora was the Roman goddess of flowers, fertility and spring. Plants and trees become green in spring. So all these colour and visual images are associated with mirth. Thus, if the poet drinks good-quality wine taken out of the earth, the intoxication he gets will help him appreciate the beautiful environment of the spring season with flora and green plants. Moreover, he will be tempted to dance and sing Provençal songs. “Provençal song” means a song sung in a local language of Provence in France. “Sunburnt mirth” refers to all the warm and festive joy of the south. Again, all these kinesthetic and auditory images associated with dancing and singing bring happiness.

The poet wants to drink wine from a beaker from the warm South of France, or with warm, friendly people of the South. Hippocrene is the “fountain of the Muses”. The Muses were the nine goddesses who inspire all poets. He wants to drink water from the fountain of the Muses to get inspiration to be a great poet. The water from the Hippocrene is “blushful” because it is reddish, the colour of both wine and a blush. The speaker describes the appearance of the wine. It has little bubbles that burst, or “wink”, at the brim of the beaker, like little eyes. It also stains the mouth purple when drunk, like any strong red wine will do.

What do all these expressions about wine, inspiration, and drunkenness have to do with the nightingale? The poet sums up his intentions in the final two lines of the stanza. He wants to get intoxicated by drinking this fine wine so that he can leave the “world” unseen and keep company with the nightingale in the dim forest. Here, the “world” may refer to human society with its work and obligations. Thus the poet’s intention is to drink wine and live with the nightingale that lives apart from the worldly society, with a more carefree mind.

### Stanza 3

> Fade far away, dissolve, and quite forget  
> What thou among the leaves hast never known,  
> The weariness, the fever, and the fret  
> Here, where men sit and hear each other groan;  
> Where palsy shakes a few, sad, last gray hairs,  
> Where youth grows pale, and spectre-thin, and dies;  
> Where but to think is to be full of sorrow  
> And leaden-eyed despairs,  
> Where Beauty cannot keep her lustrous eyes,  
> Or new Love pine at them beyond to-morrow.

**Vocabulary**

- **fade / dissolve** — disappear
- **weariness** — tiredness
- **fret** — worry / anxiety
- **palsy** — paralysis
- **spectre** — ghost
- **lustrous** — bright / shining
- **leaden-eyed** — sad eyes with the dull grey colour and weight of lead
- **pine** — waste away

The poet dreams of “fading” out of the world, or just disappearing in a very quiet way. He wants to forget about the things that the nightingale has never had to worry about, such as the stresses and cares of living in human society. The bird is free of such cares. The poet draws our attention to three depressing things: weariness, sickness and worry. Then the poet creates a visual and an auditory image of people who sit around and listen to each other’s groaning and complaining of suffering.

The poet makes us visualise a person who suffers from paralysis. He loses his few remaining grey hairs and becomes almost bald. Young people grow pale and lean like ghosts and die. This reminds us that everyone and everything is subject to death and decay. The world is a place where any kind of thinking leads to depressing thoughts and worries. There are no thoughts that can ultimately bring joy or peace. As a result, thinking itself brings pain and sorrow. “Lustrous” means shining and beautiful, whereas “leaden” means heavy and metallic with the colour of dull grey. It is an image used to describe the forlorn, melancholic or sorrowful state of the poet. The lustrous eyes which add beauty to a person will be no more due to depressed thoughts. Beauty loved today disappears gradually by tomorrow. The lustre of someone’s eyes is yours today only to become someone else’s tomorrow. Thus new love wastes away with grief within a day. So even an emotion like love doesn’t last forever. It changes and dies. The poet thereby points out the fickleness and transience of love, life and beauty.

Only a year before this poem was written, in 1818, Keats’s brother Tom died of tuberculosis. Keats looked after him until his last day. Therefore, the poet also expresses his own fear of death as a youth suffering from tuberculosis. The last two lines of this stanza are often read as referring to Fanny Brawne, the woman Keats loved.

### Stanza 4

> Away! away! for I will fly to thee,  
> Not charioted by Bacchus and his pards,  
> But on the viewless wings of Poesy,  
> Though the dull brain perplexes and retards:  
> Already with thee! tender is the night,  
> And haply the Queen-Moon is on her throne,  
> Cluster'd around by all her starry Fays;  
> But here there is no light,  
> Save what from heaven is with the breezes blown  
> Through verdurous glooms and winding mossy ways.

**Vocabulary**

- **Bacchus** — Roman god of wine [Greek Dionysus]
- **chariot** — two-wheeled vehicle drawn by animals
- **pards** — [archaic] leopards that drew the chariot of Bacchus
- **poesy** — poetry
- **perplexes** — baffles / puzzles
- **retards** — holds back progress / delays / slows down
- **tender** — kind / gentle
- **cluster'd** — gathered together
- **verdurous gloom** — darkness due to thick green vegetation
- **winding mossy ways** — moss-covered zigzag paths

The poet suddenly cries out, “Away! away! for I will fly to thee.” He turns to fantasy again; he rejects wine in line two, and in line three he announces he is going to use “the viewless wings of Poesy” to join the nightingale. Previously, in the second stanza, the poet wished to fade away into the world of the nightingale on a “draught of vintage”. In other words, he wanted to get drunk and escape from reality. Bacchus is the god of wine, whose chariot is believed to be drawn by pards [leopards].

In choosing poesy, the poet discards the notion of becoming immortal through drugs and alcohol. Just as the bird lives forever through its song, he wants to live forever through his poems. The poet describes his wish to “fly”, or imaginatively transport himself, to the nightingale. The “viewless wings of Poesy” will be the vehicle of his transcendence, allowing the imaginative power of poetry to carry out this task despite the interference of his “dull brain” that “perplexes and retards” him through its tendency toward thought and argument. The poet comes to the realisation that he is, in fact, with the nightingale: “Already with thee!”

In line 5, he succeeds, or seems to succeed, in joining the bird. The poet’s imagination functions in the rest of the stanza. He perceives the night as tender. He also observes the Queen-Moon clustered around by starry Fays. Here *Fays* means fairies. It is obvious that now the poet is in an imaginative world with poetic expressions like “Queen-Moon” and “starry Fays”. We get visual and light imagery and deification as literary devices. The imagery here is associated with darkness and night which is soft and “tender”, and the moon is visible in the sky. The imagery is more fanciful and imaginative here. The moon is compared to a queen surrounded by her attendants, the stars or fairies. This comparison is appropriate because the moon is associated with the goddess Diana in classical mythology. Thus we get the image of the Queen-Moon seated on her throne in the sky, which is supposed to be heaven.

Despite all these sources of light in heaven, there is no light in the nightingale’s world beyond what filters down through the trees, because the nightingale lives in the forest where trees block the light. Thus the moonlight is hidden by the trees, except where it breaks through when the breezes blow the branches. The phrase “verdurous glooms” means the darkness caused by plants getting in the way of the moon. So darkness falls at the foot of the trees in the forest glade where the paths are covered with moss.

### Stanza 5

> I cannot see what flowers are at my feet,  
> Nor what soft incense hangs upon the boughs,  
> But, in embalmed darkness, guess each sweet  
> Wherewith the seasonable month endows  
> The grass, the thicket, and the fruit-tree wild;  
> White hawthorn, and the pastoral eglantine;  
> Fast fading violets cover'd up in leaves;  
> And mid-May's eldest child,  
> The coming musk-rose, full of dewy wine,  
> The murmurous haunt of flies on summer eves.

**Vocabulary**

- **incense** — fragrance
- **boughs** — branches of trees
- **embalmed darkness** — darkness filled with balmy fragrance
- **thicket** — bushes / dense group of trees
- **hawthorn** — thorny shrub of the rose family
- **pastoral** — rural
- **eglantine** — sweet-brier / wild rose with fragrant flowers and leaves
- **violets** — blue or white five-petalled flowers
- **musk-rose** — rambling rose with large white musk-scented flowers
- **dewy wine** — nectar sweet like dew / wine
- **murmurous** — soft low sound

The poet remains in the nightingale’s nighttime world. The speaker can’t see the flowers on the forest floor or the plants that produce that pleasant smell in the trees. He is still groping around in the dark. He has to guess what “sweet” flowers and plants he smells. The darkness is “embalmed”, where “balm” is a sweet-smelling substance like perfume. He’s guessing all kinds of different plants such as grass, thickets, fruit trees, white hawthorn, eglantine, violets and musk-roses. The dew of the musk-rose is intoxicating like the wine he mentioned earlier. At the same time, there is the murmurous sound of flies on summer evenings. In short, he seems to experience both spring and summer at the same time. The poet produces a series of sensuous images. Except for the imagery of sight, the other olfactory, auditory and gustatory images blend together to create the beauty of the seasons and the poet’s imagination.

Nevertheless, it is ironic that there are even death images hidden between the soothing and pleasing imagery in the stanza. Mainly, darkness is associated with death. The poet’s sense of sight is already dead because he cannot see anything in darkness. He perceives everything through smelling and hearing. On the other hand, the word “embalmed” is also associated with death, as dead bodies are embalmed to protect them from decay. Moreover, “fast fading violets” imply that the beauty of violets is disappearing fast. They appear in March, bloom well in April and fade fast in May. Thus the beauty depicted in the night and evening of the spring and summer seasons seems to be an illusion. In other words, the poet’s imagination foreshadows his imminent death as well. Therefore, this stanza juxtaposes both life and death.

> **Note:** *Musk-rose* — rambling rose with large white musk-scented flowers, traditionally blooming in mid-May.

---

### Stanza 6

> Darkling I listen; and, for many a time  
> I have been half in love with easeful Death,  
> Call'd him soft names in many a mused rhyme,  
> To take into the air my quiet breath;  
> Now more than ever seems it rich to die,  
> To cease upon the midnight with no pain,  
> While thou art pouring forth thy soul abroad  
> In such an ecstasy!  
> Still wouldst thou sing, and I have ears in vain —  
> To thy high requiem become a sod.

**Vocabulary**

- **darkling** — in darkness
- **easeful Death** — easy death without much pain
- **mused rhyme** — inspired poetry
- **ecstasy** — extreme delight / joy
- **requiem** — a Mass for the repose of the souls of the dead
- **sod** — lump of earth / grass and the surface layer of earth [symbol of graveyard]

At the end of stanza five, the poet moves from olfactory imagery to the auditory imagery of the murmurous haunt of flies. Now he continues the auditory image, saying that he is listening in the darkness. The experience of being alone in the dark seems related to the experience of death. Death would be another way to free himself of all his worldly cares and suffering. The speaker whispers sweet nothings to Death and writes rhymes thinking of death. It’s true that Keats was obsessed with the idea of death, and he often wrote about it. He wants Death to take the air from his lungs, or that the air should take his breath along with his verses. While in the world of the nightingale, he thinks it would be “rich to die”. Many people are afraid that death will be empty, but richness is associated with an abundance of good things, which is almost the opposite of emptiness.

He would like to die quietly in the middle of the night without pain while listening to the nightingale’s singing. There is a similarity between the nightingale and the poet. The nightingale is like a poet, sending its voice into the air just as Keats sends his rhyme into the air. The bird’s music expresses its “soul”. So the bird is completely lost in the moment of pure joy and “ecstasy”. He imagines what would happen after the moment of his death. Basically, the bird would keep singing as if nothing had happened. Even though he died, he would still have ears. But the ears would be useless, like a lump of clay on the earth, because there is no brain to process the sounds. The poet imagines that the bird would then be singing a “high requiem”, a kind of church Mass with music sung for a dead person.

### Stanza 7

> Thou wast not born for death, immortal Bird!  
> No hungry generations tread thee down;  
> The voice I hear this passing night was heard  
> In ancient days by emperor and clown:  
> Perhaps the self-same song that found a path  
> Through the sad heart of Ruth, when, sick for home,  
> She stood in tears amid the alien corn;  
> The same that oft-times hath  
> Charm'd magic casements, opening on the foam  
> Of perilous seas, in faery lands forlorn.

**Vocabulary**

- **immortal** — eternal
- **hungry generations** — envious / consuming generations
- **tread** — trample
- **Ruth** — a woman in the Old Testament, a Moabite widow devoted to her mother-in-law Naomi
- **alien corn** — foreign / remote cornfield
- **casement** — a window attached to its frame by one or more hinges
- **charm'd** — influenced by incantation / enchanted
- **perilous** — dangerous
- **forlorn** — isolated / sad

In this stanza, the poet seems to be listening to the singing of the nightingale. He tells the bird that it is immortal and that envious generations cannot suppress its voice. The voice or singing of the nightingale he hears on this passing night has been heard by many people, such as emperors, clowns and even homesick Ruth.

What does Keats really mean by “immortal Bird”? Members of the nightingale’s species have undoubtedly been singing the same notes for ages, perhaps ever since the species first existed thousands of years ago. That is what leads Keats to think back in time as far as he is able to travel in his imagination, drawing mainly upon his reading. This takes him back to ancient times, older than the Bible, or to times that exist only in fairy tales, which often begin with the words “Once upon a time”. The nightingale singing to the poet as he composes his ode is thus the same nightingale who sang to Ruth in the Old Testament. But this is not literal truth; it is a poetic conceit.

Who is Ruth? Ruth is a woman who appears in the Old Testament. A Moabite, she married an Israelite. When her husband died, Ruth did not leave her husband’s people. She decided to stay with her mother-in-law, Naomi, out of devotion. She gleaned in foreign cornfields in Judah. She would have felt homesick and shed tears standing amid the cornfield. The poet imagines that the same song of the nightingale he hears would have been heard by homesick Ruth while gleaning corn, and that it would have touched her melancholic heart and caused weeping amid the alien corn.

The poet shifts from the Biblical allusion to fantasy. He thinks that the nightingale’s song also has magic or mystic power. Accordingly, the same song heard by him had very often charmed magic casements facing the dangerous sea in isolated fairy lands.

### Stanza 8

> Forlorn! the very word is like a bell  
> To toll me back from thee to my sole self!  
> Adieu! the fancy cannot cheat so well  
> As she is fam'd to do, deceiving elf.  
> Adieu! adieu! thy plaintive anthem fades  
> Past the near meadows, over the still stream,  
> Up the hill-side; and now 'tis buried deep  
> In the next valley-glades:  
> Was it a vision, or a waking dream?  
> Fled is that music: — Do I wake or sleep?

**Vocabulary**

- **elf** — a supernatural creature of folk tales
- **toll** — ring a bell [especially a funeral bell]
- **sole self** — his self / own mind
- **deceiving** — cheating / misleading
- **adieu** — goodbye / farewell

“Forlorn” is the most significant word at the beginning of this stanza. It means lonely, and it is like a bell ringing to remind the poet to go back to his own world [sole self], away from the imaginative world of the nightingale. The word “toll” is used for tolling funeral bells. It suggests that the poet’s imagination and attachment to the nightingale is now dead. He bids goodbye to the bird and then lashes out at his imagination for being a “deceiving elf”. The poet says that fancy cannot cheat him any further, though she is famous for deceiving. It means that the poet had been deceived previously by the song of the nightingale. At the beginning the poet’s intention was to escape from the everyday world. He thereby expected to get rid of human suffering such as weariness, fever and fret. But he has not been successful in his attempt.

Wordsworth believed that the company of Nature gives joy to the human heart and he looked upon Nature as exercising a healing influence on sorrow-stricken hearts, like balm to weary souls. Romantic poets like Wordsworth usually glorify Nature in their poems. Being a Romantic poet, Keats does not like to be too fanciful by forgetting reality. He personally feels that even Nature has its boundaries, and that human imagination has no power to exceed reality. So there is a certain point of imagination at which one has to stop and return to the starting point. It is just like you cannot live in a dream world for a long time. Thus the poet returns to the world of reality from the imagined world of the nightingale. Fancy or imagination cannot hold the poet in thrall permanently, and ultimately the poet is released from the spell of poetic imagination.

After returning to his senses, the poet finds the nightingale’s song fading away. The poet’s bidding adieu to the nightingale marks a return to the world of reality. He bids goodbye twice more to the nightingale using the French word “adieu”, which means goodbye for a long time. The bird’s sad or “plaintive” song grows harder to hear, as the bird flies from the nearby meadows, across a stream, up a hill, and into the next valley. “Fled is that music” suggests that now he can’t hear it at all. The poet’s journey into imagination and fantasy ends and he is left almost confused, wondering whether he received a vision from the nightingale’s song or whether it was indeed his imagination. He further wonders, “Do I wake or sleep?” Generally the question has a deep meaning about the nature of human life. Are we really awake and conscious of the reality of life, or asleep, being semi-conscious and numb to the true nature of life? Personally, the question at the end of the poem reflects Keats’s uncertainty and perhaps insecurities in life.

## Themes

### 1. Melancholy, Pessimism and Frustration of Human Life

“Ode to a Nightingale” reflects the richness and maturity of Keats’s poetic excellence. This poem is considered a masterpiece of Romantic poetry. The poet draws our attention to several aspects of nature, humanity, love, beauty, death and so on. One of the key points highlighted in the poem is the melancholy, pessimism and frustration of human beings.

At the beginning of the poem, the poet says how his heart is painful and how he feels sleepy, losing his power of movement. He further says that his senses are lost as if he has drunk hemlock or taken an opiate:

> My heart aches, and a drowsy numbness pains  
> My sense, as though of hemlock I had drunk

Thus he does not show any active behaviour or optimistic mood towards life until he hears the happy songs sung by the nightingale. It is obvious that he derives happiness from another’s happiness, but not from something inspired within him:

> 'Tis not through envy of thy happy lot  
> But being too happy in thine happiness

He further expresses how the world is full of weariness, fever and fret. Accordingly, this is a world where people sit and listen to each other’s mental or physical suffering. Fatal illness like palsy causes aged people to lose their few remaining grey hairs. Here, palsy may be the result of man’s excessive stress, depression and weariness, which lead even to premature ageing:

> The weariness, the fever, and the fret  
> Here, where men sit and hear each other groan;  
> Where palsy shakes a few, sad, last gray hairs

Moreover, even young people fall prey to fatal diseases and lose the fun and joy of youth. They grow pale and thin and get the look of ghosts. Thus, they lose their young attractive look and die untimely:

> Where youth grows pale, and spectre-thin, and dies,

When people think of their problems too much, they become so melancholic and sorrowful that their eyes get the dull grey colour of lead and the weight of despair.

The poet then expresses the sense of the tragedy of life and the sadness resulting therefrom. He refers to the weariness, fever, and fret of human life. This is a world where men sit and hear each other groan. Palsy shakes the few last hairs of aged people. Young people fall prey to fatal diseases like tuberculosis. They merely think and become sorrowful. We cannot keep our love and beauty forever. The poet’s mood of ecstasy at the beginning turns to a mood of deep pessimism and despair. Even beautiful women cannot maintain the lustre of their eyes. Perhaps love and beauty go hand in hand. When young lovers fail to preserve their bewitching look with lustrous eyes, it may negatively affect love affairs. Budding love affairs thereby end very soon with grief within a day:

> Where Beauty cannot keep her lustrous eyes,  
> Or new Love pine at them beyond to-morrow.

The poet further points out how his brain is baffled and slow, without enough intellectual capacity for logical reasoning and understanding:

> Though the dull brain perplexes and retards

The poet’s pessimism comes to a climax when he gives up his love for life and thinks of death. As Keats suffered a lot during his short life due to tuberculosis, he expects a painless death to get rid of his melancholy and frustration caused by his own illness, his brother’s death in his arms, and the failure of his love:

> Darkling I listen; and for many a time  
> I have been half in love with easeful Death

When a person gets frustrated over and over, he does not think of any other alternative but only embracing death. He was therefore weighed down by a profound sense of the tragedy of life:

> To cease upon the midnight with no pain

Thus the poet’s poignant expression of melancholy, pessimism and frustration is conveyed throughout the poem with Romantic flavour.

### 2. Fantasy vs. Reality

Reality is the state of things as they actually exist, rather than as they may appear or might be imagined. In a wider definition, reality includes everything that is and has been, whether or not it is observable or comprehensible. A still broader definition includes everything that has existed, exists, or will exist. Fantasy, on the other hand, is not constrained by facts and theories about the way things work, but rather by the imagination of an individual.

The theme of fantasy or dreaming versus reality is one of the key themes in “Ode to a Nightingale”.

Keats was suffering from tuberculosis, which was a fatal disease in his time as there was no permanent remedy. Even his brother Tom died of the same disease. Being a medical student, Keats had a thorough awareness of the gravity of the disease. Therefore, we have no doubt that he would have lived anticipating imminent death. What he did not know was only the date and time of death. It is also not a secret for his other family members, relations, friends and loved ones. Thus it is the reality he faced and expressed in the poem. He intended to escape from this stark reality. The only alternative for his escapism was remaining in fantasy as much as possible. The poem really portrays his endeavour of escaping from reality into fantasy.

The poem opens with Keats expressing pain and his desire to numb himself to everything around him. He feels as if he has drunk something poisonous that has drugged his senses:

> My heart aches, and a drowsy numbness pains  
> My sense, as though of hemlock I had drunk,  
> Or emptied some dull opiate to the drains  
> One minute past, and Lethe-wards had sunk:

He continues to convey a depressing view of life, which tempts his desire to escape. He wants to become like the nightingale, hidden from the harsh light of reality and existing only in forests of darkness, singing in a realm free from life’s suffering. The poet tries to escape into the world of fantasy in several ways. One way is drinking good-quality wine, cooled and matured for a long time in underground cellars. Thus the intoxication of wine carries him to a fully imaginative and fantastic world full of dancing, music and joy:

> Tasting of Flora and the country green,  
> Dance, and Provençal song, and sunburnt mirth

As we know, when a person is under the influence of liquor, he loses his normal consciousness and gains oblivion. In the poem the Flora festival is a fine occasion for the poet to have fun and joy by drinking, singing and dancing. The poet also thinks that he can be much more creative and imaginative by drinking wine. Even in the modern world there are some people who get addicted to drugs and different kinds of liquor so as to improve their literary and aesthetic skills and self-confidence. There are some who think that liquor is the only remedy for their agonies. In the poem the nightingale symbolises imagination and happiness. That is why the poet intends to enter the nightingale’s world after having a glass full of wine:

> That I might drink, and leave the world unseen,  
> And with thee fade away into the forest dim

The poet shifts from fantasy to reality in the third stanza, dealing with human suffering caused by fatal diseases, ageing, losing good looks, breaking love affairs, etc. All this physical and mental suffering reminds the poet that wine is not the remedy to escape from reality and keep company with the nightingale. He cries loudly and asks for the chariot of Bacchus to be taken away. Bacchus is the god of wine and his chariot is drawn by pards. Poetically, the poet would have gone to the imaginative world of the nightingale accompanied by Bacchus in his chariot. Now he is not ready to enter his fantasy under the influence of liquor:

> Away! away! for I will fly to thee,  
> Not charioted by Bacchus and his pards

Instead of wine, he chooses “the viewless wings of Poesy” to escape into the new world of fantasy from reality. This new world of fantasy is more poetic and romantic, and it is depicted with a lot of imagery associated with the colourful sights and lights of heaven:

> And haply the Queen-Moon is on her throne,  
> Cluster'd around by all her starry Fays

Moreover, he gets more sensuous pleasure through his imagination of the fragrance of a number of flowers such as hawthorn, pastoral eglantine, violets, musk-rose, etc. And also he admires the murmur of the flies flying around on summer evenings. Nevertheless, the darkness around him is an impediment to satisfying his sense of sight.

Again he comes back to reality, anticipating his death. Being a victim of consumption, it is quite obvious that the reality he has to face is death. But he dwells on fantasy, longing for easeful death without much pain and sorrow. Death is personified and called dearly for pity and sympathy:

> To cease upon the midnight with no pain,  
> While thou art pouring forth thy soul abroad  
> In such an ecstasy!

When he says “I have been half in love...”, it is clear that he oscillates between fantasy and reality. There are more imaginings and anticipations in relation to the nightingale’s requiem for his dead body, Ruth’s homesickness, charmed magic casements and so on.

It is ironic that at the end of the poem, Keats bids goodbye to his fantasy and comes back to reality. He lets the bird fly away and hears its plaintive anthem fading past the meadows, streams, hills and valleys. Now he is not going to escape from reality via wine, poesy or easeful death. He is prepared to face life as it is and welcome death when the time comes.

At the end of the poem, there is a transition from fantasy to reality. Therefore, the poet remains in a dazed and confused state:

> Was it a vision, or a waking dream?  
> Fled is that music: — Do I wake or sleep?

### 3. Immortality vs. Mortality

As far as nature is concerned, the universal truth is that everything and everyone is subject to death and decay. In other words, all living and non-living things are impermanent. Therefore, we have to accept the phenomenon of mutability and mortality. Nevertheless, Keats goes against this established truth in “Ode to a Nightingale”.

When describing environment, Keats emphasises fertility and growth of plants, trees and flowers. On the surface, we feel that even they have to face death. For instance, in the fifth stanza, the poet says “Fast fading violets cover’d up in leaves”. The violets start blooming in late winter or early spring and fade fast in May when spring ends. It seems to be the death of the violets. But it is not really death. It is only a cyclic change of the seasons. Consequently, the world of nature can appear fresh and immortal.

Even the nightingale is immortalised by the poet in stanza seven:

> Thou wast not born for death, immortal Bird!

It suggests the bird’s immortality through its song, which seems to be its soul and spirit. Its song is heard not only by the poet but also by emperors and clowns who lived in the past. Even Ruth heard the same song while gleaning corn in the alien field:

> In ancient days by emperor and clown;  
> Perhaps the self-same song that found a path  
> Through the sad heart of Ruth, when sick for home

Thus the nightingale experiences a sort of death superficially, but actually it is not real death. The nightingale is mysterious and even disappears at the end of the poem. So the nightingale itself is a symbol of continuity or immortality.

What about human beings? Are they mortal or immortal? The answer can be found in the third stanza. Humans have no power to fight against fatal diseases like palsy. They show deadly symptoms like fever, losing hair, leaden eyes, ageing, and ultimately embrace death, either timely in old age or untimely, like Keats and his brother at a young age:

> Where palsy shakes a few, sad, last gray hairs,  
> Where youth grows pale, and spectre-thin,

Even in the modern world of the twenty-first century, man has not been able to prevent some deadly diseases completely, in spite of the advancement of medical science. On the other hand, man does not have any chance to be immortal even under the cyclic change of flowers. We do not wish to adhere to religious views, as that is outside the discussion of the poem.

Would you think even Keats is also subject to death and decay? That is true. The poet is always conscious of his death and he tried to escape to the world of poetic imagination symbolised by the nightingale. Keats himself described this theory as “Negative Capability”, which means living in uncertainties, mysteries and doubts without reaching after fact and reason. In his poetry, Keats proposed the contemplation of beauty as a way of delaying the inevitability of death. Although we must die eventually, we can choose to spend our time alive in aesthetic revelry, looking at beautiful objects and landscapes. Unlike mortal beings, beautiful things will never die but will keep displaying their beauty for all time.

Does this idea look ambiguous or rather paradoxical? If so, let’s pay attention to the poem “Ode on a Grecian Urn”. In that poem the speaker is envious of the immortality of the lute players and trees depicted on the ancient vessel because they will never cease playing their songs, nor will they ever shed their leaves:

> Ah, happy, happy boughs! that cannot shed  
> Your leaves, nor ever bid the Spring adieu;  
> And, happy melodist, unwearied,  
> For ever piping songs for ever new;

He reassures young lovers by telling them that even though they never catch their mistresses, these women will always stay beautiful:

> She cannot fade, though thou hast not thy bliss,  
> For ever wilt thou love, and she be fair!

They will remain permanently as depicted, while the speaker is subject to change by growing old and embracing death. In the same way, “Ode to a Nightingale” uses the bird’s music to contrast the mortality of humans with the immortality of art. Caught up in beautiful birdsong, the poet imagines himself capable of using poetry to join the bird in the forest. The beauty of the bird’s music represents the ecstatic, imaginative possibilities of poetry. As mortal beings who eventually die, we can delay death through the timelessness of music, poetry, and other types of art. In that sense, Keats’s poetry makes him immortal. He will be with us forever through the work of his imagination, as he himself emphasised in his theory of Negative Capability.

William Shakespeare also brings immortality to his fair young man in Sonnet 18, “Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day?”, through his poetry:

> Nor shall Death brag thou wander’st in his shade,  
> When in eternal lines to Time thou grow’st.  
> So long as men can breathe, or eyes can see,  
> So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.

### 4. Beauty and Glory of Nature

Keats lived a short time between the 18th and the 19th century (born in 1795 and died in 1821). His poems were based on his conception of Beauty and Nature. He is considered to have been of great importance in his time since, by exalting Beauty, he grew as a source of inspiration to many English 19th-century poets, becoming the idol of such writers as Tennyson, Rossetti and the Pre-Raphaelites, as well as Oscar Wilde and many other aesthetes.

Keats’s life was imbued with family tragedies, because both his father and his brother Tom died. He had to face financial problems and professional and personal setbacks. He was unable to marry Fanny Brawne because of his ill health.

Poetry, he thought, should spring naturally from his inner soul and should reproduce what his Imagination suggested to him, and what struck his Imagination most was Beauty — not the “intellectual beauty” of Shelley, but the beauty which reveals itself to his senses. Beauty, in fact, became the central theme of all Keats’s poems, since it was the only consolation he found in life. The memory of something beautiful brought him joy, as he wrote in the opening lines of *Endymion*: “A thing of beauty is a joy for ever”. Beauty could be either physical, like women, nature, statues and paintings, or spiritual, like friendship, love and poetry.

Nature was one of the greatest sources of inspiration for Keats. Like Wordsworth he was highly devoted to nature, though unlike him he did not see an immanent God in it. He simply saw another form of Beauty, which he could transform into poetry without the aid of memory; he only enriched it with his Imagination. While Wordsworth thought that “sweet melodies are made sweeter by distance in time”, Keats believed that “heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard are sweeter”. While Wordsworth’s love for nature is well explained by the fact that he grew up in the Lake District, thus being influenced by its suggestive landscape, it is harder to understand the connection between Keats and nature, since he was a city boy. For this reason, unlike Wordsworth, whose relationship with nature was spiritual, Keats looked at nature with the eye of the aesthete, recreating the physical world, including all living things.

Nature was a major theme among the Romantics, but Keats turned natural objects into poetic images. When he already knew that he was going to die, he looked back at childhood and realised that concrete contact with natural objects at that time was responsible for the positive associations they continued to communicate in adulthood.

Nature led Keats to the formulation of a concept he called “negative capability”, described as the ability to experience “uncertainties, mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact or reason”, managing to negate personality and open oneself to the reality around. It is an intuitive activity of mind, a metaphysical process in which nature is a potential source of truth. The poet’s activity is visionary; it uses natural objects as means to represent the poet’s ideas. A great number of images connected with nature in Keats’s poems are used only to represent experiences, thus becoming symbols of the psyche.

In the beginning the bird is presented as a real bird, but as the poem progresses, the bird becomes a symbol. The bird may symbolise more than one thing, but mainly it symbolises music as one aspect of the beauty of nature. Therefore, the bird is not alienated from Nature, but wholly merged in Nature. Keats loves listening to the song of the nightingale and being intoxicated by the bird’s happiness. He feels like one who has taken a benumbing poison or a dulling drug. This effect is produced on him by the happy song of the nightingale, singing in a joyous, glorious voice among the green beech trees, and called by the poet a light-winged nymph of the trees.

The poem is one of the finest examples of Keats’s pictorial quality and his rich sensuousness. We have an abundance of rich, concrete, and sensuous imagery. The lines in which the poet expresses a passionate desire for some Provençal wine or the red wine from the fountain of the Muses have a rich appeal:

> O for a draught of vintage, that hath been  
> Cool'd a long age in the deep-delved earth,  
> Tasting of Flora and the country green,  
> Dance, and Provençal song, and sunburnt mirth!

These lines bring before us a delightful picture of Provence with its fun and frolic, jollity, merry-making, drinking and dancing. Similarly, the beaker full of the sparkling, blushful Hippocrene is highly pleasing.

Then there is the divine picture of the moon shining in the sky like heaven and surrounded by stars, looking like a queen surrounded by her attendant fairies:

> And haply the Queen-Moon is on her throne,  
> Cluster'd around by all her starry Fays.

The rich feast of flowers in the next stanza is one of the outstanding beauties of the poem. Flowers, soft incense, fruit trees, white hawthorn, eglantine, fast-fading violets, the coming musk-rose full of sweet juice — all this is a delight for our senses.

## Poetic Techniques

### Form

Like most of the other odes, “Ode to a Nightingale” is written in ten-line stanzas. However, unlike most of the other poems, it is metrically variable — though not so much as “Ode to Psyche”. The first seven and last two lines of each stanza are written in iambic pentameter; the eighth line of each stanza is written in trimeter, with only three accented syllables instead of five.

The lines 1–5 of the first stanza demonstrate iambic pentameter:

> .......1...........2...........3...........4...........5  
> My HEART | aches, AND | a DROW | sy NUMB | ness PAINS  
> .......1...........2...........3...........4...........5  
> My SENSE | as THOUGH | of HEM | lock I | had DRUNK,  
> .......1...........2...........3...........4...........5  
> Or EMP | tied SOME | dull OP | iate TO | the DRAINS  
> .......1...........2...........3...........4...........5  
> One MIN | ute PAST, | and LETH | e-WARDS | had SUNK:  
> .......1...........2...........3...........4...........5  
> 'Tis NOT | through EN | vy OF | thy HAP | py LOT

However, the eighth line of each stanza is in iambic trimeter, as the following lines demonstrate:

> .......1...........2...........3  
> In SOME | mel O | dious PLOT *(line 8 — read “-dious” as a single syllable)*  
> .......1...........2...........3  
> And PUR | ple-STAIN | ed MOUTH *(line 18)*  
> .......1...........2...........3  
> And LEAD | en-EYED | des PAIRS *(line 28)*

### Rhyme Scheme

“Nightingale” also differs from the other odes in that its rhyme scheme is the same in every stanza. Each stanza in “Nightingale” is rhymed ABABCDECDE, Keats’s most basic scheme throughout the odes. The first stanza demonstrates the pattern:

> My heart aches, and a drowsy numbness pains — **A**  
> My sense, as though of hemlock I had drunk, — **B**  
> Or emptied some dull opiate to the drains — **A**  
> One minute past, and Lethe-wards had sunk — **B**  
> 'Tis not through envy of thy happy lot, — **C**  
> But being too happy in thine happiness, — **D**  
> That thou, light-winged Dryad of the trees — **E**  
> In some melodious plot — **C**  
> Of beechen green, and shadows numberless, — **D**  
> Singest of summer in full-throated ease. — **E**

### Style

The poem is written in a superb style. It displays Keats’s power as a master of poetic language at its highest. Keats shows a knack for selecting appropriate words and making original and highly expressive phrases. Certain phrases, expressions and lines are highly memorable because of their poetic excellence. The phrase “the blushful Hippocrene”, which refers to the fountain of the Muses and its red wine looking like the blushing cheeks of a pretty girl, is indeed beautiful. Again, this wine has beaded bubbles “winking at the brim”. The word “winking” here means sparkling, but how much more is suggested by this word! The bubbles seem to invite a man to the wine as a girl’s wink would invite him to her company. Another expressive phrase is “purple-stained mouth”, that is, a mouth stained red by wine.

The following phrases and expressions are also memorable: “the verdurous glooms” (line 40); “embalmed darkness” (line 43); “Mid-May’s eldest child, the coming musk-rose” (lines 48–49); “The murmurous haunt of flies on summer eves” (line 50). The line “the weariness, the fever, and the fret” admirably describes the sorrows and perplexities of life. “Leaden-eyed despair” effectively conveys the dullness in the eyes of a man in despair. Still another memorable line is: “To thy high requiem become a sod.”

### Figurative Language

#### Personification

- *Where Beauty cannot keep her lustrous eyes* (line 29) — Beauty compared to a person
- *The Queen-Moon is on her throne* (line 36) — moon compared to a queen
- *Adieu! the fancy cannot cheat so well* — abstract noun *fancy* compared to a person

#### Deification

- *That thou, light-winged Dryad of the trees* — nightingale compared to a nymph
- *Cluster’d around by all her starry Fays* — stars compared to fairies

#### Metaphor

- *And haply the Queen-Moon is on her throne* — moon compared to a queen
- *To thy high requiem become a sod* — Keats’s dead body compared to a sod in a graveyard

#### Simile

> Forlorn! the very word is like a bell  
> To toll me back from thee to my sole self! *(lines 71–72)* — the word “forlorn” compared to a bell

#### Symbolism

- **Nightingale** symbolises beauty, immortality, freedom, happiness, etc. The name implies singing at night. Keats finds that the nightingale’s song represents ideals and perfection lacking in society. Her song is timeless and can be heard even after Keats’s death.
- **“Embalmed darkness”** symbolises death.

#### Allusion

There are several allusions to classical mythology and the Bible:

- **Dryad** — nymph
- **Lethe** — a river in the underworld
- **Hippocrene** — fountain of the Muses
- **Flora** — Roman goddess of flowers and spring
- **Bacchus** — god of wine
- **Ruth** — a woman in the Old Testament

#### Metonymy

- **Bacchus** is a metonymy for alcohol.

#### Apostrophe

> Thou wast not born for death, immortal Bird!

The poet addresses the nightingale throughout the poem as if the bird is singing before him at night.

### Mood

The poet’s mood in the two opening stanzas is one of joy and ecstasy which almost benumbs his senses. This mood is due to the rapturous song of the nightingale. This mood leads him to a desire for a beaker of wine, by drinking which he can forget this world of sorrows and misfortunes and fade away into the forest where the nightingale is singing its joyous song.

The mood of ecstasy changes into a mood of deep pessimism and despair when describing human suffering due to fatal diseases like palsy, ageing, losing the lustre of beauties’ eyes, and so on.

Again the mood of deep pessimism and despair gives way to a mood of delight occasioned by his imaginative contact with the beauty and glory of Nature.

Next we find the poet “half in love with easeful Death”. He refers to this desire for death on earlier occasions, but at this moment especially he thinks it “rich to die”. This desire for death shows a morbidity in the poet.

In the final stanza, he is again overcome by a feeling of melancholy and finds himself facing the stern realities of life. He finds that the nightingale’s song gives rise to an illusion, an illusion which fails, leaving the listener alone with his cares and grief. At the end of the poem, the poet seems to be in a confused or dazed mood.
