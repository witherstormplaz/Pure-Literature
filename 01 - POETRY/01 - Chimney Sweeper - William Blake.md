# **Chimney Sweeper [Songs of Innocence] BY William Blake [1757-1827/Romantic Age]**

  

William Blake was a highly original thinker. He was a mystic, poet, engraver and illustrator and radical influenced by the French Revolution. He is often associated with the poets of the English Romantic movement. He was born on 28 November 1757 at 28 Broad Street (now Broadwick St.) in Soho, London. He was the third of seven children, two of whom died in infancy. Blake's father, James, was a hosier. The Bible was an early and profound influence on Blake, and remained a source of inspiration throughout his life.

Blake's early ambitions lay not with poetry but with painting and at the age of 14, after attending drawing school, he was apprenticed to James Basire, engraver. After his seven-year term was complete, Blake studied at the Royal Academy, but he is known to have questioned the aesthetic doctrines of its president, Sir Joshua Reynolds, and his stay there was brief. It nonetheless afforded him friendships with John Flaxman and Henry Fuseli, academics whose work may have influenced him.

In 1784, he set up a print shop, but within a few years the business floundered and for the rest of his life Blake eked out a living as an engraver and illustrator. His wife, Catharine, whom he married in 1782, remained faithful and diligent and she helped him to print the illuminated poetry for which he is remembered today.

Blake's first collection of poems, Poetical Sketches, was printed around 1783. In 1784 Blake composed his unfinished manuscript An Island in the Moon. In 1788, aged 31, Blake experimented with relief etching, a method he used to produce most of his books, paintings, pamphlets and poems. The process is also referred to as illuminated printing, and the finished products as illuminated books or prints. Illuminated printing involved writing the text of the poems on copper plates with pens and brushes, using an acid-resistant medium. Illustrations could appear alongside words in the manner of earlier illuminated manuscripts.

Blake used illuminated printing for most of his well-known works, including Songs of Innocence and of Experience. In 1789, he published his Songs of Innocence, the gentlest of his lyrics, but the collection was followed by Songs of Experience, containing a profound expression of adult corruption and repression. His long list of works shows relentless energy and drive. As one of the most complex writers known, it is impossible to summarise his career - he was a combination of extremes. His vision of civilisation as inevitably chaotic and contradictory mirrors the political turmoil of his era. It is only in retrospect that we can begin to appreciate his work and unravel its complex and allusive sources.

Blake's last years, from 1818 to 1827, were made comfortable and productive as a result of his friendship with the artist John Linnell. Blake died in his cramped rooms in Fountain Court, the Strand, London, on Aug. 12, 1827.

## **Blake's Songs of Innocence and Experience**

  
Blake's Songs of Innocence and Experience (1794) juxtapose the innocent, pastoral world of represent a meek virtue, poems like "The Tyger" exhibit opposing, darker forces. Thus the collection childhood against an adult world of corruption and repression; while such poems as "The Lamb" as a whole explores the value and limitations of two different perspectives on the world. Many of the poems fall into pairs, so that the same situation or problem is seen through the lens of innocence first and then experience. Blake does not identify himself wholly with either view; most of the poems are dramatic that is, in the voice of a speaker other than the poet himself. Blake stands outside innocence and experience, in a distanced position from which he hopes to be able to recognize and correct the fallacies of both. In particular, he pits himself against despotic authority, restrictive morality, sexual repression, and institutionalized religion; his great insight is into the way these separate modes of control work together to squelch what is most holy in human beings.

The Songs of Innocence dramatize the naive hopes and fears that inform the lives of children and trace their transformation as the child grows into adulthood. Some of the poems are written from the perspective of children, while others are about children as seen from an adult perspective. Many of the poems draw attention to the positive aspects of natural human understanding prior to the corruption and distortion of experience. Others take a more critical stance toward innocent purity: for example, while Blake draws touching portraits of the emotional power of rudimentary Christian values, he also exposes over the heads, as it were, of the innocent-Christianity's capacity for promoting injustice and cruelty.

The Songs of Experience work via parallels and contrasts to lament the ways in which the harsh experiences of adult life destroy what is good in innocence, while also articulating the weaknesses of the innocent perspective ("The Tyger," for example, attempts to account for real, negative forces in the universe, which innocence fails to confront). These latter poems treat sexual morality in terms of the repressive effects of jealousy, shame, and secrecy, all of which corrupt the ingenuousness of innocent love. With regard to religion, they are less concerned with the character of individual faith than with the institution of the Church, its role in politics, and its effects on society and the individual mind. Experience thus adds a layer to innocence that darkens its hopeful vision while compensating for some of its blindness.

The style of the Songs of Innocence and Experience is simple and direct, but the language and the rhythms are painstakingly crafted, and the ideas they explore are often deceptively complex. Many of the poems are narrative in style; others, like "The Sick Rose" and "The Divine Image," make their arguments through symbolism or by means of abstract concepts. Some of Blake's favorite rhetorical techniques are personification and the reworking of Biblical symbolism and language. Blake frequently employs the familiar meters of ballads, nursery rhymes, and hymns, applying them to his own, often unorthodox conceptions. This combination of the traditional with the unfamiliar is consonant with Blake's perpetual interest in reconsidering and reframing the assumptions of human thought and social behavior.

## **Analysis of Chimney Sweeper [Songs of Innocence]**

  

### **Stanza - 1**



When my mother died I was very young,

And my father sold me while yet my tongue

Could scarcely cry 'Weep! weep! weep! weep!'

So your chimneys I sweep, and in soot I sleep.

  

At the beginning of the poem, the speaker tells us that his mother died when he was very young. The speaker further tells us about his childhood. His father sold him when he was so tender that he could not even pronounce the word 'sweep' properly. He thereby, lisped it 'weep' and wept all the time. His pathetic condition is stressed by the repetition of the word 'weep' four times. In the later eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries small boys were employed to clean chimneys because they could crawl up there easily. Therefore, the father sold him to a Master Sweeper who runs a chimney sweeping business. Thus, as his father sold him, he sweeps chimneys and sleeps in soot. Perhaps, chimney sweepers would sleep under the blankets or cloths they used to collect soot during the day. It could be known as sleeping in soot. It also could mean that the boys at tender age, have soot- smeared bodies and they have to sleep at night without washing off the soot.

  

### **Stanza-2**

  

There's little Tom Dacre, who cried when his head,

That curled like a lamb's back, was shaved: so I said,

"Hush, Tom! never mind it, for when your head's bare,

You know that the soot cannot spoil your white hair."

  

Tom Dacre. Poor little Tom Dacre cried when his head cas shaved. His head was curled like a In this stanza, the little chimney sweeper tells us the woeful condition of his fellow chimney sweeper lamb's back. So it is obvious that Tom had curly white hair. This simile also implies the innocence of little Tom. Perhaps, Tom's hair would have been shaved to prevent soot from getting all over his hair. The speaker tells us what he told Tom after his head was shaved. He asked Tom to be quiet and not to worry about it because Tom's hair would not get messed up by all that dirty soot. When Tom lost his curly white hair, it is clear that he lost his innocent and cute look as well. The whiteness of hair disappeared and blackness appeared on his head covered with soot.

  

### **Stanza -3**

  

And so he was quiet, and that very night,

As Tom was a-sleeping, he had such a sight!--

That thousands of sweepers, Dick, Joe, Ned, and Jack,

Were all of them locked up in coffins of black.

  

In this stanza, there is an extension of the story of Tom. He was calmed by the speaker with his consoling words. That same night while sleeping, Tom saw a wonderful vision. He saw in his dream thousands of chimney sweepers were dead and their bodies were locked up in coffins, made of black-colored wood. A few of them were named as Dick, Joe, Ned and Jack. There is a similarity between the black chimneys and the black coffins. The black colour is further associated with the soot which the poor chimney sweepers who sleep at night. So it is ironic that Tom's dream reflects the reality of thousands of chimney sweepers like Tom and the speaker.

  

Stanza - 4

  

And by came an angel, who had a bright key,

And he opened the coffins, and set them all free;

Then down a green plain, leaping, laughing, they run

And wash in a river, and shine in the sun.

  

Even in this stanza, there is an extension of Tom's dream.. Accordingly, an angel arrives and lets all the children free out of their coffins. They gain freedom from the suffering of their everyday life. "The bright key' is significant because it could be the key to open the gate of the heaven. It is ironic that this powerful imagery of the dream suggests that the only way which these children can escape from their present suffering is death. Thus all the visual, colour and kinesthetic images in the third and fourth lines are associated with heaven. In the third line 'green' symbolizes prosperity and a ray of new hope for the children. There is a religious image in the last line. The images of the washing in a river and shining in the sun suggest the rebirth of the children and it is like the religious ritual of baptism. The children are now leaping, laughing and running just like little angels.

  

In this stanza, the poet is critical about the role of the angel. He does not help the children to become free from their present harsh life. Instead, they have to gain happiness, lost childhood and innocence only after death in heaven.

  

Stanza - 5

  

Then naked and white, all their bags left behind,

They rise upon clouds, and sport in the wind;

And the angel told Tom, if he'd be a good boy,

He'd have God for his father, and never want joy.

  

Tom's dream is further narrated in this stanza. The former chimney sweepers are depicted as naked and flying on clouds in the wind. The image of "The naked and white' implies that now the children are free from the clothes covered with chimney soot and they have regained their purity and innocence of childhood perhaps with white hair and fair physical complexion. Moreover, the image of 'Bags left behind" suggests that the children seem to be free from their burdens of their chimney-sweeping equipment and agonies of chimney sweeping. They are neither boxed in chimneys nor coffins. Consequently, the children enjoy the optimum freedom by rising upon clouds and sporting in the wind.

  

The third and the fourth lines are live and dramatic because Tom has a conversation with the angel. Accordingly, the angel tells Tom that, if he had become a good boy, God would be his father and he would never lack joy. [The word 'want' is used to mean 'lack' here]. Here, Blake introduces the doctrine of Christian Resignation: the more you suffer in silence, the happier you'll be in Heaven. This seems to be a positive and optimistic message. Then, how would Tom be a good boy? It is really by continuing his present dull and deadly life full of suffering without any protest and anger. The angel thereby, does not have any solution to save him from the worldly hell except filling the innocent child's mind with fantastic expectations after death devoid of practical remedies for the present reality.

  

Stanza - 6

  

And so Tom awoke, and we rose in the dark,

And got with our bags and our brushes to work.

Though the morning was cold, Tom was happy and warm:

So, if all do their duty, they need not fear harm.

  

In these lines, the speaker finishes narrating Tom's dream, and describes how Tom awoke and the two of them arose early in the morning to face the practical reality taking their chimney-sweeping equipment such as bags and brushes. It is quite obvious that there is a stark contrast between the Tom's sweet dream and harsh reality. However, Tom is in a happy and warm mood in spite of the cold in the morning.

  

The sad ironic ending describes how the children, in their young naivety are happy to work as they can anticipate a free and joyful life after death. It is much more ironic that these children suffer twofold. Not only do they physically suffer, but they also suffer mentally and emotionally because it is impossible for them to remain good and pure in such an uncondusive and unpleasant working condition.

## **Poetic Techniques**

### **Form - Quatrain form**

  

Narrative form - The poet employs both first person narrative form and third person omniscient narrative form. In the first stanza, it is the first person narrative form. The speaker is a young chimney sweeper. Then he narrates Tom's dream being aware of the whole dream. The poet uses first person plural narrative form in the first two lines of the last stanza. Thus there is a variation of the narrative form and it boosts the dramatic and live quality.

  

### **Rhyme scheme**

  

The poem is made up of six stanzas of AA BB rhyme

  

When my mother died I was very, young - A

And my father sold me while yet my tongue - A

Could scarcely cry "Weep! weep! weep! weep!' - B

So your chimneys I sweep, and in soot I sleep. - B

  

Thus, except the last stanza, rhyming couplets have been used in all the other five stanzas in a fairy tale manner or like a nursery rhyme. But, in the last stanza the rhyme scheme breaks down. It indicates that Tom Dacre's fantasy is over and all the chimney sweepers are going to face the harsh reality.

  

The harsh K sound at the end of the first two lines, together with a lack of perfect full rhyme, draw attention to the negative words in the verse: "dark" and "work"

  

"And so Tom awoke, and we rose in the **dark**,

And got with our bags and our brushes to **work**."

### **Similes**

  

"There's little Tom Dacre, who cried when his head,

That curled **like a lamb's back**, was shaved: so I said,"

  

Tom Dacre's white curly hair is compared to a lamb's back. Tom Dacre's hair represents his innocence. It is "like a lamb's back," evoking the lamb of God, a Christian symbol of piety and innocence. Blake explores the role of the lamb more deeply in "The Lamb," another poem in Songs of Innocence. The shaving of Tom's head is akin to the act of sheep-shearing, a moment of innocence lost. The same pattern can be found in the contrast between the whiteness of Tom's hair and the darkness of the chimney soot. Whiteness is a classic literary motif of purity, and darkness is one of experience.

### **Meter**

  

Like many of Blake's poems, the meter of "The Chimney Sweeper" blends with iambic feet and anapestic feet.

  

Each line has four stressed syllables and four unstressed syllables.

  

1. When my **moth**/ 2. er **died**/ 3. **I** was **ve**/4. ry **young**

  

When breaking this line up into feet, it has four feet.

  

So the first foot and the third foot contain two unstressed syllables followed by a stressed syllable.

  

It is called an anapest and the second and fourth groups contain an unstressed syllable followed by a stress syllable. This is called an iamb, and it is the most common foot type in English.

  

Thus "The Chimney Sweeper" contains lots of anapests and lots of iambs. so this poem is a mixture of anapestic and iambic tetrameter.

### **Tone**

  

The tone of the speaker in the opening lines is frustration and it is helplessness as reflected in the final line of the stanza:

  

"So your chimneys I sweep & in soot I sleep"

When another child, "little Tom Dacre" cries getting his head shaved the speaker says:

  

""Hush, Tom! never mind it, for when your head's bare,

You know that the soot cannot spoil your white hair."

  

The speaker's tone in these lines seems friendly, trying to help Tom make the best of a bad situation.

  

After Tom falls asleep he has a dream. Tom believes that the angel will intervene and save the chimney sweepers from subjugation. From Blake's point of view, the lines take on a sinister tone in that as long as Tom obeys his masters, "is a good boy", as the angel tells him, then God will look upon him fondly (Line 19-20). To Tom, God and the angel in the poem are representations of authority figures, to be obeyed without question, just like his masters who make him sweep chimneys.

  

The final stanza of the poem is from the perspective of the speaker introduced in the first stanza and he tells how the next morning Tom woke up and was happy and warm even though it was very cold outside while they were working. The speaker's final line of the poem is "So if all do their duty, they need not fear harm" (Line 24). This line reveals how the speaker really feels about his situation as a chimney sweeper. He knows that as long as he and the other boys continue to work they will not feel the wrath of the bosses. The tone of the speaker in this line is matter-of-fact, he knows what the situation is and furthermore, he knows that he can't change it. The helplessness present in the speaker's tone in the final line can be seen as a further indictment of society by Blake in that, Blake doesn't believe that "if all do their duty, they need not feel harm" (Line 24). Blake feels that duty really doesn't matter, that society through the church, through the cruel parents, through the bosses, all have responsibility for the fate of the chimney sweepers and it is Blake's tone of injustice that stays with the reader.
