# Spring and Fall

> *A lyric meditation on mortality, childhood innocence, and the universal grief that binds all sorrows — by Gerard Manley Hopkins*

---

## Poem Text

> **Márgarét, áre you grieving**  
> **Over Goldengrove unleaving?**  
> **Leaves, like the things of man, you**  
> **With your fresh thoughts care for, can you?**  
>   
> **Ah! as the heart grows older**  
> **It will come to such sights colder**  
> **By and by, nor spare a sigh**  
> **Though worlds of wanwood leafmeal lie;**  
>   
> **And yet you will weep and know why.**  
> **Now no matter, child, the name:**  
> **Sórrow's springs áre the same.**  
> **Nor mouth had, no nor mind, expressed**  
> **What heart heard of, ghost guessed:**  
>   
> **It is the blight man was born for,**  
> **It is Margaret you mourn for.**

---

## Note on the Poet

> [!info]+ Gerard Manley Hopkins (1844–1889) — Biographical Sketch
> **Gerard Manley Hopkins, S.J.** was an English poet, Roman Catholic convert, and Jesuit priest whose posthumous fame established him among the leading Victorian poets.
>
> - **Born:** 28 July 1844, Stratford, Essex — first of nine children to Manley and Catherine (Smith) Hopkins
> - **Family:** Father founded a marine insurance firm; served as British consul general in Hawaii. Parents were High Church Anglicans.
> - **Early art:** First ambition was painting; became a skilled draughtsman — visual training informed his poetic imagery.
> - **Education:** Highgate School (1854–63), where he studied Keats; Balliol College, Oxford (1863–67) for classics.
> - **Conversion:** Deeply influenced by Christina Rossetti and John Henry Newman; received into Catholic Church 21 October 1866.
> - **Sacrifice:** Burned his early poems in 1868, renouncing poetry for seven years to join the Jesuits.
> - **Return to verse:** 1875 — asked by superior to write *The Wreck of the Deutschland*; thereafter produced his masterpieces (*God's Grandeur*, *The Windhover*, *Pied Beauty*, *The Sea and the Skylark*).
> - **Later years:** Ordained 1877; served in Chesterfield, London, Oxford, Manchester, Glasgow, and finally Dublin (1884) — disliked Dublin, health failed, eyesight deteriorated.
> - **Death:** Died of typhoid fever, 8 June 1889, aged 44. Buried in Glasnevin Cemetery, Dublin. Final words: *"I am so happy, I am so happy. I loved my life."*
> - **Legacy:** Poems published 1918 by friend Robert Bridges; now recognized as a revolutionary innovator of **sprung rhythm** and a bridge to modernism.

## Line-by-Line Analysis

### Lines 1–4 — The Child's Grief

> [!note]+ **Scene & Speaker**
> - **Márgarét** — stressed name (sprung rhythm); a young, innocent child
> - **Goldengrove unleaving** — coined compound: *unleaving* = shedding leaves; visual image of autumn
> - **Leaves, like the things of man** — simile: falling leaves = human mortality
> - **Fresh thoughts** — child's unformed, intuitive mind; associated with *Spring*

> [!quote] **Key Insight**
> Margaret grieves for the *leaves* but does not yet understand she grieves for *mortality itself*. Her "fresh thoughts" cannot articulate the "things of man" (death, decay).

---

### Lines 5–8 — The Prediction of Maturity

> [!warning]+ **Heart Grows Older → Colder**
> - **Ah!** — exclamation of realization / prophetic tone
> - **Heart** = synecdoche for Margaret's whole emotional being
> - **Colder** — not cruel, but *accustomed*; maturity numbs the raw grief of autumn leaves
> - **By and by** — internal rhyme; emphasizes inevitability
> - **Nor spare a sigh** — she will not waste breath on fallen leaves

> [!tip] **Hopkins's Coinages (Sprung Rhythm Drivers)**
> | Word | Etymology / Effect |
> |------|-------------------|
> | **wanwood** | *wan* (pale, sickly) + *wood* → faded, dying forest |
> | **leafmeal** | cf. *piecemeal* → fragmentary, scattered, haphazard decay |
> | **unleaving** | *un-* + *leaving* → active shedding, not passive falling |

> **Visual:** *"worlds of wanwood leafmeal lie"* — vast, scattered, decaying leaves beyond Goldengrove.

---

### Lines 9–11 — The Universal Spring of Sorrow

> [!success]+ **Paradox: She Will Still Weep**
> - **And yet you will weep and know why** — dramatic climax (stresses clustered: *And yét yóu wíll wéep ánd knów whý*)
> - **Now no matter, child, the name** — the *label* for grief is irrelevant
> - **Sorrow's springs are the same** — metaphor: *spring* = source of water → all grief flows from one source: **mortality**

> [!quote] **Philosophical Core**
> > Whether grief is for falling leaves, a lost friend, or one's own death — the *source* is identical. The child's intuitive "ghost" (spirit) already knows this.

---

### Lines 12–13 — The Inarticulate Knowing

> [!abstract]+ **Heart Heard, Ghost Guessed**
> - **Nor mouth had, no nor mind, expressed** — repetition (*no nor*) = emphatic negation; she cannot *speak* or *think* it yet
> - **What heart heard of, ghost guessed** — alliteration (H/G); *heart* = feeling, *ghost* = soul/spirit (not scary)
> - The unconscious knowledge of mortality precedes language and reason.

---

### Lines 14–15 — The Final Couplet (Consolation & Truth)

> [!danger]+ **The Blight Man Was Born For**
> - **Blight** — disease, decay, the *curse* of mortality (biblical echo: *thorns and thistles*)
> - **It is Margaret you mourn for** — double meaning:
>   1. She mourns *for herself* (her own future death)
>   2. She *is* the embodiment of the universal human condition

> [!tip] **Feminine Rhyme**
> - *born for / mourn for* — two-syllable (feminine) rhyme
> - Typical of nursery rhymes / children's verse → suits the addressee (a child) and the poem's sing-song opening

---

## Themes

> [!tip]+ **Central Themes**
> 1. **Innocence vs. Experience** — Margaret's fresh grief vs. the poet's philosophical knowledge
> 2. **Mortality & Mutability** — All living things die; *Spring* (youth) → *Fall* (age/death)
> 3. **Universal Grief** — *Sorrow's springs are the same* — one source for all sorrows
> 4. **Intuitive vs. Articulate Knowledge** — *ghost guessed* what *mouth* and *mind* cannot express
> 5. **The Child as Everyman** — Margaret = every human confronting mortality
> 6. **Nature as Mirror** — Goldengrove's autumn = human life cycle

## Poetic Techniques

### Form & Structure

- **15 lines** — compact, dense, no stanza breaks (single breath)
- **Sprung rhythm** — 4 stresses per line (tetrameter), but variable syllables:
  - *Márgarét, áre you grieving* (4 stresses, 7 syllables)
  - *Ah! as the heart grows older* (3 stresses, trimeter)
  - *It is Margaret you mourn for* (3 stresses, trimeter)
- **Dramatic monologue** — one speaker (poet), silent listener (Margaret)

---

### Rhyme Scheme: **AABBCCDDDEEFFGG**

```text
grieving / unleaving          (A)
you / can you                 (B)
older / colder                (C)
sigh / lie                    (D)
why / name / same             (D D D)  ← triplet at climax
expressed / guessed           (E)
born for / mourn for          (F F)    ← feminine rhyme
```

- **Internal rhyme:** *By and by* (line 7) ↔ end rhymes
- **Triplet (DDD)** at lines 9–11 marks the philosophical turning point

## Critical Context: Sprung Rhythm

> [!info]+ **What is Sprung Rhythm?**
> - **Invented by Hopkins** — based on Anglo-Saxon alliterative verse (e.g., *Beowulf*)
> - **Feet:** 1–4 syllables, **stress always on first syllable**
> - **Contrast:** *Running rhythm* (traditional iambic/trochaic) = fixed pattern → "same and tame"
> - **Effect:** Mimics natural speech; clusters stresses for emphasis; anticipates free verse
> - **In this poem:** 4 stresses/line (tetrameter) but syllable count varies wildly (7–12)

> **Example (Line 8):**
> ```
> Though worlds / of wan / wood leaf / meal lie
> ˘   /    ˘  /     /    /      /   /   (scansion approximate)
> ```
> Four strong stresses drive the line's weight.
