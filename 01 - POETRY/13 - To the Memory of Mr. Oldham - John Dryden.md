## Note on the Poet

John Dryden, an English poet and dramatist who did dominate literary efforts of The Restoration, was born on August 19, 1631, in Aldwinkle, Northamptonshire, England. He received a classical education at Westminster School and Trinity College, Cambridge, then moved to London in 1657 to commence his career as a professional writer. The young playwright's reputation grew quickly, and in 1668, only ten years after his move to London, Dryden was appointed Poet Laureate of England.

John Dryden died in London on May 12, 1700, and was buried in Westminster Abbey next to Chaucer. He left behind almost 30 works for the stage as well as a major critical study (An Essay on Dramatic Poesy) and a number of translations including the works of Virgil.

After John Donne and John Milton, John Dryden was the greatest English poet of the seventeenth century. After William Shakespeare and Ben Jonson, he was the greatest playwright. And he has no peer as a writer of prose, especially literary criticism, and as a translator. Other figures, such as George Herbert or Andrew Marvell or William Wycherley or William Congreve, may figure more prominently in anthologies and literary histories, but Dryden's sustained output in both poetry and drama ranks him higher. After Shakespeare, he wrote the greatest heroic play of the century, The Conquest of Granada (1670, 1671), and the greatest tragicomedy, Marriage A-la-Mode (1671). Не wrote the greatest tragedy of the Restoration, All for Love (1677), the greatest comitragedy, Don Sebastian (1689), and one of the greatest comedies, Amphitryon (1690). As a writer of prose he developed a lucid professional style, relying essentially on patterns and rhythms of everyday speech. As a critic he developed a combination of methods-historical, analytical, evaluative, dialogic-that proved enabling to neoclassical theory. As a translator he developed an easy manner of what he called paraphrase that produced brilliant versions of Homer, Lucretius, Horace, Ovid, Juvenal, Persius, Giovanni Boccaccio, Geoffrey Chaucer, and above all Virgil. His translation of The Aeneid remains the best ever produced in English. As a poet he perfected the heroic couplet, sprinkling it with judicious enjambments, triplets, and metric variations and bequeathing it to Alexander Pope to work upon it his own magic.

Dryden the poet is best known today as a satirist, although he wrote only two great original satires, Mac Flecknoe (1682) and The Medall (1682). His most famous poem, Absalom and Achitophel (1681), while it contains several brilliant satiric portraits, unlike satire comes to a final resolution, albeit tragic for both David and his son. Dryden's other great poems Annus Mirabilis (1667), Religio Laici (1682), The Hind and the Panther (1687), Anne Killigrew (1686), Alexander's Feast (1697), and "To My Honour'd Kinsman" (1700)-are not satires either. John Dryden died in London on May 12, 1700, and was buried in Westminster Abbey next to Chaucer. He left behind almost 30 works for the stage as well as a major critical study (An Essay on Dramatic Poesy) and a number of translations including the works of Virgil.

In his elegy To the Memory of Mr. Oldham John Dryden laments the death of John Oldham (1653-1683), a satirist who imitated the classical Satires of Juvenal. This young poet's Satires upon the Jesuits which Dryden admired, were written in 1679, before Dryden's major satires appeared. Dryden therefore, would have known Oldham's satires, which appeared in print only a few months before Dryden's satiric masterpiece Absalom and Achitophel (1681). Dryden laments that he has made Oldham's acquaintance much too late and that Oldham died much too young. Their poetry and their thinking were very similar. They both disliked "knaves and fools" which are objects of satire. They both had the same goals. Oldham is compared to Nisus, who on the point of winning a footrace, slipped in a pool of blood in Vergil's Aeneid.

Oldham died of smallpox, on 9 December 1683, aged only 30. He may also have suffered from tuberculosis during his lifetime. However, John Dryden was one of Oldham's admirers. Therefore, Dryden laments Oldham's premature death and assesses his literary merit in twenty-five lines of heroic couplets, an unusual verse form of the elegy, To the Memory of Mr. Oldham.

## Analysis of the Lines

### Lines 1-10

> Farewell, too little and too lately known,  
> Whom I began to think and call my own;  
> For sure our souls were near ally'd; and thine  
> Cast in the same poetic mould with mine.  
> 
> One common note on either lyre did strike,  
> And knaves and fools we both abhorr'd alike:  
> To the same goal did both our studies drive,  
> The last set out the soonest did arrive.  
> 
> Thus Nisus fell upon the slippery place,  
> While his young friend perform'd and won the race.

At the beginning of the poem, Dryden follows the accepted elegiac convention by announcing the farewell. Dryden thereby, identifies himself with the younger poet Oldham. Their souls were associated with each other because of their mutual devotion to the poetic school of satire. As well, they had mutual dislike of naves and fools. Dryden accepts the fact that Oldham achieved poetic fame earlier in life than him. It is compared to the race narrated by Vergil in the Aeneid, book 5, involving Nisus and Euryalus. This footrace was arranged by Aeneas in honor of his dead father. The older Nisus, leading in the foot race, slips in a pool of sacrificial blood, getting absolutely soaked in it; then, he grabs the second-place runner, Salius, by the foot, so that his good friend Euryalus is able to cross the finish line first and become the victor. This event is really a parallel to the relationship between Oldham and Dryden. 

### Lines 11-21

> O early ripe! to thy abundant store  
> What could advancing age have added more?  
> It might (what nature never gives the young)  
> Have taught the numbers of thy native tongue.  
> But satire needs not those, and wit will shine  
> Through the harsh cadence of a rugged line.  
> 
> A noble error, and but seldom made,  
> When poets are by too much force betray'd.  
> Thy generous fruits, though gather'd ere their prime  
> Still show'd a quickness; and maturing time  
> But mellows what we write to the dull sweets of rhyme.

After expressing and emphasizing the commonality as poets he had with Oldham, Dryden regrets that Oldham had to face untimely death before he was given a chance to gain maturity in old age. "O early ripe!" In the 12th line, Dryden wonders "What could advancing age have added more" to his friend. Dryden suggests that Oldham could have gathered more experience and exhibited his talents, if he had not died at his immature age. Therefore, he realizes and worries that nature never gives an opportunity for the young to be perfect. Dryden further points out that Oldham required learning on "the numbers of thy native tongue." [The meter of English poetry] Dryden here, brings out a common criticism of Oldham's poems as rough and lack of refined language. Oldham gained fame as a verse satirist, especially through his 'Satire' on the Jesuits' published in 1679. His satire is vigorous and witty but the verse, by comparison with Dryden is rough and unpolished. Nevertheless, Dryden admires Oldham's poetry because his chosen field satire does not need strict metrical lines. He emphasizes the fact that Oldham's wit will shine through the cadence of a rugged line. Thus Dryden uplifts Oldham's esteem by attacking his poetic rivals: rough meter is "a noble error" in a time "When poets are by too much force betray'd"

In the 19th and 20th lines, the poet draws our attention to Oldham and says about the poems he wrote before gaining maturity. "generous fruits...gathered ere their prime". Dryden further says about Oldham's poems "showed a quickness," one which "maturing time" may not have aided, for it "mellows what we write to the dull sweets of rhyme." Thus his 'generous fruits' would only have mellowed and would have lost their sharp wit. 

### Lines 22-25

> Once more, hail and farewell; farewell thou young,  
> But ah too short, Marcellus of our tongue;  
> Thy brows with ivy, and with laurels bound;  
> But fate and gloomy night encompass thee around.

Dryden says farewell to Oldham and praises him saying that he also had a similar style, opinions and ideas like him. He further says that Oldham won fame and if he had lived, time wouldn't have added anything to his poetry, but experience and excellence. It is true that Oldham was young but, he was always mature and older than his age. Dryden ends the poem by comparing Oldham with Marcus Claudius Marcellus. "Marcellus of our tongue". Marcellus was a Roman general and he was the nephew of the Roman emperor Augustus. Although he became the successor of Agustus after winning battles, he died at young age. In the same way, although Oldham died at his young age, his poetry will live and he will be famous through it. The line "Thy brows with ivy, and with laurels bound" alludes to the flower wreath awarded to outstanding poets during that era. Dryden recalls the opening visual image of John Milton's pastoral elegy Lycidas in placing laurels and ivy around the young poet's head. The laurel is a small evergreen tree, and it's one of those plants that means something in literature. In fact, it's associated with poetry. Although Oldham was deserved such a floral tribute, he had to face death at that gloomy night according to his fate.

## Themes

The poem 'To the memory of Mr. Oldham' is an elegy written by John Dryden. He laments the untimely death of the young poet John Oldham who wrote 'Satires upon the Jesuits'. Dryden got to know about Oldham much too late. Dryden felt his death much because both of them followed same genre of poetry; satire. Consequently, their poetic approach was similar.

> "For sure our souls were near ally'd; and thine  
> in the same poetic mould with mine".

Thus, they were so much alike as poets, but the one who set out later arrived first. Alluding to The Aeneid he compares himself and Oldham to Nisus and Euryales: during a running race, the former slipped and the latter outran him.

> "Thus Nisus fell upon the slippery place,  
> While his young friend perform'd and won the race."

They also hated 'knaves and fools' who are the main targets of satire. Dryden admires Oldham's poetic achievement and excellence in spite of some technical deficiencies in versification. Dryden points out that Oldham's lack of literary perfectness does not affect the poetic school of satire because the sharp edges of ironic humour are more significant in satire than avoiding of smooth metrical lines. It is quite obvious that Dryden adheres to the Renaissance idea that satire becomes best when its verses are "rough" and not too polished. Thus, the main intention of Dryden is to appreciate and admire Oldham based on the aspiration derived from his poetry.

As far as the themes of the poem are concerned, it is quite obvious that Dryden conveys the universal truth that death is unpredictable and uncertain. The poet proves and highlights this reality focusing on some classical and mythical stories. The poet further expresses that people have both personal and professional bonds. In that sense, the bond between Dryden and Oldham is more professional because of their chosen poetic field of 'satire'. Their literary bond makes them eternal and immortal though everyone and everything is transient. Dryden also conveys through Oldham's character that some achieve greatness within a short period of life. Though they are no more with us physically, their work remains with us forever and ever.

### Allusions

Though elegic, Dryden does not want to make it entirely mournful because it is not suitable for a poet like Oldham. It cannot be light and playful either. Dryden tries his best to face the challenge of balancing the feelings specially through the classical allusions. One of them is taken from the epic poem 'The Aeneid' by Vergil.

Accordingly, Dryden refers to a footrace that Nisus and Euryalus partake in during the funeral games for Anchises. Nisus lost his footing falling upon the slippery place which allowed Euryalus, who had been trailing, to come from behind and win. This is really a parallel for the relationship between Oldham and Dryden.

Marcellus, who is mentioned in line 23, is the nephew of the Roman emperor Augustus, adopted by him as his successor. He died at the age of twenty. During his short life span he won a lot of battles and gained fame and glory in the war field just like Oldham in the poetic field. Unfortunately, both of them were unable to gain maturity in chosen field.

### Metaphor

In the fifth line `"One common note on either lyre did strike"`, there is a metaphor. Oldham's poetry is compared to the musical instrument lyre and it implies the lyrical quality of his poetry.

There is another metaphor in the 11th line `"O early ripe! To thy abundant store"`  
Here, Dryden compares Oldham to a fruit that had ripened early.

### Rhetorical Questions

The 12th line `"What could advancing age have added more"` is a rhetorical question which makes us think of the poetic excellence that Oldham would have gained if he had lived to continue his writing poetry.

### Language

Dryden starts the poem by saying goodbye. `"Farewell, too little and too lately known"` The words `"too little"` and `"too lately known,"` express Dryden's respect and endearment for late Oldham. The repetition of the word `"too"` intensifies the tragic element. In the 14th line `"native tongue."` is a metonym for English Language. The phrase `"the harsh cadence of a rugged line"` suggests the lack of versification of Oldham's poetry.

### Sound Devices

The first line includes the balanced construction, `"too little and too lately,"` balance being reinforced by double alliteration on the 'l' and 't' consonants. The use of open vowels and sonorants serves to lengthen the lines, adding weight and somberness to the tone. Balance further continues through `"think and call"` and `"knaves and fools,"`. It then develops emphatic and artful balance and double antithesis in line 8, `"the last set out the soonest did arrive."` Even the use of assonance of the 'O' sound in `"call my own"`, `"both abhorr'd"` creates melancholic tone.

### Repetitions

In this line `"Once more, hail and farewell; farewell, thou young"`, repetition of farewell with alliteration intensifies and emphasizes Dryden's deep-felt sadness on Oldham's bereavement and authentic wish of his goodbye to the diseased.
